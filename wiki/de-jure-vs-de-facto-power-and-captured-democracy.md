# De Jure vs. De Facto Power and Captured Democracy

Acemoglu & Robinson's *Persistence of Power, Elites and Institutions* formalises, in a dynamic game-theoretic model, why a shift in **de jure political power** (what formal institutions — elections, franchise, constitutional rules — allocate) need not change who actually controls outcomes. De facto power — influence from wealth, organisation, or capacity for violence — is, for a small elite, an **endogenous investment** they can raise to offset a formal loss; for a large, diffuse citizenry it is exogenous, because their numbers prevent them from solving the collective-action problem needed to invest strategically. In the model's baseline case this produces an exact **invariance** result: the equilibrium probability that the elite controls economic institutions is mathematically identical in democracy and nondemocracy, because the elite's optimal response to a democratic de jure gain (η) is to raise de facto spending by exactly enough to cancel it (η/φM).

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** throughout — a formal dynamic game (Markov Perfect Equilibrium), no independent statistical testing. Section 7's historical material (US South, Central America, Liberia, Colombia) is illustrative narrative citing secondary sources, offered by the authors explicitly as illustration rather than test: "Our objective is neither to provide a comprehensive historical account nor to test our model."

## Captured democracy: the paper's sharpest result

**[model]** If overthrowing a democratic *regime* costs strictly more political power than merely capturing *economic institutions* within it, democracy can become durable and long-lived — while the elite secures its preferred (labour-repressive) economic institutions with probability **higher** than under nondemocracy (Proposition 9: p(N) < p(D) < 1). The mechanism: durable democratic regimes give the elite an *additional* motive to invest in de facto power — capturing policy inside a stable system, rather than risking regime overthrow — so they overinvest relative to nondemocracy and win more often. Citizens still prefer and defend democracy in the model only because of an assumed direct, non-economic value they place on it (ν(D) > 0); absent that assumption they would prefer nondemocracy, since the economic outcomes converge.

**[wiki synthesis]** "Captured democracy" is a distinct institutional category from either straightforward democratic accountability or straightforward authoritarian capture: procedurally sound, competitively elected, genuinely valued by citizens for its own sake — and substantively still accountable to a narrow elite on the dimension that determines material outcomes. This bears directly on [[credible-commitment]]'s shackling argument and the wiki's existing open conflict [[constraint-vs-capacity-as-the-investment-mechanism]]: Wade's East Asian developmental states sustained investment under low-constraint regimes; this paper supplies the mechanism for the mirror case — a high-constraint (democratic) regime that fails to secure the *distributional* commitment investment actually depends on, because de facto power reasserts itself inside the formal structure rather than outside it.

## Comparative statics: what predicts elite capture

**[model]**, Proposition 4: elite dominance is more likely when (i) elite rents from repression (ΔR) are higher; (ii) the elite are more patient (higher β) — a reversal of the usual "patience improves outcomes" intuition, because patient elites invest more in de facto power; (iii) the elite are **fewer** (smaller M — a smaller free-rider problem in funding collective de facto-power investment); (iv) counter-intuitively, a **larger** formal democratic advantage for citizens (η) — because it raises the stakes enough to induce more elite counter-investment, unless η is large enough to make democracy self-consolidating instead (Corollary 1); (v) the elite's de facto-power technology (φ) is more effective.

**[wiki synthesis]** Point (iii) is the formal statement behind a claim elite theory and organisational-ecology sources in this wiki both gesture at informally: small, cohesive groups systematically out-organise large diffuse ones on collective action, so **smaller elites are more, not less, able to capture nominally accountable institutions.** This is the same mechanism [[iron-law-of-oligarchy]] describes qualitatively for internal party leadership, applied here to a whole polity's elite.

## Concrete capture mechanisms, catalogued from the case material

**[model]**, illustrative only: (1) direct co-optation of oversight bodies (the Freedmen's Bureau); (2) legal engineering to entrench control absent formal exclusion (Black Codes, vagrancy and enticement laws, poll taxes, literacy tests); (3) violence as a labour- and vote-control technology (Klan terror, lynching, the assassination of Colombian party leaders Gaitán and Galán); (4) electoral fraud and party-system engineering (Colombia's "incomplete vote" elite power-sharing pact; Liberia's 1927 election, described by the *Guinness Book of Records* as the most fraudulent ever held); (5) kinship/dynastic networks monopolising office (Central American presidents descended from two named conquistadors — Figures 1–2 of the source).
