# Institutional Sclerosis

Olson's thesis — that in societies left stable for long enough, small distributional coalitions accumulate, extract redistribution rather than produce growth, and thereby slow the economy, with war, coup or revolution as the only reset — is the single most directly relevant claim in this wiki to the proposition that institutions decay with age. This page anchors the `institutional-stagnation` batch's verdict because it carries the only source in it that *counts* evidence rather than asserting a mechanism: Heckelman's 25-years-on review of **53 empirical and descriptive tests**, finding **57% support, 23% mixed, 21% no support**, with no significant difference by methodology, publication outlet, author location or year. Two things must be carried alongside that headline and never separated from it. First, the level of analysis: **the age variable in this literature is always the duration of a national polity's political stability, never an individual institution's own tenure** — no study in the review tests whether a firm, an agency or an organisation becomes more sclerotic as it ages. Second, the mechanism is mostly untested: by Heckelman's own statement, only "a handful" of the 53 studies directly test whether coalitions actually accumulate; the rest test stability-duration against growth and leave the coalitions "implicitly in the background". This page is written so that neither "the evidence supports sclerosis" nor "sclerosis is debunked" can be read off it, because neither is what the source says.

**Evidence tier note.** **(a) empirical, at one remove — a literature review, not a study.** Heckelman collects and codes 53 works, roughly half cross-sectional regressions of national or US-state growth on a sclerosis proxy (28 studies) and roughly half narrative case studies or institutional histories (25). He runs significance tests on the *coding* — whether support rates differ by method, outlet, author location, publication year, or author identity — but he does not re-analyse anyone's data. He also **discloses a conflict of interest and tests it**, which is unusual enough to be part of the evidence (below). Strongest content: the tabulation itself, plus the two caveats he volunteers against his own headline. Weakest: the case-study half, which he calls "eclectic" and "fuzzier to interpret" and whose inclusion he flags as selection-biased by his own sampling rule.

## The theory as Heckelman restates it

**[model]** Olson's chain, from *The Logic of Collective Action* into *The Rise and Decline of Nations*:

1. In stable societies with unchanged borders, **small distributional coalitions form more easily than large encompassing ones**, because concentrated benefits and diffuse costs favour narrow organisation.
2. These coalitions **accumulate over time** (Olson's Implication 2).
3. They extract redistribution rather than produce growth, since growth is a public good and redistribution an excludable private one (Implications 4 and 7).
4. Accumulation reduces efficiency, slows technology adoption and slows the reallocation of resources — hence slower growth.
5. **Reset:** wars, coups and revolutions destroy coalitions and their access channels, restoring growth until new coalitions re-accumulate. Occasionally a non-violent institutional rupture does the same — Olson's own presidential-address example is the 1965 Voting Rights Act and the end of Jim Crow resetting southern US state institutions.

Olson's chapter 3 lists nine implications. **The literature concentrates on three** — 2, 4 and 7 — and mostly on the latter two.

## The numbers

**[empirical]** Heckelman's Table 2, reported here in full because rounding it in either direction is the failure mode this page exists to prevent:

| Cut | n | Support | Mixed | No support |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **All studies** | **53** | **57%** | **23%** | **21%** |
| Cross-sectional regression | 28 | 57% | 18% | 25% |
| Narrative case study | 25 | 56% | 28% | 16% |

**No significant difference in support rate by methodology, by publication outlet, by author location, or by publication year.** Author location skews North American (32 of 53) against European and New Zealand (21), but this is author location, not country studied; country samples similarly skew OECD and US-state.

**[wiki synthesis]** The correct reading of the top row is that roughly one study in five finds nothing and roughly one in four is equivocal, in a literature written substantially by people who found the thesis interesting enough to test. That is neither vindication nor refutation. It is a literature that has not converged.

## The caveat that matters more than the numbers

**[empirical — Heckelman's own words]** From his section on tests of the theory: *"Only a handful of studies have directly tested Implication 2"* — the claim that interest groups actually accumulate with elapsed stability. And: *"The vast majority of the literature has instead either tested some variant of Implications 4 and 7 or combined the three implications to relate stability to economic growth, leaving the role of special interest organizations and distributional coalitions implicitly in the background."*

**[wiki synthesis]** Two epistemic layers are being conflated by the 57% figure and must be kept apart:

- **Layer 1 — does a sclerosis proxy correlate with growth?** This is what most of the 53 studies test. The proxy is almost always years of stability: state age, years since statehood or the end of Reconstruction, years of uninterrupted democracy, years since foreign occupation, independence, revolution or civil war, years since the consolidation of "modernizing leadership". Roughly thirteen different operationalisations.
- **Layer 2 — do coalitions accumulate, and is that accumulation what produces the growth effect?** This is Olson's actual mechanism, and it is directly tested by "a handful" of studies out of 53.

The 57% is a Layer-1 number being read as if it settled Layer 2. **Heckelman never enumerates which studies constitute the handful** — a gap this wiki cannot close from this source.

He is critical of the proxy on his own account: years of stable borders "does not address Olson's theory or the spirit of Olson's tests very well, as wars or internal coups can create upheaval without changing the society's borders." And he insists the comparison must be conditioned: *"Care should be taken, though, not to mix stable and unstable nations in the same sample... the proper comparison would be to relate length of stability to growth, as long as each nation was stable during the entirety of the growth period."* His own 2000 study found the correlation significant **only** for the subset of nations with no coup or revolution during the growth period — i.e. the age effect is conditional on uninterrupted stability, not a raw time trend.

## The level-of-analysis problem

**[wiki synthesis — the reason this page anchors the batch]** The time variable across all 53 studies is **years since a stability-defining event at the level of a nation-state or a US state**. It is not organisational size, headcount, budget, or years since an organisation's founding. "Scale" in Olson's sense is the *number and entrenchment of accumulated interest groups in a polity*, not the size of any single institution.

Consequences, stated precisely:

- **No study in this review tests whether a single firm, agency or organisation becomes more sclerotic with its own age.** The source supplies essentially zero direct evidence on institution-level ageing.
- The interest groups themselves — unions, business associations, sectoral lobbies — appear only as inputs to a national growth regression. Their own internal behaviour is never tested for sclerosis, even though they are institutions.
- The unit shifts exactly once: the **communist-bureaucracy extension** (Murrell & Olson 1991, theoretical; Faith & Short 1995, empirical on Czechoslovakia and Hungary), where bureaus inside a centrally planned economy are the unit. It is the batch's only source-native statement of age-driven drift in public bureaucracies: bureaucratic interests "became more entrenched... more efficient at siphoning resources away from other needed areas... while continuing to improve their own positions", and over time bureaus "became more autonomous and formed into distributional coalitions, thereby lowering technical efficiency". **This strand rests on two sources and one two-country empirical test**, not on the 53-study corpus. It is the closest thing in this batch to an institution-level age claim, and it is thin.

This is the same substitution the rest of the batch makes: [[regulatory-accretion]] locates accumulation in a 130-year national rule corpus, [[state-capacity-and-level-of-government]] in a 1970s–80s legal regime applied uniformly regardless of any agency's founding date, [[political-decay-and-vetocracy]] in a 230-year-old constitutional structure whose dysfunction its own author dates to the 1980s. See [[open-questions]] Q61.

## Heckelman's conflict of interest, and his self-check

**[empirical]** Heckelman is a public-choice economist and an Olson specialist: co-editor of *Collective Choice: Essays in Honor of Mancur Olson* (2003), and author or co-author of three of the 53 reviewed studies (Heckelman 2000; Coates & Heckelman 2003a, 2003b), all coded "Support". This is a material conflict for a review presenting itself as neutral, and it should be recorded as such.

**He tests it, in footnote 13.** He runs a dummy for Olson-authored studies and a broader dummy for Olson's colleagues and students — a group that includes himself, Coates, Choi, Wallis & Oates, Knack & Keefer, and James, and which is **not monolithically pro-Olson** (Wallis & Oates 1988 and Knack & Keefer 1997 are both coded "Does not support"). Result: the Olson dummy "was generally significant" — Olson's own five studies do skew supportive — but "its presence did not alter the lack of significance for the other variables, nor did dropping the Olson studies from the sample altogether", and the broader insider dummy "was never significant."

**[wiki synthesis]** So the disclosed conflict does not move the 57/23/21 split. The self-check is a genuine strength; the underlying conflict still belongs in any citation of the headline.

Two further signs he is not simply thumbing the scale, both worth recording because they cut in opposite directions:

- **Against Olson:** he casts doubt on a study *he coded as support* — Weede (1986a) on less-developed countries uses an index of price distortions as the sclerosis proxy, "far removed from any direct connection to Olson. Neither groups nor national stability appear in the regression... Thus, Weede's claimed support for Olson must be qualified."
- **For Olson:** he offers an alternative reading of a null. Gray & Lowery (1988), coded "does not support" on US states, measured business groups relative to union size on the premise that business groups are pro-growth, which Heckelman says is "counter to Olson's view that considers business groups to be like cartels." This is the one clear instance of the reviewer defending Olson against a contrary result rather than reporting it flatly.
- On the case-study half he discloses a sampling bias against himself: he tabulated only case studies giving institutional sclerosis "prominent attention", and notes in footnote 6 that this "may perhaps bias the sample of reported studies in favor of *Rise and Decline* since brief mentions are more likely to be dismissive rather than supportive."

## The practitioners disagree with the literature

**[empirical]** Heckelman reports, via Heckelman & Whaples (2006), a survey of AEA and APSA members on the theory's signature prediction — that "economies grow faster after coups and revolutions due to the destruction of existing special interest organizations". On a 1 (disagree) to 5 (agree) scale, **economists averaged 2.53 and political scientists 2.39** — both below the midpoint. Working economists and political scientists *disbelieve* the distinctive prediction of a theory his own tally finds generally supported.

He names the tension and does not resolve it: *"Apparently, there is not widespread belief in Olson's explanation for the rain."*

**[wiki synthesis]** This is the sharpest qualifier on the 57% figure available inside the source. Two readings are open and this wiki adopts neither: that the profession is under-updating on a real result, or that the literature's coding is generous relative to what practitioners think the studies actually show.

## Levers

**[wiki synthesis]** Only one appears anywhere in the source, and it is destructive: **upheaval** — war, coup, revolution, or a discrete non-violent rupture on the Voting Rights Act model. The evidence base for it is narrative and case-study only; Heckelman reports no regression evidence directly testing the reset claim. **No incremental or reformist lever is discussed anywhere** — no sunset clauses, no competitive entry, no structured periodic reorganisation. A theory of decay whose only stated remedy is catastrophe is a theory with a prescriptive gap, and that gap is worth naming rather than filling from elsewhere. It bears directly on [[open-questions]] Q8, which asks whether renewal without external shock is documented anywhere.

## Dimensions this source names

All at the national or interest-group-ecology level, none at the organisational level: years of political stability, operationalised roughly thirteen ways; density or count of interest groups (sectional groups per capita, union membership rate, number and revenue of business associations); **encompassingness** of coalitions — narrow distributional versus broad, with Swiss direct democracy cited as the encompassing counter-case; regime type (democracy vs. autocracy, market vs. centrally planned); development level; and a methodological axis worth keeping — **direct interest-group measurement versus indirect stability-duration proxy**, which is exactly the Layer-1/Layer-2 distinction above.

## Scope

Post-1945 literature reviewed as of 2007, 25 years after Olson's 1982 book. Predominantly OECD nations and US states; less-developed countries "have received much less attention", with only four case studies focused exclusively on one (Manzetti on Argentina, Weyland on Brazil, Hojman on Chile, Demir on Turkey) and the largest LDC-inclusive sample being Koubi's 114-nation study. Overwhelmingly democracies. The centrally-planned extension is a separate, thinly evidenced strand.

**Public/private:** silent, and this should be flagged rather than assumed general. Where private-sector organisations appear (unions, business associations) they are regression inputs, not institutions whose own behaviour is tested. The only extension beyond developed democracies is to **state** economies, not to private firms.

## What would settle the question this page exists for

**[wiki synthesis]** A study that measures a **specific institution's own age** against a **directly observed accumulation variable** — coalition count, rule count, or a capture indicator attached to that institution — rather than regime-stability duration or cross-sectional density. Heckelman's review makes it possible to say how far the field is from that: a handful of 53 studies test accumulation at all, and none of those tests it at the level of a single organisation. See [[decay-as-real-vs-decay-as-overstated]].
