# AI and the Organisation of Knowledge Work in Legal Services

Un-refereed arXiv preprint (2611.23354, Zhang, Esposito and Yang, November 2026). The authors work at micro1, which publishes three of the legal-AI benchmarks the paper cites; they disclose this, and say the court, employment, spending and revenue findings do not depend on those benchmarks. The paper asks where cheaper specialist knowledge ends up: self-provision by households, internalisation by client firms, or reallocation among outside suppliers. In US legal services the clearest movement is self-representation by individuals and a reshuffling of work across law-firm tiers. Lawyer employment shows no clear new shift toward client firms, and premium firms report revenue growth on flat hours.

**Evidence tier note.** (a) for the court-record result: a difference-in-differences over 89 federal districts (non-prisoner cases against prisoner petitions), with an event study and a pre-trend-adjusted estimate. The in-house employment, law-firm revenue, billable-hours and firm-level AI-integration results are descriptive and weaker. The firm-level piece is 41 firms, uses associates' survey ratings, and has a Spearman correlation of 0.32 (p = 0.04). The make-or-buy and pricing framework is the authors' **[model]**. The authors say the data cannot separate AI from the older disaggregation of legal work, or a changing rate mix from within-service markup growth. Scope is the US legal sector.

## Findings

- **[empirical]** The share of non-prisoner federal civil cases with a self-represented party reached 16.8% in fiscal 2025, after sitting between 9.5% and 12.1% for nearly two decades. Against prisoner petitions in the same districts, the relative rise is 6.6 percentage points by FY2025, or 5.7 after allowing for a modest pre-existing trend. The rise is concentrated in case types that need substantial drafting. Real household spending on legal services stayed above its pre-pandemic trend, so self-provision has not come with a broad fall in paid demand.
- **[empirical]** The in-house share of private-sector lawyers rose from 22.8% (2010) to 28.7% (2019) and stood at 29.0% in 2024. Most of the movement was in 2013-2016, before generative AI. Within-state estimates show no clear post-2022 acceleration, and industry-level AI adoption is not positively associated with in-house hiring. The authors offer one possible explanation: legal-AI benchmarks show unreliable completion of multi-step work (the leading model fully passed about a quarter of tasks in one October 2026 evaluation). That is a hypothesis, not a tested mechanism.
- **[empirical]** Among outside suppliers, the Am Law 100 reported 13.0% gross-revenue growth in 2025 while billable hours in the Thomson Reuters sample rose 0.1%. Worked rates rose about 9%, lower-priced and midsize firms grew faster, and top-segment rates exceed $1,000 an hour against about $600 elsewhere. Patent and tax were already losing billable hours before 2022, and specialties with stronger benchmark scores show no systematically faster post-2022 decline.
- **[empirical]** Among 41 firms, reported AI integration is stronger at firms with lower revenue per lawyer, which fits the idea that firms facing contestable work have more urgent reasons to adopt.
- **[model]** Clients can reallocate work across outside providers without internalising it. If the departing work is more price-sensitive than the work that stays, premium firms' optimal markups can rise while their share of work falls. Restrictions on what clients may delegate limit the premium firm's return to adopting AI.

## Reading for the wiki

**[wiki synthesis]** The paper is a modern test of where the firm boundary moves when the cost of knowledge falls, and its answer is that the boundary mostly does not move. That sits awkwardly with simple readings of [[transaction-costs]] and [[governance-structures]], which predict internalisation when outside purchase becomes costly. It fits better with the view that retained expertise, relationships and recurring demand hold the boundary in place. It does not discriminate between boundary theories: nothing here measures asset specificity, residual control rights or measurement costs.

For [[knowledge-hierarchies-and-the-cost-of-scale]], the paper is about the knowledge-cost parameter. Cheaper access to specialist knowledge should, on that model, let generalists solve more problems themselves and thin out the specialist layers. The individual-litigant result is a small-scale instance of this, with the problem solver being the client rather than a junior in a hierarchy. The employment result shows no corresponding flattening inside client organisations so far. The paper does not study layers, spans or headcount within any organisation, so it neither supports nor contradicts the Parkinson-versus-knowledge-hierarchy question. It is also a reminder that the 2013-2016 in-house shift predates the technology in question, so timing matters for any AI attribution.

For [[institutions-as-a-design-space-for-artificial-agents]] and [[when-institutions-beat-intelligence]], the capability evidence on long-horizon legal work is a reason to expect AI to substitute for tasks before it substitutes for the organisational wrapper around them. Candidate register rows for [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]]: share of knowledge work done in-house versus bought; client restrictions on delegation; contestability of a supplier's work.
