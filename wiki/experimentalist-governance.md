# Experimentalist Governance

Sabel & Zeitlin's handbook chapter defines a governance architecture built on a **four-step recursive cycle**: provisional framework goals and metrics set jointly by central and local actors; broad discretion delegated to local units to pursue them; mandatory reporting and peer review comparing each unit's results against others taking different approaches, with corrective action required where results lag; and periodic revision of the goals, metrics and procedures themselves by a widening circle of actors. The claim is that this substitutes **"dynamic accountability"** — action justified as plausibly advancing a purpose, judged against peer practice — for conformance to fixed rules, and that it therefore handles a class of problems (strategic uncertainty, entrenched structural obstruction) that command-and-control hierarchy cannot. **The chapter is theory from the paradigm's own originators. It examines zero failed cases, reports no base rates, and reproduces no effect sizes for its two strongest empirical claims.** That is stated here because it determines how the framework may be used: as a coherent, actionable hypothesis set, not as a lever with a measured success rate.

**Evidence tier: (b) theory and institutional-history case description, built overwhelmingly on the authors' own prior work.** No formal model, no cross-case comparison, no natural experiment appears in this chapter. The strongest empirical assertions — that experimentalist reform improved outcomes for disadvantaged students in New York City schools and in the Finnish national system while narrowing the socio-economic outcome gap — are made by citation (Kemple 2011; Sabel et al. forthcoming) **with no effect size, sample size, or comparison group reproduced in the text**. The weakest material is the transnational extension (WTO disciplines, EU FLEGT timber licensing), which is prospective — "appears likely to be accepted as legitimate", "can likewise be expected to have a major impact" — rather than evaluated. The authors hedge in their own voice: "it is still too soon to know whether these successes will be further generalized."

**Selection on the dependent variable: confirmed, and structural.** Every worked example is a case where experimentalism is presented as succeeding or as "most promising". **No failed or abandoned experimentalist reform is examined anywhere in the chapter.** The case set therefore cannot distinguish "experimentalist design causes success" from "successes get retrospectively described as experimentalist." Sabel & Zeitlin are one of three sources in this batch with this problem — see [[open-questions]] Q72.

## The cycle and what it replaces

**[model]** The four steps, in the authors' order:

1. Central and local actors **provisionally** set broad framework goals and the metrics for gauging progress.
2. Local units are given **broad discretion** to pursue those goals their own way.
3. As a condition of that discretion, units **report** and submit to **peer review**, comparing their results against other units using different approaches; corrective action is required where results lag.
4. Goals, metrics and procedures are **periodically revised** in light of the review, by a widening circle of actors — and the cycle repeats.

What it displaces: rule-conformance accountability, in which a principal rewards or sanctions an agent against fixed rules. The authors' diagnosis of why that regime fails is specific and worth separating from generic anti-bureaucracy complaint — **rules that were once functional decay relative to a moving environment faster than they can be enforced, and the resulting gap between rule and practice is "bridged by an unaccountable proliferation of discretionary waivers and exceptions."** That is a different failure mode from [[red-tape]] (rules that impose burden and achieve nothing): here the rules worked once and the environment moved.

The second target is the **street-level bureaucrat** problem — low-visibility frontline discretion evading superior oversight — which the authors argue team-based decision-making under peer scrutiny structurally supersedes rather than suppresses, by making the discretion transparent and reviewable instead of furtive.

## Destabilization regimes: why blockers submit

**[b — asserted and illustrated; the authors concede its payoff is uncalculable]** The distinctive mechanism, and the one that matters most for the wiki's reform question. Where an empowered party benefits from the status quo, central authorities do not seize control; they threaten to make the status quo **unworkable** — penalty defaults, destabilization rights exercised through courts, legislatures, or administrative threat. The claim is that this makes joint deliberation the dominant strategy even though its payoff cannot be known ex ante. Illustrations: EU energy policy, US school and child-welfare litigation. It is explicitly *in terrorem*, and the authors say so.

**[model] This is a wager against the conventional theory of reform, and they present it as one.** The conventional view attacks power disparities head-on: redistribute or share power as a *precondition* for reform (community organising, labour "power mapping", at the limit revolutionary redistribution). The experimentalist view holds that obstruction is **diffuse rather than located at a hierarchical apex**, so reform proceeds by creating local decision-making space plus destabilization mechanisms, *without* formal power redistribution at the top. The authors flag their own open question about it: whether affected parties — parents, citizens — actually gain a voice, or only frontline workers and firms do.

## Scope conditions, stated twice by the authors

**[model]** Experimentalism is not offered as universal. Two conditions:

1. **Strategic uncertainty** — goals and effective methods cannot be determined ex ante and must be discovered by problem-solving.
2. **Polyarchic / multi-polar power** — no single actor can impose its preferred solution unilaterally.

The EU is the paradigm case because it combines both more consistently than other polities. Domain scope: EU and US regulation and public services, roughly 1990–2011. Nothing is claimed for authoritarian settings, non-EU/US polities, or the period before 1990.

**[c — wiki synthesis]** The second scope condition is where this source collides productively with the wiki's existing material. Hammond ([[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]]) shows that veto-point *count* predicts nothing about discretion without the preference profile; Sabel & Zeitlin treat "no actor can impose a solution" as the condition that *enables* experimentalism to emerge. Hammond's framework would call that underspecified without knowing how far apart the multipolar actors' preferences are. Adjacent conceptual territory, not a tested conflict. Distinguish this from the register's D74 (independent-venue count, **merged into D14 on 2026-08-20**; score D14): venue count *enables mobilisation*, veto count *blocks change*, and they are not the same measurement.

Against Dixit ([[incentives-under-multiple-principals]]) the relation is sharper and more interesting: Dixit's `m = 1/(1+nrcv)` says that adding noncooperating principals mechanically collapses incentive strength — polyarchy causes paralysis. Sabel & Zeitlin say polyarchy is *generative* of a new accountability form that substitutes for incentive-based accountability. Neither engages the other's mechanism. **Candidate synthesis worth stating and not adopting: experimentalist governance as an institutional response to the Dixit problem** — dynamic accountability being what is left when explicit incentives cannot be made strong because too many uncoordinated principals own the outcome.

## Cases, and what they are worth

**[b — illustration]** The recurring case set: the EU Water Framework Directive and its Common Implementation Strategy (provisional "good water status" metrics, revised through comitology, producing both binding and non-binding standards from the same architecture); HACCP food-safety regulation and nuclear and air-traffic event notification (firms build and continually verify their own hazard-detection systems, with public review of plan adequacy); child welfare via Quality Service Review; school districts; the EU FLEGT timber legality-assurance regime and its supply-chain traceability requirements.

Two of these supply axes the wiki did not have. **Monitoring trigger**: routine stratified-sample intensive case review (QSR — scheduled, systemic) vs. **event notification** (triggered only by a disruption or near-miss). **Enforcement mechanism**: direct sanction vs. penalty default / destabilization right. Both are recorded in [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] commentary rather than as their own rows, since neither source supplies a measurement procedure that separates them cleanly from D9 and D42.

FLEGT is also the chapter's only concrete economic-production claim: illegal logging depresses prices for legally harvested wood and undercuts sustainable producers, so a legality-assurance licensing regime is explicitly a **rent-reallocation** mechanism favouring compliant firms.

## What is not established

- **No failed case, anywhere.** The single most important limitation.
- **No base rate, no effect size, no counterfactual.** For every lever: peer review is never compared against an alternative monitoring regime; mandated discretion is never compared against discretion without a reporting obligation; the penalty-default mechanism is never observed absent.
- **The cross-sector generality claim is asserted, not tested.** The chapter states the architecture applies to public regulation of private firms and to public service provision alike, and juxtaposes examples of both — but never compares success or mechanism strength across the two sets.
- **Silent on scale and age.** No claim about headcount, budget, hierarchy depth or years since founding. The nearest analogue — a "widening circle of actors" over successive cycles — is scope of participation, not size. Flag this as a gap, not as an omitted finding.
- **Silent on how central actors are selected or removed.** Commission officials, national Water Directors and agency administrators appear as actors with no account of who appoints or can remove them.
- **Advocacy-adjacent.** The authors originated both "experimentalist governance" and "directly deliberative polyarchy", and the chapter is built almost entirely on self-citation to their own prior case studies. They self-locate on "the 'optimistic' side of the post-modern family of views" about diffuse power. The conclusion — that experimentalism durably overcomes structural barriers to reform — outruns the evidence presented *in this chapter*; if stronger grounding exists it is in the cited but unexamined external literature.
