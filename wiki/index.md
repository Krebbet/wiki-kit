# Wiki Index

A general theory of institutions: how they function, what makes them function better or worse, and how they change with scale and age — carrying their interlock with the distribution of power and with economic production as the working decomposition under test rather than as the subject (Q17), and the decay-with-age premise likewise, five research jobs having so far found it unevidenced at the level of a single institution (Q60).

Catalog of all pages in this wiki. Updated on every ingest.

| [[credible-commitment]] | Why long-horizon investment needs a ruler who *cannot* expropriate: shackling vs. self-restraint, the Glorious Revolution case, and a market-priced measure of commitment. |
| [[polycentric-governance]] | Ostrom's case against the state/market dichotomy: the four-goods typology, the eight design principles verbatim, monitoring, nesting and scale. **Corrected 2026-08-19**: the design-principles evidence now comes from the primary source (Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás 2010 — 91 studies, 77 cases, mean support 3.73/5, every principle positively correlated, nesting weakest at the 10% level), replacing a secondhand "100+ studies / two-thirds confirm" figure taken from Ostrom's Nobel lecture that appears nowhere in the primary. Carries the note that Cox measures **survival of existing commons systems, not reform outcomes**. |
| [[bureaucratic-growth-and-parkinsons-law]] | Why bureaus grow: Parkinson's two motive forces (assertion only, and our capture of the essay is partial), Klimek/Hanel/Thurner's quantified test (cabinet threshold N≈20–21, Colonial Office +6%/yr, Navy +80% with headcount decoupled from workload), and Niskanen's budget-maximising bureau as the contrasting mechanism. **A third channel added 2026-08-20**: Downs's climber ambition + rational territorial self-protection + life-cycle scope expansion, distinct from Niskanen because it contains **no budget variable and no size variable at all** — and with no evidence whatsoever, against Niskanen's invented numbers and Parkinson/Klimek's n=2 series. The three-way comparison table now scores each account on budget, size, age, slack and evidence. **A framing tension added 2026-08-20, deliberately not filed as a conflict**: Aghion & Tirole prove it is *always* optimal for a firm to run in overload, because genuine overload is the only credible commitment not to stifle initiative — the opposite reading of the same observable, from a model with no data, against three accounts with almost none. What it supplies is a null hypothesis any bloat diagnostic has to clear. |
| [[knowledge-hierarchies-and-the-cost-of-scale]] | Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg: layers and span derived from the time constraint on knowledge. Larger output rationally buys more layers; growth *by adding a layer* cuts existing layers' wages, growth *without* one raises them; communication cost and information cost move depth in opposite directions. |
| [[hierarchy-and-near-decomposability]] | Simon 1962: hierarchy as what can be *built* — assembly time scaling with levels rather than elements — plus near-decomposability as the formal licence for delegating on aggregates. Simon disclaims his own social extrapolations. |
| [[risk-aversion-in-large-institutions]] | The umbrella page on institutional timidity, organised around two crossed distinctions (individual-payoff vs. structural mechanism; size claim vs. sector claim). **Headline: the batch does not support the presupposition it was commissioned under.** No source tests size against risk-taking; the one direct measurement (Nicholson-Crotty, n=300 randomized experiment) tests sector and finds against it. Embeds that experiment and the OECD advocacy synthesis. |
| [[blame-avoidance-and-negativity-bias]] | Hood: blame risk is weighted above credit (negativity bias + fire-alarm oversight), producing a "mediocrity bias" and organisational architecture built for deniability. Individual career payoffs, not size; public-sector evidence only, private extension asserted. An argumentative chapter, not a study. |
| [[isolation-errors-and-portfolio-framing]] | Kahneman & Lovallo: loss aversion plus narrow framing price a portfolio 27% below its pooled value — paired with an equally-evidenced *bold-forecast* bias. The authors explicitly deny that managerial decisions are "generally risk averse". Private-sector evidence only; no institutional size variable. |
| [[career-concerns-and-managerial-risk-taking]] | Holmström: a **risk-neutral** manager acts risk-averse because projects are tests of talent priced by an external labour market; unverifiable private information collapses the equilibrium to zero investment. Explicitly rejects the size-based risk-bearing-capacity account. |
| [[exploration-exploitation-incentive-contracts]] | Manso: the contract that motivates exploration (path-dependent pay, commitment, feedback, job security) is structurally opposite to the one that motivates exploitation. Holds for risk-neutral agents — **a moral-hazard result, not a risk-preference result.** No size parameter. |
| [[red-tape]] | Bozeman: rules that impose burden and achieve nothing, distinguished sharply from neutral formalisation. Two origin pathways, an external-control model, and a four-quadrant typology. Most causes are general to any externally-controlled organisation; the word "risk" never appears in the source. |
| [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]] | Hammond: bureaucratic discretion is a function of veto-point count *interacting with* preference divergence, and count alone predicts nothing — "anything can happen". Never uses the word "risk", and **undercuts** the more-veto-points-implies-more-caution story rather than supporting it. |
| [[multitask-incentive-theory]] | Holmstrom & Milgrom plus Holmström's Nobel lecture: when tasks compete for one attention budget and only some are measurable, rewarding the measurable ones destroys effort on the rest — so fixed pay, conduct rules and bureaucratic rule-boundedness are the rational design response, not a pathology. All seven propositions untested by the authors' own account; carries Prendergast's measured selection-vs-incentive decomposition and his "not a ringing endorsement" verdict. **Extended 2026-08-20**: Gibbons's employee-vs-contractor derivation gives a **third** formal route to low-powered incentives inside firms, and his signal-jamming model *derives* the influence-cost mechanism this page previously carried as assertion. |
| [[property-rights-theory-of-the-firm]] | Grossman & Hart plus Hart's Nobel lecture: ownership *is* the residual right of control, so it sets each party's ex post bargaining position and hence their ex ante non-contractible investment. Integration is never a free lunch. Includes the contractibility/verifiability/observability split, the private-prison public/private application, and Hart-Moore "shading" kept separate because it abandons full rationality. **Extended 2026-08-20** with Gibbons's "drone employees" critique — the theory has no internal-organisation content by construction, not by oversight — his refined contractibility taxonomy (the never-contractible decision is a separate ingredient generating a separate theory), and two terminology traps recorded so neither is mistaken for a conflict: residual *claimant* vs. residual *control rights*, and Fama's "ownership is irrelevant" vs. this page's "ownership is control". |
| [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]] | Dixit: the batch's only direct public-sector source. `m = 1/(1+nrcv)` — noncooperating principals mechanically collapse incentive strength — plus Wilson's action × outcome observability typology and the claim that public/private differences are "of degree, not kind". Micromanagement reframed as rational response to unobservable outcomes owned by uncoordinated principals. |
| [[motivation-crowding]] | One phenomenon, two distinct mechanisms kept apart: Frey's psychological self-determination/self-esteem channel (field evidence largely correlational, self-citation-heavy, and *not* flagged by him as selection-contaminated) and Bénabou & Tirole's Bayesian channel in which the reward's *size* is itself bad news about the task. The latter's source is a slide deck with no test of its own propositions. |
| [[institutional-isomorphism]] | DiMaggio & Powell: coercive, mimetic and normative pressure push organisations in a **field** toward homogeneity independent of efficiency, with professionalisation and credentialed hiring as the selection channel. Field-level, not society-level. Unusually explicit for this literature — twelve numbered directional hypotheses, tabulated here, and **none tested by the authors**. |
| [[institutional-myths-and-decoupling]] | Meyer & Rowan: formal structure adopted as **ceremony** to satisfy rationalised myths and buy legitimacy, then **decoupled** from actual work and sustained by a "logic of confidence and good faith" that suppresses inspection. Scoped by the authors to organisations in institutionalised — not output-tested — environments, and that condition is kept on the page. Supplies no age or size evidence and says so. |
| [[gradual-institutional-change]] | Thelen (with Streeck and Mahoney): layering, drift, conversion and displacement, predicted by veto possibilities × interpretive discretion. Positive feedback preserves the form, not the function — German VET, built in 1897 to weaken labour, became the core of social partnership. **Argues contestation begins as soon as a rule is laid down**, which denies an age-gated model. Paired with the Krasner page. |
| [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]] | Krasner: long stasis broken by crisis-driven reorganisation — stated as a **metaphor borrowed from evolutionary biology**, with no ex ante criterion for when a shock produces a punctuation, and with his own cited case data (Skowronek's 20-year "punctuation" against a 23-year "stasis") not clearly supporting the short-burst claim. The sunk-cost stability half is stronger than the change half. Paired with the Thelen page. |
| [[institutional-sclerosis]] | **The anchor of the `institutional-stagnation` batch's verdict.** Olson's thesis — coalitions accumulate in stable societies and crowd out growth, cleared only by rupture — as reviewed by Heckelman across **53 studies: 57% support, 23% mixed, 21% no support**, with no significant difference by method, outlet, author location or year. Two things kept inseparable from that headline: only "a handful" directly test coalition *accumulation*, and the duration variable is always **national political stability, never an institution's own age**. Carries Heckelman's disclosed conflict of interest and self-check, and the practitioner survey where economists (2.53) and political scientists (2.39) disbelieve the theory's signature prediction. |
| [[regulatory-capture]] | Three sources steelmanned on one page. Stigler's acquired-regulation model, with what he actually tested marked out — one robust trucking regression, one licensing regression he calls non-robust, and otherwise illustration — plus the self-immunising treatment of counterexamples. Carpenter & Moss's three-part evidentiary test, the strong/weak/**corrosive**/cultural typology, and their deconstruction of the 1892 Olney letter. Novak's ground-norm critique and Charles Francis Adams anticipating capture at the ICC's 1871 design. **Capture-as-ageing is the batch's one genuinely contested claim; all three sources locate capture at or before founding.** |
| [[goal-displacement-and-bureaucratic-ritualism]] | Merton 1940: the features that make bureaucracy reliable — rule-generality, career discipline, esprit de corps, impersonality — over-cultivate the sentiments attached to rules until "an instrumental value becomes a terminal value". A conceptual essay with **no data, no falsification condition and no levers**, and **explicitly age- and size-independent** by the author's own construction. His one precise scope claim runs on monopoly vs. competition, not public vs. private. **Contested from 2026-08-20** by Downs's C.11, which ties the same displacement pattern to bureau age — filed at [[downs-vs-merton-on-age-dependence]] as a clash of two undata'd **models**, with the page's standing instruction unchanged: neither source may be cited as evidence for or against age-driven decay. |
| [[cooptation]] | Selznick 1948: formal cooptation buys legitimacy by sharing "the responsibility for power rather than power itself"; informal cooptation surrenders "the substance of power and not its forms" to an organised power centre, and is kept hidden because admitting it would itself destroy legitimacy. Theory only — **the TVA case study is a different source this wiki has not read**, so cooptation's empirical grounding is absent here. Explicitly not age-linked, and carries Selznick's own warning against attributing exogenous size changes to organisational theory. |
| [[regulatory-accretion]] | Miller & Dudley: rules accumulate because **creation is gatekept and survival is not** — OIRA clears new rules, nothing reviews old ones, and no consequence attaches to leaving one unexamined. Compounded by vested-interest lock-in (the JPMorgan "moat") and by rules written without the metrics needed to evaluate them. System-level over 130 years of the US regulatory corpus; **they claim a time series and do not reproduce one**, and the single dataset is cross-sectional within one year. |
| [[political-decay-and-vetocracy]] | Fukuyama: vetocracy, judicialisation of administration and interest-group "repatrimonialization", worked through the Forest Service's un-discarded mandate layers. An **essay, not a study** — narrative plus secondary citation plus illustrative statistics. Records that his own evidence undercuts a smooth age-decay reading: a 230-year-old veto structure whose dysfunction he dates to 1980s polarisation, and two earlier crises from the same structure. |
| [[state-capacity-and-level-of-government]] | Schleicher & Bagley: the state-capacity debate is aimed at the wrong tier. Three pathologies at state and local level — nationalised second-order elections, a procedural "tax" whose fixed cost bites smaller institutions harder, and legally procyclical fiscal capacity. The batch's cleanest instance of the level-of-analysis substitution: the procedural accumulation is a **1970s–80s legal regime imposed identically on a new agency and a century-old one**. Their own best evidence (Caughey & Warshaw, ~70% eventual majority-alignment) cuts against them. |
| [[reorganisation-base-rate]] | **The anchor of the `enabling-institutional-change` batch and the page carrying its headline finding.** NAO's near-census audit: 93 UK central-government reorganisations 2005–09, 51 surveyed, £780m (an under-estimate on seven stated grounds), value for money **"cannot be demonstrated"**, **0% of departments setting benefit metrics** against 73% of arm's length bodies. NAO's criterion is evidential, not outcome-based — business case, ex-ante projection, post-hoc tracking — so a low score means nobody *can* tell, not that it failed. Records the batch's base-rate position: **only two of eight sources give a reform base rate, both show measured success overstating genuine change, and the wiki has no basis to generalise beyond those two populations.** |
| [[political-economy-of-public-sector-reform]] | Bunse & Fritz: ~134 World Bank public-sector-reform operations FY2000–10. **75% operation-level success against 47% country-level transformation**, with the gap attributed by the authors themselves to selecting politically easy, non-rent-threatening components — the lever that raises the measured rate is partly what causes the gap. Underneath: concentrated immediate costs, diffuse benefits taking **10–20 years**, and limited access orders in which rent distribution buys elite peace. 78% of failed operations cite political-economy obstacles. |
| [[personnel-economics-of-the-state]] | **Extended 2026-08-20 with Besley/Burgess/Khan/Xu, after an explicit study-by-study de-duplication check** (six studies shared, adding citation weight and no new numbers): selection under rules vs. discretion with an unstable sign, the thin causal evidence on entry exams, performance-based postings, autonomy as a sign-flipping lever, the priced downside of seniority rules, and the embeddedness-vs-rotation tension with four studies each way. Underneath, unchanged — Finan, Olken & Pande: ~40 field experiments on state personnel — **the strongest evidence base in its batch by a wide margin**, and the source of every tier-(i) lever in the wiki. Randomised recruitment wages (+35.2% fill rate, elasticity 2.15), career-vs-mission ad framing, teacher performance pay (+0.27 SD at 2 years, +0.54 SD at 5), audits (−8pp unaccounted expenditure), fixed-salary randomised auditors, scorecards that work only **with** deliberation. **Tax-collector performance pay raised revenue growth 46% *and* raised bribery** — both halves always reported. Closes the personnel-selection gap open since the foundations batch. |
| [[experimentalist-governance]] | Sabel & Zeitlin: provisional framework goals, delegated local discretion, peer review against units doing it differently, periodic revision — "dynamic accountability" replacing rule-conformance, with destabilization regimes and penalty defaults to unblock entrenched interests. **Theory from the paradigm's own originators: zero failed cases examined, no base rates, no effect sizes reproduced for its two strongest empirical claims.** Scope conditions stated by the authors: strategic uncertainty and polyarchic power. |
| [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-policy-change]] | True, Jones & Baumgartner: serial attention plus institutional friction plus venue multiplicity produce **leptokurtic** policy and budget change, replicated across ~9 countries and multiple independent datasets. Punctuation is stronger at lower levels of aggregation and transmits top-down more readily than bottom-up. **A crisis opens a window; it does not guarantee reform** — agenda access is a precondition, subsystems resist, venue-shopping backfires. A distribution shape, not a success rate. The citable form of the PE claim; Krasner supplies the state-theory lineage. |
| [[isomorphic-mimicry-and-capability-traps]] | Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock: donors fund and legitimate the **form** of best-practice institutions, so governments adopt forms without function and settle into self-reinforcing capability traps. Diagnosis and prescription kept strictly apart on the page — the diagnosis rests on ~5 hand-picked cases with **no base rate anywhere in the paper**, and PDIA's four principles are **explicitly untested by the authors at publication** in their own words. Bunse & Fritz's 75%/47% gap is the quantified instance of the same form/function mechanism. |
| [[ambidexterity-as-a-dynamic-capability]] | O'Reilly & Tushman — **the wiki's only private-sector renewal source, and it is a draft working paper.** Separately aligned exploit and explore subunits held together by shared vision, common-fate senior incentives and targeted integration. ~5 recurring success-firm narratives restated across five propositions, ~9 successes to 1 thin failure, and **no base rate for ambidexterity attempts** — the mortality statistics it quotes are base rates for the problem, not the solution. "Dynamic capability" is partly circular by the authors' own definition and is registered `rejected` at D77. Supplies resource vs. **routine** rigidity, which is sharper than "inertia". |
| [[reform-levers]] | **The manifesto triage (project outcome 3).** Every lever the `enabling-institutional-change` batch surfaced, sorted into three explicitly labelled tiers by *kind of evidence*: **(i) randomised/quasi-experimental** — recruitment wages, ad framing, tax-collector performance pay (with the bribery side-effect), teacher performance pay, fixed-salary randomised auditors, government audits, scorecards with facilitated deliberation, biometric payment rails; **(ii) plausible, case-illustrated only** — windows of opportunity, ex-ante political-economy analysis, structural ambidexterity, the experimentalist cycle, incremental adjustment, accountability checklists; **(iii) pure prescription** — PDIA's four principles, "quick wins" (contradicted by their own source's cases), integrity screening, five of NAO's six recommendations. Nothing in tier (iii) is advice this wiki is giving. |
| [[colonial-origins-and-the-settler-mortality-instrument]] | **The anchor of the `institutions-and-growth` batch, and the wiki's page on cross-country causal identification of institutional effects on income.** AJR's colonial-origins and reversal-of-fortune papers together, since they share one instrument. The contested section is **co-equal, not a caveat** — that structure is the finding. Carries Albouy's data audit in full (**36 of 64 mortality rates borrowed or conjectured, 22 of those from two thin extrapolations**; first stage insignificant and sign-reversing after correction; Anderson–Rubin confidence regions unbounded), Glaeser et al.'s durable-rule-vs-outcome measurement attack and their 2SLS horse race in which predicted schooling beats predicted executive constraints, and Deaton's general external-≠-exogenous / LATE-≠-ATE framework **with an explicit correction: Deaton names AJR once, in a list, and does not audit them.** States what is well-evidenced, what is theorised, what is contested and what is evidenced against. |
| [[institutions-as-fundamental-cause]] | AJR's Handbook chapter: the theory behind the regressions. De jure vs. de facto power, the commitment problem that makes efficiency and distribution inseparable, and three failure channels — holdup, political losers, economic losers — which the authors themselves rank, political losers strongest and economic losers weakest. Records their own verdicts on their own work: the framework is "largely verbal rather than mathematical, and thus, by its very nature, not fully specified", and "we do not as yet have a satisfactory understanding of the mechanisms through which institutions persist". |
| [[institutions-vs-geography-vs-trade-horse-race]] | Rodrik, Subramanian & Trebbi: institutions "trump" geography and trade openness in a simultaneous IV horse race. **Kept separate from the anchor page but explicitly not independent of it** — the institutions instrument is AJR's, imported and not re-defended. Carries the three things the paper reports against itself and that citations drop: the **over-identification tests fail** in the larger sample (p = 0.0071, 0.0365), settler mortality is not cleanly excluded from the trade equation, and the first stage decays by decade. Plus "An instrument does not a theory make" and the China/Russia contrast. |
| [[schooling-and-institutional-quality]] | **The schooling hypothesis, on one source, marked for expansion.** Glaeser, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer's positive claim — that human capital is upstream of institutional quality rather than downstream of it — separated from their measurement critique, which lives on the anchor page. Carries their Table 12 sequencing panel coefficient by coefficient (initial schooling predicts five-year institutional improvement on three of four measures; no political measure predicts schooling growth; **initial income predicts schooling growth strongly**, making the paper's own chain income → schooling → institutions rather than schooling-first), the Table 11 2SLS horse race with its unremarked **0.82 correlation between the two predicted regressors** and its wrong-signed institutions coefficient, and the Lipset/Djankov mechanism, which the paper adopts by citation and never measures. **One paper, one team, a party to the dispute, no recorded replication** — the page says so and is deliberately provisional pending research job 17, to be rewritten rather than patched. |
| [[endogenous-state-capacity]] | Besley & Persson: fiscal and legal capacity as an **investment a ruling group chooses**, complementary in payoff, driven by common-interest demand, political stability and inclusiveness — with the investment trap (Proposition 4) as a forward-looking mechanism for persistent state weakness that needs no accumulation and no lock-in, only correct forecasting. **An affirmative-camp source that denies the causal reading of its own correlations** and states that causation runs from income to taxation, not the reverse. |
| [[developmental-state-and-embedded-autonomy]] | Wade: East Asian catch-up driven by directed credit, protection with tariff rebates, hard MNC bargaining and above all **performance conditions withdrawn on failure** — discipline running from state onto capital, the reverse of the shackling story. **His fastest-growing cases score low on AJR's own constraint axis**, which is a direct tension with [[credible-commitment]]. Embedded autonomy registered `rejected` (D94) on the strength of his own Bank of England counter-case. Evidence tiered sharply within the source: one quasi-experimental sector panel, otherwise comparative history and fieldwork by a committed advocate. |
| [[rent-seeking-and-the-welfare-cost-of-transfers]] | Tullock 1967, and the page exists partly to hold a boundary the wiki had blurred. The precise claim is narrow: the standard deadweight-loss triangle **undercounts** a transfer's social cost by the resources rational actors spend competing to capture or defend it, dissipating up to the whole prize. Proved invariant to legality via the theft detour — the cost of theft is the thief's effort plus the locks and the police, not the stolen goods. **A formal model with no measurement, by the author's own statement** ("I can suggest no way of measuring these expenditures"), and the one place it overreaches is named. "Rent-seeking" is Krueger's later coinage, not Tullock's term. No institution, no bureau, no age, no size. |
| [[rational-actor-accounts-of-bureaucratic-behaviour]] | Downs (1964) and Wilson (1967) paired, and **both contain zero empirical content — that is the page's first line, not a caveat**. Downs: a bureau defined structurally (market-evaluability, not ownership — GM is not a bureau, Chevrolet's PR department is), five official types, ~20 asserted hypotheses. Wilson: five *incompatible* bureaucracy problems, with **fiscal integrity winning by default because it is the only observable one**. Carries the provenance correction that **Wilson's 1967 essay contains no version of the production/procedural/craft/coping typology** — that is the 1989 book, which this wiki does not hold — and Downs's C.11, the first age-dependent decay mechanism asserted by any source in this project. |
| [[weberian-structure-and-growth]] | Evans & Rauch's 35-country, 126-expert survey (**r = .67, p < .001** against 1970–90 growth, robust to income and human capital, **not** robust with all three regional dummies, and the authors disclose it) paired with Rauch's 144-city Progressive-Era natural experiment (civil-service adoption raises the infrastructure share, 0.0147 SE 0.0057; **manufacturing-employment effect robust, value-added effect not significant**; the growth figure is an extrapolation across two papers). **Opens with the Weber gap**: the ten-item Weberianness Scale is an operationalisation of two features chosen for measurability, whose authors deliberately excluded rule-governed jurisdiction as "a double-edged sword… producing rigidity and organizational sclerosis". Never decomposed by scale item. |
| [[civil-service-tenure-and-political-insulation]] | Guo Xu's two-margin model — **entry protection (γ) changes who is appointed, progression protection (κ) changes their behaviour once in post** — with the result that the two are welfare complements or substitutes depending on which party is more biased, so **the sign flips on a quantity nobody measures**. The reform inventory is reported whole: mostly positive, **two nulls and one negative**, plus the author's own entrenchment counter-case (Bazzi et al.: less protection improved delivery where protection was already high). Aneja & Xu attribute the Pendleton Act's gains to the **tenure** margin, not the exam. Explicitly not an argument for maximal protection. |
| [[organizational-economics-of-the-state]] | Besley, Burgess, Khan & Xu's systems-level half: multiple principals, M-form architecture, O-ring interdependence, independent-agency design, and **capture reframed as one information channel with two outcomes** — firms supply genuinely needed private information, or bribe to misreport it. **Section 4 is agenda-setting and contains no evidence at all, which the authors state**, and they give two structural reasons the gap may be permanent. Carries the "Weberian Facts" (country fixed effects explain **73%** of variation — national persistence, not institutional age) and the paper's two precise, opposite public/private claims. |
| [[team-production-and-monitoring]] | Alchian & Demsetz: the firm as a solution to a metering problem. Nonseparable production (∂²Z/∂Xi∂Xj > 0) makes individual marginal product unmeterable, so a specialist **monitor** is made **residual claimant** to solve the who-monitors-the-monitor regress, bundled with central-contracting status, the right to alter any one member's contract, and the right to sell the bundle. Generates the whole firm typology by varying who holds the residual claim and whether it can be capitalized — and predicts more shirking in mutuals and nonprofits, asserted with no data. **Untested theory throughout, by the authors' own labelling.** Carries the terminology note separating residual **claimant** from residual **control rights**. |
| [[agency-costs-and-ownership-structure]] | Jensen & Meckling: agency cost decomposed as **monitoring + bonding + residual loss**, and the pricing argument that makes it bite — a rational buyer marks down what he pays, so the whole loss lands on the owner-manager rather than being captured by anyone. Plus asset substitution under debt (equity as a call option). **A formal model with no test of its own predictions**, whose three cited datasets anchor assumptions rather than test comparative statics, and which explicitly defers both the control right (to "a future paper") and the very large low-managerial-equity corporation. |
| [[separation-of-ownership-and-control]] | Fama: separation of security ownership from control is efficient, disciplined by the managerial labour market, a self-interested board and takeovers as last resort. **Carries a correction to this wiki's own commissioning brief**: the initiation / ratification / implementation / monitoring decomposition of decision rights is **not** in this paper — it is Fama & Jensen (1983), which the wiki does not hold, and the taxonomy has deliberately not been written from memory. Also the "ownership is irrelevant" line, and why it is not a contradiction of Grossman-Hart. No measurement of any kind. |
| [[organizational-capabilities-and-the-m-form]] | Chandler on capabilities and the M-form decision-rights split (general office: strategy, allocation, monitoring; divisions: operations). **The captured source is his own compressed synthesis of two books this wiki has not read**, so every empirical claim is one remove from its archive and the article supplies no coding scheme or ranking criterion. Carries the inverted-U — capability compounds, then capability-blind diversification forces retrenchment via the takeover market — the explicit capital-intensive scope restriction, and the British-failure residual he declines to explain. |
| [[formal-and-real-authority]] | Aghion & Tirole: the right to decide against effective control over decisions, with the gap located entirely in information. An uninformed principal optimally **rubber-stamps** a better-informed subordinate. Full comparative statics (overload, delegation, urgency, reputation, performance measurement, multiple principals), the **optimal-overload-as-credible-commitment** result, an age mechanism running the opposite way to the decay literature, and the authors' own list of measurement instruments they propose and do not build. Explicitly domain-general, including political hierarchies. |
| [[decision-rights-and-authority-theory-of-the-firm]] | Gibbons: four elemental theories of the firm — rent-seeking, property-rights, incentive-system, **adaptation/authority** — shown to be **formally orthogonal, not nested**, which refutes the common reading that Grossman-Hart formalised Williamson ("just plain wrong"). Houses the fourth theory, which needs **no specific investment at all**, plus the payoff-rights/decision-rights and control-branch/contract-branch distinctions, and the internal rent-seeking / influence-cost model in which holding a decision right invites lobbying proportional to the lobbyist's stake. |
| [[corporate-governance-index-and-firm-control-rights]] | **The wiki's first document-scored institution-level instrument.** Gompers, Ishii & Metrick's 24 provisions hand-coded from charter, bylaws and state law across ~1,500 US public firms/year, predicting an **8.5%/yr return spread** and a **−2.4pp to −8.9pp Tobin's Q effect** — with the authors **explicitly refusing a causal reading** and setting out three rival stories their design cannot separate. Scoreable tomorrow from documents; generalising beyond US public companies needs an equivalent settled taxonomy of control-shifting provisions built first. |
| [[world-management-survey]] | **The wiki's first live-respondent institution-level instrument.** Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen's 18-item, 1–5, double-blind interview across **11,383 firms in 34 countries**; management explains **~30% of TFP gaps with the US**, and **77% of the variation sits within country-and-industry** — the batch's most important number, and the first direct evidence that a measurable dimension of institutional quality is a property of the organisation, not the nation. Works on private firms by construction and, untested, possibly on bureaus; fails on scripted answers rather than on decoupled documents. |

## Open Conflicts

| Conflict | Status | Summary |
|---|---|---|
| [[efficiency-of-institutions-north-vs-williamson]] | OPEN | Williamson presumes governance arrangements are cost-economising (remediableness); North denies any efficiency default and gives a mechanism for inefficient lock-in. |
| [[functional-vs-rent-seeking-growth]] | OPEN | **Now three mechanisms, not two.** Niskanen: bureau growth is budget maximisation and distorts output even with zero internal slack. Garicano/Rossi-Hansberg and Simon: added layers are cost-minimising responses to scale. **Meyer & Rowan and DiMaggio & Powell (added 2026-08-19): structure is ceremony adopted for legitimacy, independent of both output and budget** — and it predicts the efficiency audit will not happen at all. Probably a scope condition on funding form for the first two — but no source says so, so not adopted. |
| [[parkinson-vs-knowledge-hierarchy-growth]] | OPEN | Headcount growth independent of workload (Parkinson, Klimek et al.) vs. layers and headcount set by output and knowledge cost (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg). Each evidenced only inside its own domain — public bureaus vs. private firms — and never tested on the other's cases. |
| [[self-organisation-vs-property-rights-in-commons]] | OPEN | Ostrom: user self-organisation routinely outperforms state and market. Libecap: private bargaining fails through holdout and heterogeneity; assign secure transferable rights. Likely a scope condition, not yet argued. |
| [[rival-firm-boundary-theories]] | OPEN | **Four mechanisms since 2026-08-20, not three.** Williamson's asset specificity, Grossman-Hart's residual control rights, Holmstrom-Milgrom's measurement cost, and — added by Gibbons — **adaptation/authority**, which requires zero specific investment. Gibbons's headline is that the four are **formally orthogonal, not nested**: their ingredient sets are disjoint, so "Grossman and Hart formalized Williamson" is "just plain wrong", and Williamson's own corpus contains two of the four. His candidate discriminating test (only the margins matter, for the property-rights theory) comes with his own flag that the relevant margins are unobservable, so it is likely untestable and unrun. Underneath it all, Masten-Meehan-Snyder: the standard asset-specificity→integration regression **cannot identify causal direction**, and Gibbons generalises that to all four. Also carries Chandler against Williamson on whether four historical integration decisions were "mistakes". |
| [[presumed-vs-measured-public-sector-risk-aversion]] | OPEN | Hood, Bozeman, Hammond and the OECD presume or assert a public/private risk-aversion gap; none tests it. Nicholson-Crotty et al. test it directly (randomized experiment, n=300 real managers) and find no support, with a reversal under loss framing. Settled only by a design measuring behaviour and structural variables on the same subjects — which no source does. |
| [[rival-models-of-institutional-change]] | OPEN | **The headline conflict of the `institutional-evolution` batch.** Three incompatible accounts of how institutions change: lock-in deepens with age (North, Pierson, Krasner's stability half), punctuated by exogenous crisis (Krasner), and gradual endogenous transformation running continuously from founding (Thelen). They disagree about no observation — they classify the same observations differently, and German VET fits all three. None supplies an ex ante criterion for when a shock produces discontinuity. |
| [[homogenisation-vs-loose-coupling]] | OPEN | Within one tradition: from the same antecedent (uncertainty, weak technology), DiMaggio & Powell predict substantive homogenisation and *less* internal variation; Meyer & Rowan predict loose coupling and *more*. DiMaggio & Powell name the divergence themselves at Hypothesis A-3. Neither tests it, so "sociological institutionalism" is not a usable citation about internal behaviour without naming the limb. |
| [[does-institutions-growth-survive-identification]] | OPEN | **The central conflict of the `institutions-and-growth` batch, and it sits on the user's own working thesis.** Not "do institutions matter" — both camps agree the index/income correlation exists. The dispute is whether the settler-mortality instrument identifies *institutions*, and whether the indices measure institutions at all. Position A: AJR ×2 and Rodrik et al., an unusually broad robustness programme reproducing across two vendors and two teams. Position B: Albouy's archival audit and re-estimation, Glaeser et al.'s measurement critique and 2SLS horse race, Deaton's framework. **Position A is internally split** — Rodrik's over-ID test fails and he disclaims policy lessons, Besley & Persson deny their own correlations are causal, AJR concede the culture confound and call their framework not fully specified, and Wade's fastest growers score low on AJR's constraint axis. Settlement criterion: an independently reconstructed mortality series with transparent provenance, **plus** a timing/sequencing design — only Glaeser et al.'s Table 12 attempts the latter, and it runs against Position A. |
| [[constraint-vs-capacity-as-the-investment-mechanism]] | OPEN | **A direct tension with an existing page.** [[credible-commitment]] states that self-restraint fails and only structural shackling secures investment. Wade's Korea, Taiwan and Singapore sustained record investment under a military dictatorship and two one-party states; Glaeser et al. show the standard indices cannot tell a ruler who *cannot* expropriate from one who *chooses not to*; Rodrik's China/Russia contrast concedes formal legal form is not the operative variable; Besley & Persson locate the constraint on the ruler's own *turnover risk* instead. Two unargued reconciliations recorded — different objects (entitlement-level vs. programme-level), and selectivity as a reputational commitment technology. Cheapest discriminating test: run Libecap's lease-to-sale price ratio on state-granted entitlements inside a developmental state. Neither camp has. |
| [[downs-vs-merton-on-age-dependence]] | OPEN | **A conflict between two models, not between two bodies of evidence — neither side has any data, and the file exists to stop it being read as movement on the age question.** Downs's C.11 ties goal displacement explicitly to elapsed bureau age (rules accumulate, goals shift toward self-maintenance, inertia rises, scope expands); Merton's mechanism is age-independent by his own unprompted construction. Downs is a bare assertion with no derivation and no falsification condition; Merton is a conceptual essay. **What changed is the shape of the dispute**: the affirmative side now has a mechanism-level theoretical statement at organisational level where it previously had only regime, corpus and polity duration proxies. Downs's five directional predictions against Merton's flat line are the sharpest discriminating test the age literature has produced here, and it is unrun. |
| [[decay-as-real-vs-decay-as-overstated]] | OPEN | **The central conflict of the `institutional-stagnation` batch and the one the wiki's own thesis turns on.** Not decay-yes vs. decay-no: both camps agree rigidity, accretion and capture are real. The dispute is whether an institution's **own elapsed age** is the driver, and whether the evidence usually cited for that survives inspection. Position A has Olson/Heckelman's 53-study count, Fukuyama, Miller & Dudley, Schleicher & Bagley, and second-hand Huntington/Bernstein/Olney. Position B has Merton, Stigler and Selznick each disowning the age reading of his own mechanism unprompted, Carpenter & Moss and Novak dismantling the canonical age evidence, and Fukuyama and Schleicher & Bagley partly undercutting themselves. **The age reading is unevidenced, not refuted** — the critics run no age test either. Settlement criterion recorded: a specific institution's own age against a directly observed accumulation variable, not regime-stability duration or cross-sectional density. |
