# Neo-Weberian State and Administrative Traditions

Bouckaert — co-originator of the Neo-Weberian State (NWS) concept — argues public-sector reform trajectories since the 1970s cluster by **legal/administrative tradition**, not by a single universal reform sequence: New Public Management (NPM) "could only develop, initially and substantially, in Anglo-Saxon countries which have a 'common law' base"; NWS — a *modernisation* of Weberian hierarchy rather than its replacement by markets or networks — "seems to develop more easily in countries which have an 'administrative law' tradition," tracing a path-dependency back through the Napoleonic administrative lineage. This is this batch's most direct, explicit test of whether the wiki's existing bureaucracy/reform framework is a universal sequence or an artefact of the mostly Anglo-American sources (Wilson, Niskanen, NPM-adjacent literature) it was built from.

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** — Bouckaert's own three-decade comparative synthesis (with Pollitt), not new data collection in this paper. Cited **[empirical]** sub-claims are attributed to named studies throughout (the COCOPS EU civil-servant survey; Kuhlmann et al.'s ~10-year evaluation of Germany's "New Steering Model" reform).

## The claim that most directly bears on this wiki's framework

**[model]** NPM's hierarchy is a principal-agent relation subordinate to markets; NWS's hierarchy is law-based authority; NPG's (New Public Governance) is informal, embedded in networks. NWS retains four Weberian elements (state as central problem-solver, representative democracy, administrative law protecting citizen-state relations, distinct public-service status) while adding four modifications (citizen-oriented rather than purely rule-following culture; participatory devices supplementing, not replacing, representative democracy; ex-post results controls alongside ex-ante procedure; professionalisation of bureaucrats into manager-professionals) — Table 1 of the source.

## Even NWS's own home territory resists it

**[empirical, cited]** Kuhlmann et al.'s ~10-year evaluation of Germany's local "New Steering Model" reform found **no full paradigm shift** occurred — NWS, in the authors' own assessment, "has not been put into practice," even in a country Bouckaert's framework predicts should be NWS-favourable. **[wiki synthesis]** This is directly useful as a caution against the wiki treating any single reform-trajectory label — NPM or NWS — as a clean predictor even within its own predicted zone.

## A moderator variable this wiki's reform dimensions currently lack

**[wiki synthesis]** If a wiki dimension scores "degree of NPM-style marketisation" or reform intensity as a single cross-national scale (touching the register's D70–D77, D100–D104, D60–D65 reform/review rows), this source's argument is that such a scale category-errors continental European reform: Bouckaert cites scholars *within* Germany who self-identified as "anti-Weberian" reformers operating inside a system Anglo observers would still code as deeply Weberian. Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D133**: an administrative-legal-tradition moderator (Anglo-Saxon/common-law, Napoleonic, Germanic, Nordic/Scandinavian, per Pollitt & Bouckaert's own country groupings) to be checked before any reform-trajectory dimension is compared cross-nationally — a gate, in the same spirit as D90, not a new trait of an institution in its own right.

## Its own stated limit

**[model]** Bouckaert's own concluding assessment: "NWS is only carefully, if at all, travelling outside continental Europe" — only two cases outside Europe are cited (Uruguay, Moroccan healthcare), both tentative. **[wiki synthesis]** Read this source as regionalising the wiki's reform framework, not as installing a second universal ideal type to replace Anglo-centrism with a different overreach — Bouckaert's own advocacy for NWS as normatively superior is contested even within the literature he cites (Byrkjeflot et al. argue for graded "degrees of Weberianism" rather than a discrete typology; Torfing et al. call NWS "a superficial characterization").
