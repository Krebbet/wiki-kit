# Colonial Origins and the Settler Mortality Instrument

The single most-cited empirical claim that institutions cause economic production, and the identification strategy the claim rests on. Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson instrument current national institutional quality — a foreign-investor expropriation-risk score — with the mortality rates European soldiers, sailors and bishops faced in the same territories 100–400 years ago, and report a 2SLS coefficient implying institutions account for **over three-quarters** of cross-country income variance. Their companion paper reuses the same instrument to argue that colonies rich in 1500 are poor today because Europeans imposed extractive institutions where dense populations made extraction profitable. **This page gives the critique equal standing with the claim, because that is what the evidence supports.** Both camps agree the raw correlation between measured institutional quality and income exists. What is contested — unresolved, not settled either way — is whether the settler-mortality instrument identifies *institutions*, and the sharpest attack is not conceptual but archival: Albouy's line-by-line audit finds **36 of AJR's 64 mortality rates are borrowed from other countries or conjectured**, 22 of those traceable to just two thin extrapolations, and reports that with the data problems corrected the first stage loses significance, sometimes reverses sign, and the second-stage confidence interval for the institutions effect becomes the entire real line.

**Evidence tier note.** AJR (2001, 2002) are (a) empirical — cross-country IV on N≈64 ex-colonies, with the causal interpretation resting on an exclusion restriction that is untestable by construction. Albouy is also (a) empirical, of a specific kind: an archival audit of AJR's published data appendix against AJR's own cited primary sources, plus re-estimation of AJR's own specifications under corrected samples and weak-instrument-robust inference. Glaeser et al. is (a) empirical — cross-country OLS/2SLS plus one fixed-effects sequencing panel. Deaton is **(b) a methodological essay with formal derivations** and supplies no data. The asymmetry matters: this is not theory attacking evidence. Two of the three critiques re-run the affirmative camp's own regressions on its own data.

## The claim, and the chain it runs on

**[empirical]** AJR's causal chain, stated in their order: settler mortality → European settlement decisions → colonisation strategy (extractive state vs. "Neo-Europe" transplanting European property-rights institutions) → institutions at independence → current institutions, via persistence → current income.

- **Instrument.** Log potential settler mortality *M*: annualised deaths per 1,000 mean strength among European soldiers, bishops and sailors stationed in the colony, mostly 17th–19th century, primarily from Philip Curtin's historical epidemiology, supplemented by Gutierrez (1986) bishop mortality for Latin America where military records do not exist.
- **First stage.** Settler mortality alone explains ~26% of the variance in current institutions. Each link of the longer chain is separately regressed and each individually holds: settlement share explains >50% of the variance in early institutions; early institutions explain 21% of current institutions.
- **Second stage.** Baseline 2SLS α = **0.95 (s.e. 0.16)** on log GDP per capita (PPP, 1995). The 25th-to-75th-percentile institutions gap implies a 171-log-point income gap against 185 actual — hence "over three-quarters".
- **The IV exceeds the OLS estimate**, which AJR read as attenuation from measurement error in the institutions index dominating any reverse-causality or omitted-variable bias.

**[empirical]** **Institutions, operationally, are one number per country.** The primary variable is *Risk of Expropriation*, 0–10, published by Political Risk Services — a private political-risk consultancy scoring, for foreign investors, the risk that a government will seize a foreign firm's assets. It is not a general measure of domestic rule of law and is not decomposed into any sub-institution. AJR average it over 1985–95. Robustness measures — Polity III constraints on the executive, a PRS law-and-order index, Heritage Foundation property rights, Fraser rule of law, Business International judicial efficiency — are substituted in wholesale with "very similar" results. **None is constructed by AJR;** all are secondhand expert-coded composites treated as interchangeable proxies for one latent construct, without independent validation that they are.

**[empirical]** The exclusion restriction, stated precisely: log *M* must be uncorrelated with the second-stage error. Settler mortality 100–400 years ago must have **no channel to current national income other than through the current expropriation-risk score.** That rules out mortality reaching income via current disease burden, via settler-transplanted "culture", via human-capital selection among who migrated or survived, via any *other* institutional channel (education systems, legal formalism, state capacity), and via geography correlated with both disease and productivity.

### What AJR do to defend it, and what they concede

**[empirical]** The defence is breadth: control sets for climate, latitude, colonizer identity, legal origin, religion, temperature, humidity, current malaria, current European descent, ethnolinguistic fragmentation, soil quality, natural resources, landlocked status. α stays positive and significant across roughly 0.6–1.3. They also run over-identification tests using early institutions and settlement share as additional instruments and fail to reject.

Four things they concede in their own text, which a reader of the abstract would not learn:

1. **The malaria control is admittedly endogenous.** Current malaria prevalence is plausibly a *consequence* of poor institutions, so AJR argue controlling for it *understates* the true effect. That defence only works if their causal story is already right; it does not independently test the exclusion restriction.
2. **The culture confound is named as the threat they cannot rule out.** In their words, early settlers "might have brought a 'culture' conducive to economic progress... Our instrumental variables strategy would then incorrectly assign this effect to institutions." Their only defence is the over-identification test, whose power they concede is limited.
3. **Appendix B admits large-scale imputation.** A substantial share of the mortality series is not measured for the country in question but assigned from a neighbour judged to share a disease environment — Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, Togo, Nigeria, Guinea, Angola, Cameroon, Rwanda, Uganda, Morocco, Pakistan, Surinam and most of Latin America. AJR themselves note that if this measurement error varies systematically it is not classical and could bias the IV in either direction.
4. **Within Africa the first stage collapses.** Reported near the end of §5.2, almost as an aside: restricting to African countries weakens the first stage and renders the 2SLS estimate on expropriation risk insignificant. Most of the identifying power therefore comes from cross-continent variation — Neo-Europes against everyone else.

**[empirical]** They also decline to supply levers, and say so: their results indicate that reducing expropriation risk would raise income "but do not point out what type of concrete steps would lead to an improvement in these institutions." Record that as an explicit non-lever, not a gap to fill speculatively.

## The companion: reversal of fortune

**[empirical]** AJR's second paper establishes a pattern and then attributes it. The pattern: among former European colonies, those relatively rich in 1500 — proxied by urbanisation rates and population density — are relatively poor today, and vice versa. This raw reversal is the robust part. It survives alternative source series (Bairoch, Eggimann, Chandler, civilisation-level aggregation), latitude, temperature, humidity, soil quality, natural resources and landlockedness, and instrumenting 1500 density with density in 1000 to isolate its less error-prone long-run component.

**[empirical]** The attribution: dense pre-colonial populations made extraction profitable (seizable labour, existing tax-and-tribute systems) and settlement unattractive; sparse populations induced settlement and broad-based property rights. Extractive institutions could sustain agricultural output — a narrow elite can coerce land-based production efficiently — but blocked 19th-century industrialisation, which requires investment by entrepreneurs outside the incumbent elite whom the elite has reason to block. The formal test regresses income on both 1500 density and institutions, instrumenting institutions with **settler mortality imported wholesale from the 2001 paper**, and finds the direct effect of 1500 density falls to statistical zero.

**[wiki synthesis]** The two papers are therefore **not two pieces of evidence.** The reversal paper's causal architecture is entirely dependent on the 2001 instrument, which it does not re-derive or re-defend. Anything that breaks the instrument breaks both. AJR's timing argument against "temperate drift" — that the reversal is a 19th-century industrialisation phenomenon, not a 16th–18th-century agricultural-diffusion one — is independent of the instrument and survives it; the institutional attribution does not.

**[empirical]** The reversal paper's weakest robustness check is its own: a "European-control" variable (fraction of Europeans in the non-slave population) constructed ad hoc to test whether Europeans simply did better wherever they went. It rests on a single constructed variable with an untested slave/non-slave power assumption.

**[empirical]** Their own footnote 18 concedes the operational gap: "what matters for economic development is a broad range of institutions, whereas we only have an index for a particular type of institutions, e.g., enforcement of private property rights."

## Contested: does the instrument identify institutions?

This section is co-equal with the two above, not a caveat on them. Three sources attack the identification, and they are of very unequal force. Taken together they establish that the causal claim is **not identified as it stands** — which is a different verdict from "institutions do not matter", and none of the three critics asserts the latter.

### Albouy — the data audit

**[empirical]** Albouy's paper is a line-by-line reconstruction of AJR's published data appendix against AJR's own cited primary sources (Curtin 1989, 1998; Curtin et al. 1995; Gutierrez 1986), followed by re-estimation of AJR's own first- and second-stage specifications. It is the hardest fact on this page. What he finds:

- **Geographic borrowing: 36 of 64.** Only 28 of AJR's 64 countries have a mortality rate originating from data collected inside their own borders. The other **36 are assigned a neighbour's rate** on AJR's judgement of "similar disease environment", with little stated justification. AJR's own text records that neighbours differ enormously — Malaysia 17.7 against Indonesia 170 — which undercuts the premise of the borrowing.
- **22 of those 36 trace to just two extrapolations.** Over a third of the entire sample rests on two thin data points:
  - **The Mali chain.** Three rates from French military campaigns in western Mali (2940, 400 and 280 deaths/1,000/year, all from Curtin 1998) are spread across six countries: Mali itself gets 2940 — an epidemic spike Curtin's own source calls an overestimate; Niger gets 400, itself inflated by coastal-garrison deaths; and Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Gabon, Angola and Uganda all get 280, several of them nowhere near Mali. Albouy traces the spread to AJR mistaking historical colonial place names, and uses 280 as his correction.
  - **The Latin American bishops: 16 countries on 19 deaths.** Gutierrez's bishop-mortality-by-climate-region figures rest on 4, 5 and 10 deaths across three temperature bands with at-risk populations of 24–30.5 bishops each. The rates are not statistically distinguishable from each other, nor from contemporary Swedish or European-barracks mortality (F-test fails to reject equality at 12%). AJR then scale them up by **4.25×**, benchmarked to a single French campaign in 1862–63 Mexico that Albouy shows is miscalculated — not annualised. The correct annualised, region-matched rate lowers the factor from 4.25 to **1.86**.
- **Campaign and barracks rates conflated, and the conflation runs with the hypothesis.** Curtin explicitly separates peacetime "barracks" from wartime "campaign" mortality; campaign rates run 66–2000% higher purely from living conditions, independent of disease environment. AJR mix them without adjustment — and Albouy's test finds campaign rates used significantly more often in countries with *higher* expropriation risk and *lower* GDP (rejected at 2%). The measurement error is correlated with the outcome in the direction that manufactures the result. Concrete instance: Malta gets a peacetime 16.3 while climatically similar Tunisia (63), Egypt (67.8), Algeria and Morocco (78.2) get campaign rates roughly four times higher, though Curtin's own source records Algeria converging to Mediterranean levels within a decade of the fighting ending.
- **Laborer rates use maxima against soldier averages.** Congo is coded at the imported-laborer maximum of 240 rather than the average of 100; Kenya at a maximum of 145 with no average reported at all.
- **Hong Kong: 14.9, from a brief summer campaign near Beijing**, duration-only and not annualised. AJR's own 2005 Response reports the actual Hong Kong peacetime rate as **285** — 19× higher.
- **The stated selection rule is not followed.** For Sudan, Egypt and Madagascar, AJR use rates that are neither the earliest available (their rule in the original paper) nor the earliest peacetime rate (their rule in the 2005 Response). In each case they use the *highest* available rate.

**[empirical]** **What survives when the data is corrected.** Two checks, applied singly and jointly, against AJR's own Table 4 control sets:

| Check | First-stage β (mortality → expropriation risk) |
|---|---|
| Drop the 36 conjectured rates, correct Mali to 280 (n=28) | β **falls** rather than rising — the opposite of what reduced noise should do if the borrowed data were merely noisy-but-unbiased — and is significant only at the **18% level** across all control specifications |
| Add campaign-source and laborer-source dummies (n=64) | β falls and is **insignificant at 5% in every specification with controls** |
| Both together (n=28, dummies) | β **insignificant even with no controls at all**, and **switches sign** — negative to positive — in three of eight control specifications |

**[empirical]** **The second-stage consequence.** With β not reliably different from zero the instrument is weak, so conventional Wald confidence intervals for α are, in Dufour's (1997) sense, "grossly incorrect". Using the weak-instrument-robust Anderson–Rubin statistic instead, the confidence regions for the effect of institutions on income become **unbounded — the entire real line, (−∞, +∞)** — in most specifications. The data cannot rule out any value, zero and negative included.

**[empirical]** **AJR's own "results hold without Africa" defence, examined.** Excluding Africa leaves 37 rates, of which only 13 are not conjectured. Within Africa alone β is insignificant (p = 0.57). Excluding both Africa and the conjectured rates leaves **13 countries**, and the surviving relationship there is driven entirely by the Neo-Europes — Canada, New Zealand, the United States — where, Albouy argues, Europeans imported a whole transplanted civilisation rather than altering an indigenous institutional trajectory, which violates AJR's own identifying assumption that settlement reached income only through property-rights institutions. New data AJR supplied in their 2005 Response does not restore significance once the two checks are applied (β p-values 0.11–0.93).

**[empirical]** **What Albouy does not claim, in his own words.** "It is regrettable that AJR's mortality series suffers from severe measurement issues... Much of the mortality variation is due to AJR's questionable assignments, which often reflect transitory fluctuations or living conditions of the populations observed rather than actual permanent differences among these countries... Given the limited data sources currently available, it seems unlikely that a convincing set of settler mortality rates can be constructed. As such, cross-country growth regressions cannot disentangle the effect of settler mortality from that of other variables which may explain institutions and growth, such as geography, climate, culture, and pre-existing development. This leaves AJR's theoretical hypotheses without a strong empirical foundation."

He credits that West Africa and the Caribbean genuinely were dangerous for Europeans; his target is the cross-country comparability and construction of the series. He does not argue that institutions do not matter, and he frames the campaign/barracks pattern as "artificial support", not fraud. He thanks Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson by name for sharing data and their responses. **Steelmanned in reverse:** his own corrected 28-country sample is still built from the same thin colonial sources, is smaller, and is likely still noisy — a limitation he does not fully own.

### Glaeser, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer — the measurement attack and the horse race

**[empirical]** Glaeser et al. attack a different joint. Their claim is that the standard indices do not measure what the literature says they measure, and that when human capital and institutions are raced head to head off the same instruments, human capital wins.

**The measurement objection, precisely.** They separate two things the literature conflates:

- **Durable-rule / constraint measures**, matching North's own definition (rules and compliance procedures that constrain, and that must be "reasonably permanent or durable" to count as institutions at all): electoral-rule type (plurality vs. proportional representation), judicial independence (supreme-court tenure security), and constitutional review (scope of judicial review of legislation).
- **Outcome / perception measures**, which are what the growth literature actually regresses on: ICRG expropriation risk and related investor-risk indicators (AJR's variable), Kaufmann–Kraay–Mastruzzi government effectiveness (Rodrik's), and Polity IV constraints on the executive.

**[empirical]** Four findings separate the two families. (i) The outcome measures rise sharply with income; judicial independence and constitutional review are essentially **uncorrelated with per-capita income at all**. (ii) The outcome measures are highly volatile — Polity variables' within-country standard deviation (~18–19%) is roughly **twice** that of years of schooling (~10%) — and Table 2's persistence regressions show "stunning" mean reversion, while human capital is far more persistent 1960→2000. (iii) The outcome measures are close to uncorrelated with the constitutional-rule measures. (iv) They cannot distinguish a ruler who *cannot* expropriate from one who currently *chooses not to*: the USSR and Singapore rank among the ten lowest-expropriation-risk countries in 1984; Iran's score moves 1→9, Libya's 1.5→9 and Syria's 1.5→9 between 1982 and 1997 with no change in political constraint, tracking the Washington-Consensus policy shift. Polity's constraints-on-the-executive, though designed to measure constraint, tracks the most recent election result — Haiti 1 (1960–89) → 6 (Aristide, 1990) → 1 (ousted, 1991–93) → 6–7 (1994–98) → 3 (2000–01); Argentina swings between worst and best with the electoral cycle even when elected leaders are undermining courts and legislature. Pinochet's Chile scores the worst possible constraint score of 1 with relatively secure property.

**[wiki synthesis]** The authors propose the **volatility/mean-reversion of a proxy as a diagnostic** for whether it measures a durable institution or a policy outcome. That is a methodological contribution, and it is registered as an axis at [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D90.

**[empirical]** **Their attack on AJR's instruments specifically** (their Section V), stacked:

1. What settlers "brought" when they stayed could be human capital and know-how rather than, or as well as, constraining institutions.
2. The instruments are close to **uncorrelated with the objective constitutional measures** — surprising if what was transplanted was durable institutions rather than transferable rules and procedures, which colonial history is otherwise known to have shaped strongly.
3. **A modern-disease confound.** Log settler mortality correlates **.67 with modern malaria risk** and **.66 with malaria ecology**. Re-running AJR's own joint robustness regression on the full available sample rather than AJR's subset, **both** settler mortality and malaria come out significant — contra AJR's published result that only mortality survives.
4. **The instruments correlate more strongly with human capital than with institutions.** Log settler mortality correlates **−.73** with years of schooling in 2000; a small 1900 primary-enrolment sample both predicts income today and correlates strongly with the AJR instruments.
5. **The 2SLS horse race (their Table 11).** Using French legal origin, log settler mortality and log 1500 population density as instruments (the latter two one at a time) for *both* average schooling and average executive constraints 1960–2000, with log GDP per capita 2000 as the outcome: **predicted schooling is a significant second-stage predictor; predicted executive constraints is not.**

Their stated conclusion is narrow and should be kept narrow: settler mortality and 1500 population density are **not valid instruments for institutions specifically**. They concede colonisers brought "guns, germs, and steel" too, so the exercise does not establish human capital as *the* answer either.

**[empirical]** **Their positive claim, and the one design that tests it.** Reversing the arrow, they endorse Lipset: education reduces political violence and enables negotiated dispute resolution — "courts and legislatures replace guns" — so growth and human capital cause institutional improvement rather than the reverse. Their strongest single test is a **fixed-effects panel sequencing regression at 5-year intervals (Table 12): initial schooling predicts subsequent 5-year improvement in institutional outcomes on 3 of 4 measures, including executive constraints, while initial institutions predict no subsequent improvement in schooling**, and initial income predicts no institutional change either. Supporting but weaker: initial schooling predicts growth in every decade 1960–2000 while initial executive constraints predict nothing outside the 1980s; the least-educated countries spend 66% of sample-years under autocrats who last five years or more against ~13% under stable democratic leaders; and the North/South Korea pair, which they present as explicitly illustrative (n=1) — near-identical starting conditions, similarly mediocre executive-constraint scores (South 2.16, North 1.71 out of 7), with the South's dictators choosing capitalism and democratisation arriving only after 1980, *following* growth.

**Steelmanned in reverse:** the authors repeatedly hedge — "we do not wish to push these results too far", "this evidence is at most suggestive" — while the conclusion (good-for-growth dictators can substitute for constrained government, with Deng-vs-Mao cited approvingly) reads as more confident and more politically loaded than the hedged statistics license. Three of the four authors are the core of the LLSV legal-origins programme and the paper leans heavily on their own prior work. They explicitly deny arguing "institutions don't matter" and explicitly deny making a normative case for dictatorship, citing evidence that democracies are more benign on press freedom, torture, the death penalty and religious regulation.

### Deaton — the general framework, and what he does not supply

**[model]** Deaton's Keynes Lecture supplies the *general* identification framework this dispute needs, and nothing AJR-specific.

His central distinction, attributed to Heckman and tracing to Wright and Frisch: **"external" (not caused by the outcome or other model variables) does not imply "exogenous" (orthogonal to the error term in the equation of interest).** His actual sentence on AJR: "So are historical variables — the mortality of colonial settlers is not influenced by current institutional arrangements in ex-colonial countries, Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson (2001), nor does the country's growth rate today influence which country they were colonized by, Barro 1998." That establishes only that settler mortality is *external*. He follows immediately with the principle: "Whether any of these instruments is *exogenous* depends on the nature of the equation of interest, and is not guaranteed by its *externality*."

His second contribution is **LATE vs. ATE**: with heterogeneous treatment effects, "the probability limit of the IV estimator will in general depend on the choice of instrument" — different valid instruments for the same nominal question give different answers, because IV recovers the average effect only among compliers. Applied by extension, AJR's α would be the average effect of institutions on income only among the ex-colonies whose institutional trajectory was actually shifted by settler mortality, not across all ex-colonies. Deaton on the estimand: "I find it hard to make any sense of the LATE... heterogeneity is not a technical problem calling for an econometric solution, but is a reflection of the fact that we have not started on our proper business, which is trying to understand what is going on."

Third: **exogeneity cannot be tested.** "It is an identifying assumption that must be made prior to analysis of the data, [so] no empirical tests are possible." Over-identification tests are routinely misread as validating identification, when passing is consistent with *all* instruments being invalid and failing is consistent with only a subset being so. That bears directly on AJR's over-identification defence above and on Rodrik's failed test at [[institutions-vs-geography-vs-trade-horse-race]].

> **Correction to this wiki's own commissioning brief.** This job was briefed on the understanding that Deaton's lecture uses AJR as its worked example. **It does not.** Deaton names AJR **once**, in a list alongside five other external-but-not-necessarily-exogenous instruments (distance from the equator for religiosity, rivers for school districts, land gradient for dam construction, month-of-birth for schooling, rainfall for civil war). He never spells out a competing channel from settler mortality to current income — malaria, yellow fever, disease burden and ecology-driven productivity do not appear in the source at all, confirmed by full-text search — and he never engages AJR's expropriation-risk measure ("expropriation" does not appear). His worked demonstrations are elsewhere: the aid-growth instruments (population size, the "Egypt" dummy), where he concludes exogeneity "makes no sense at all", and Angrist–Lavy's Maimonides-rule class-size instrument, contaminated by parental sorting per Urquiola & Verhoogen (2008). **For AJR specifically his objection is asserted by genre membership, not demonstrated.** Albouy and Glaeser carry the technical critique of this literature; Deaton supplies the framework in which their findings are read. Do not cite Deaton as though he audited AJR.

**[model]** Deaton's own constructive position is worth recording because it is weaker than nihilism and stronger than acquiescence: he explicitly declines the option that "there are no valid instruments, and that the aid to growth question is not answerable in this way", and instead proposes abandoning the IV point estimate as the object of interest in favour of examining the **reduced form** — outcome regressed directly on the instrument — as substantive evidence open to interpretation and argument with historians. On instrument choice: "we have control over the light, but choose to let it fall where it may, and then proclaim that whatever it illuminates is what we were looking for all along."

## Where this leaves the claim

**[wiki synthesis]** Four things are true at once, and collapsing any of them into the others is how this literature gets miscited.

1. **The raw correlation is not in dispute.** Measured institutional-quality indices correlate strongly with national income. Glaeser et al. do not deny it — they explain it in the other direction and dispute what the indices measure. Albouy does not touch it.
2. **The mechanism — constraint → investment → growth — is theorised, not measured, by anyone here.** AJR call institutions a "black box" in their own text and inherit the investment channel by citation from North & Thomas, North and Jones. See [[institutions-as-fundamental-cause]].
3. **Whether the instrument identifies institutions is contested and unresolved.** It has not been shown to; it has also not been shown that a better-constructed instrument would fail.
4. **Two specific propositions have evidence running against them.** That corrected settler-mortality data still supports AJR's institutions coefficient — Albouy shows it does not, on AJR's own specifications. And that the standard indices measure durable constraint rather than recent policy outcome — Glaeser et al. show they do not, on volatility, mean reversion, and their near-zero correlation with constitutional-rule measures.

**[wiki synthesis]** **Settlement criterion, recorded so a later source can be scored against it.** Two things would move this, and only together:

- **An independently reconstructed settler-mortality series with transparent provenance** — every rate sourced to data collected inside the country it is assigned to, campaign and barracks rates separated and reported, laborer and soldier populations not mixed on different statistics, and the imputation rate disclosed per observation. Albouy's own verdict is that the surviving colonial records may not support one; if so, the honest conclusion is that this instrument is unavailable rather than that it works.
- **A timing/sequencing design**, which is what actually discriminates institutions-cause-growth from growth-causes-institutions and does not depend on any instrument. **Only Glaeser et al.'s Table 12 attempts one anywhere in this batch**, and it runs against the affirmative camp. A symmetric design run by the affirmative camp on the affirmative camp's variables does not exist here.

## Level of analysis: what this page cannot be cited for

**[wiki synthesis]** **"Institutions" here is one scalar per country, everywhere, in every source on this page.** A country with one first-rate central bank and one captured customs agency gets a single number — often, per Albouy, a number collected in a different country. There is no sub-national or institution-level variation anywhere in the design, and no source in this batch attempts a decomposition.

Consequence, stated flatly: **nothing on this page supports a causal claim about an individual institution's effect on economic production.** It supports claims about national institutional environments, contested even there. AJR themselves flag the problem from the other side — they suggest expropriation risk is "an equilibrium outcome" of more fundamental institutional choices (presidential vs. parliamentary, common vs. civil law), i.e. that their own variable is downstream of the finer-grained dimensions this wiki's framework is trying to build. See [[open-questions]] Q90.
