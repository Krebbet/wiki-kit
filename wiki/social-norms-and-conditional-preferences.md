# Social Norms and Conditional Preferences

Bicchieri supplies the micro-mechanism this job otherwise lacks: not how informal rules interact with formal ones ([[informal-institutions-typology]]) and not case studies of specific enforcement regimes ([[maghribi-traders-reputation-mechanism]], [[diamond-industry-private-ordering]]), but a precise account of **what makes a rule a social norm at all** — why it binds beyond ordinary self-interested coordination. A behavioural rule R is a social norm for population P when a sufficiently large subset of P, for situations of type S: (1) knows R exists and applies; (2) prefers to conform *conditional on* believing enough others conform (**empirical expectations**) *and* believing enough others expect — and may sanction — their conformity (**normative expectations**).

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** — a formal "rational reconstruction," explicitly not a claim about how people actually deliberate (conformity can be automatic/heuristic, not just calculated). The chapter cites experimental literature (Fehr & Gächter 2000 on cooperation decay after observed defection; Dawes 1974 on Oregon landlords' mismatched empirical/normative expectations) to motivate the theory, not to test it directly.

## Convention, social norm, moral norm — three different binding mechanisms

**[model]** A **convention** requires only empirical expectations, in a pure coordination game with no conflict of interest — "a convention is always a *followed* descriptive norm," and conforming is never against self-interest. A **social norm** requires *both* expectation types, and works by **transforming** an underlying mixed-motive game (a prisoner's dilemma, a trust game) into a coordination game: "a social norm, unlike a convention, is never a solution of an original coordination game, though it is an equilibrium of the new, transformed game it creates." A **moral norm** demands, in principle, unconditional commitment — though Bicchieri notes that if it turns out to be conditional after all, it collapses into a social norm. A **descriptive norm** (fashion, fad) requires only empirical expectations and no normative ones — conformity is "always dictated by self-interest" (information, fitting in), never obligation.

## A convention can become a social norm — and the reverse

**[model]** Traffic rules, footbinding, and table manners are cited as cases where a pure coordination convention acquired a normative-expectation layer over time (via accruing negative externalities or signalling value) and became a true social norm — meaning the empirical/normative distinction is not fixed per practice but can shift.

## Registered as a new dimension

**[wiki synthesis]** This yields a genuinely different, testable classification from [[informal-institutions-typology]]'s formal/informal interaction typology: for any given informal constraint, is its stability held up by empirical expectations alone (a convention — shift it by changing what people observe others doing, no normative work required) or by normative expectations too (a true social norm — requires shifting beliefs about what others think *ought* to happen, a harder and slower lever)? Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D136**, and read together with [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D2: conventions should be cheap and fast to shift; social norms should be exactly the slow-moving case Roland's paper ([[fast-and-slow-moving-institutions]]) describes but does not explain the mechanism of — Bicchieri's normative-expectation requirement is a candidate mechanism Roland's own paper explicitly does not supply.
