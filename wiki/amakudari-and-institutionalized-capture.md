# Amakudari and Institutionalized Capture

Colignon & Usui document *amakudari* ("descent from heaven") as a **ministry-organised**, not individually-motivated, personnel pipeline running through four distinct paths — direct movement to private boards (*amakudari* proper), movement to public corporations (*yokosuberi*), movement into political office (*chii riyo*), and serial multi-step reemployment used specifically to evade a legal cooling-off restriction (*wataridori*) — and argue prior studies underestimated the system's scope by measuring only one path in isolation. Original data: 873 ex-bureaucrats held 2.0% of board seats across 2,220 listed Japanese firms in 1994, with three ministries (Finance, Construction, MITI) supplying 58% of them.

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]** — multiple original datasets (board-seat directories, public-corporation placement surveys 1967–98, Diet-member career records back to 1945), not just synthesis. The authors' interpretive "locational power"/*go*-vs-chess framing is **[model]**.

## An organised pipeline, not an individual conflict of interest

**[empirical]** The four-path typology distinguishes: **amakudari proper** (direct private-board placement); **yokosuberi** ("sideslip" to public corporations — *tokushu hojin* — the indirect-influence channel); **chii riyo** ("position exploitation," movement into political office — 9 of 15 postwar PMs before 1980 were ex-bureaucrats, versus 1 since); and **wataridori** ("migratory bird," serial reemployment specifically routed to evade the 1962 National Personnel Act's two-year direct-transfer restriction — 32% of the 1994 board placements arrived via a prior intermediate post). **[wiki synthesis]** *Wataridori* is the sharpest data point for this wiki: it is a documented institutional workaround to a Western-style anti-capture rule (a cooling-off period), showing how such rules can be routed around by adding an intermediate placement rather than removed.

## "Surrounding," not "capturing"

**[model]** The authors' own framing, offered as an explicit challenge to Western capture theory: Stigler-style capture models expect direct confrontation and displacement (chess); *amakudari*'s effect, they argue, works by placing enough ex-bureaucrats across enough boards that a firm's available choices are pre-shaped without any overt conflict (*go* — "surrounding the opponent so that opponent's moves or choices are increasingly limited"). **[wiki synthesis]** This is a genuinely distinct capture *channel* from what [[regulatory-capture]]'s existing D64 measures (coordination cost among outside interests) — D64 asks how many actors must coordinate to shift a policy; this describes a standing personnel-presence mechanism that requires no coordination event at all, because the influence is already resident inside the regulated firm.

## Registered as a new channel-of-capture axis

**[wiki synthesis]** Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D132**: institutionalised personnel-pipeline capture, measured by tracing an institution's (or a regulator's) alumni placements across its client industry's boards, public-adjacent corporations, and political posts, and testing whether placement is concentrated/patterned (ministry-organised) versus diffuse (individual career choice). Distinct from D64's coordination-cost question and complementary to it.

## Structural, not marginal — but genuinely contested

**[empirical]** Schaede's data (100 largest firms, 1979–91) shows *amakudari* board share rising from 2.13% to 4.91–5.3%, with over half of 1991 placements arriving via an intermediate public-corporation stop by then. Official National Personnel Authority statistics show an apparent decline since the mid-1980s that the authors argue is a measurement artefact — the NPA only reports "conflict of interest" cases, capturing (per a cited estimate) under 10% of actual high-ranking placements. **Not settled**: the paper itself surveys a live dispute — Calder, Nakano and Richardson argue for minimal/no economic effect from the same phenomenon. Cite as contested mechanism strength, not settled fact.
