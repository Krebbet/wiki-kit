# Veto Players and Policy Stability

Tsebelis's *Veto Players* generalises a single mechanism — the number, ideological distance and internal cohesion of the actors whose agreement is required to change a status quo — into the wiki's most heavily empirically corroborated account of institutional accountability structure. More veto players, greater distance between them, and greater cohesion shrink the **winset of the status quo** (the set of alternatives that could replace it), producing **policy stability**: the impossibility of large departures from the status quo. The relationship is one-directional by construction — many/distant/cohesive veto players *guarantee* stability, few veto players only *may* produce instability — and the book traces policy stability downstream into government instability (parliamentary systems), regime instability (presidential systems), and, most relevant here, the **discretionary space available to bureaucrats and judges**: a large "core" that legislation cannot reach is exactly the space a first-moving bureaucrat or judge can occupy unchallenged.

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** for the formal apparatus (winset geometry, the core, the absorption rule), **[empirical]** for an unusually large, independently-authored corroboration program spanning ~15 years and multiple datasets — Doering (labour legislation, 15–16 West European countries), Franzese (budget deficits, 21 countries/35 years), Henisz (growth, 157 countries), Hallerberg & Basinger (OECD tax reform), Keefer & Stasavage (central-bank independence × veto players, 78 countries), and the author's own judicial-independence regressions (~18–24 countries). This is the register's first veto-point source with real cross-national testing behind it — [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]]'s anchor source, Hammond, is a theorem with no data at all.

## The composite claim, now with data behind it

**[empirical]** [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D44** already registers veto-point count × preference-profile divergence as one composite axis, from Hammond's proof that count alone predicts nothing. Tsebelis's program is direct empirical corroboration of the same composite claim, independently derived and independently tested: Hallerberg & Basinger find moving from one to two-plus veto players cuts OECD corporate-tax-rate change by 18.4 points and top-marginal-income-tax change by 20.3 points; Keefer & Stasavage find central-bank independence lowers inflation only when *interacted* with veto-player count (near zero under single-party majority, ~20% reduction under three-party coalitions) — precisely the interaction-not-count-alone structure D44 was built to require; Henisz finds a one-SD change in a five-veto-player political-constraints index moves growth by 17–31% of an SD across 157 countries.

## Two genuinely new results

**[model]** **The qualified-majority inversion.** Under simple-majority decision rules, policy stability rises with veto-player cohesion (agreement among distant, cohesive players locks in the status quo). Under **qualified-majority** rules, this reverses: stability *falls* with cohesion (Conjectures 2.1/2.3, Proposition 2.4). No prior source in this wiki states a condition under which the direction of the count/cohesion relationship itself flips — worth noting alongside D44 as a scope condition on when the axis's sign is even well-defined.

**[model]** **The absorption rule.** "A veto player located in the unanimity core of others does not alter policy decision-making" — a formal statement that a body can hold a nominal veto and be functionally irrelevant, because its position is already contained within the space the other veto players would allow. This is a precise, general formalisation of nominal-vs-effective power that generalises well beyond legislatures — a board seat, a sign-off gate, or a consultative body can be a veto player on paper and an absorbed non-actor in practice. Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D127**.

## Institutional vs. partisan veto players

**[model]** Tsebelis splits veto players into **institutional** (created by the constitution — a president, a chamber) and **partisan** (created by the party system inside an institutional actor — a coalition partner). The distinction matters for accountability mapping: a coalition partner is functionally a veto player with no separate constitutional standing, so counting only formal institutional actors undercounts real veto structure wherever party systems fragment power inside a nominally unified branch.

## Bureaucratic and judicial independence as a downstream measurement

**[empirical]** The author's own ordered-probit regressions (~18–24 countries, Table 10.3) find veto-player count a significant predictor of multiple judicial-independence indices; central-bank independence work (Table 10.4–10.5) finds the same pattern interacting with federalism. **[wiki synthesis]** This is the mechanism [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]] states abstractly (a narrow core leaves more room for a first-moving agent) given a real cross-national test — though note the same caution that page already carries: this measures *institutional* independence (what the rules allow), not necessarily *behavioural* independence (what the court or bureau actually does with the room), a distinction Tsebelis names but does not fully resolve either.
