# Dimensions of Institutional Variation

A standing register of every axis of institutional variation named by the sources this wiki has ingested, with — for each — who named it, how it would be measured on a real institution, and its status. This is the seed of the wiki's general analytical framework (project outcome 2); it is meant to be extended by every subsequent ingest, not rewritten. Nothing here is a finding: the register records what the literature *proposes* as an axis and what it would take to put a number on it. Entries currently come from four foundational New Institutional Economics sources (North ×2, Ostrom, Williamson) plus Libecap's NBER retrospective — a literature that is theory and comparative history, not empirical identification, with the single exception of Ostrom's experimental and meta-analytic work. D35–D41 were added by the `scale-effects` ingest (2026-08-19) from Coase, Niskanen, Parkinson/Klimek, Simon, Rajan & Wulf and Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg; they are the first axes in the register that describe the **inside of a single organisation**, which the note at the end of the register previously recorded as wholly missing. D42–D48 were added by the `risk-aversion-in-large-institutions` ingest (2026-08-19) from Bozeman, Hammond, Hood, Kahneman & Lovallo, Holmström and Manso; they describe the **control and incentive environment** an organisation and its agents sit inside. Four axes that batch also named were **not** given rows, because they are instances of axes already here — personal payoff asymmetry under D8, rule rigidity under D1, discretion-limiting rules under D9 — and the notes are recorded in those rows instead. D54–D59 were added by the `institutional-evolution` ingest (2026-08-19) from Arthur, North's Nobel lecture, Pierson, DiMaggio & Powell, Meyer & Rowan, Thelen and Krasner; they describe **how an institution changes, what its structure tracks, and the field it sits in**, and one of the six (D58) is `rejected`. That batch also declined to re-register North's adaptive-vs-allocative efficiency axis, which duplicates the already-rejected D34 and arrives with no operationalisation the second time either — the note is recorded at D34 instead. D25a and D49–D53 were added by the `incentives-and-institutional-form` ingest (2026-08-19) from Holmstrom, Holmstrom & Milgrom, Hart, Dixit, Frey and Bénabou & Tirole; they describe the **contracting parameters** that determine how strong an incentive an institution can rationally attach to anything, and **two of those six rows are `rejected`** — recorded rather than dropped, per lint check 11. D70–D77 were added by the `enabling-institutional-change` ingest (2026-08-19) from Finan/Olken/Pande, NAO, Bunse & Fritz, True/Jones/Baumgartner, O'Reilly & Tushman and Andrews/Pritchett/Woolcock; they describe **what an institution's reform attempt is made of and whether anyone can tell whether it worked**, and one of the eight (D77) is `rejected`. That batch is the first in the register whose rows differ sharply in evidence tier from each other — D70 and D71 come from randomised field experiments, D72 from a near-census audit, and D73–D76 from case narrative — so read the Named-by column before using any of them. D60–D65 were added by the `institutional-stagnation` ingest (2026-08-19) from Miller & Dudley, Schleicher & Bagley and Carpenter & Moss; they describe **review architecture, procedural load and the conditions for capture**, and one of the six (D65) is `rejected` because it scores the literature's rigour rather than an institution. That batch was commissioned to test whether institutions decay with age and **returned no age axis at all** — every duration variable in its nine sources attaches to a political regime, a legal corpus or a national polity, never to a single institution's own tenure. The gap note below records that as the fourth consecutive batch to come back empty on organisational age. **D90–D94 were added by the `institutions-and-growth` ingest (2026-08-20)** from Glaeser/La Porta/Lopez-de-Silanes/Shleifer, Wade and Besley & Persson; they cover **measurement validity and the terms on which an institution's support is given**, and the batch is the register's worst yield per source — nine sources, four rows, one `rejected` and one `candidate`. The reason is stated in the block note and in the gap note: every source in that batch measures "institutions" as a single national scalar and none decomposes below the nation-state, so **D91 is its one clear institution-level axis**. **D100–D104 were added by the `bureaucracy-and-public-choice` ingest (2026-08-20)** from Guo Xu, Rauch, Wilson and Besley/Burgess/Khan/Xu; they cover **personnel protection beyond the recruitment gate, what a selection rule actually selects for, and how to tell a reform's content from the fact of it**, and one of the five (D103) is `rejected` as subsumed by D25/D50/D51. That batch also made a **provenance correction to D50** rather than adding a row: the wiki now holds a Wilson primary text (1967) and it does **not** contain the action × outcome typology, which is from his 1989 book and is held here second-hand via Dixit alone — the row stands, its attribution is fixed, and the gap is recorded. **D92 was reserved and deliberately left unused** — the axis it was reserved for (breadth of the power-holding group) was already registered at D7, and the new naming sources are recorded there. **D120–D123 were added by the `measuring-institutions` ingest (2026-08-21)** from Kaufmann & Kraay, Pemstein et al., Munck & Verkuilen, Arndt & Oman, the WWBI codebook and methodology report, and the QoG codebook; they cover **how a measurement instrument is built and audited**, and **two of the four are `rejected` — which is the block's purpose rather than its shortfall.** D122 (V-Dem/WGI/QoG country scores) and D123 (WWBI sector/occupation aggregates) record that the field's flagship instruments are inadmissible at institution level and state exactly where each one stops, so the question is not re-opened every batch. The two `candidate` rows are not derived from any score in the batch: D120 turns Munck & Verkuilen's construction audit on an institution's own KPI system, and D121 turns Arndt & Oman's gaming warning into a two-step test on an institution's funding documents. **D115–D119 are unused**, reserved by the previous batch's numbering and not filled. **D124–D126 were added by the `organisational-ecology` ingest (2026-08-21)** from Hannan & Freeman (1977, 1984), Yang & Aldrich and Coad et al.; they are **the register's first age-of-institution rows**, after four consecutive prior batches (scale-effects, institutional-evolution, institutional-stagnation, bureaucracy-and-public-choice) returned no institutional-age measurement at all — see the note at the end of the `Scale, time and duration` section for what these rows do and do not settle. D124 is flagged explicitly as a population-level statistic (a hazard rate needs a cohort), unlike D110–D114 and D120–D121's per-institution document scores; D125 is the batch's one row scoreable on a single institution's own records tomorrow; D126 is a named, testable mechanism with no data run against it yet. **D127–D130 were added by the `power-and-accountability` ingest (2026-08-21)** from Tsebelis, La Porta/Lopez-de-Silanes/Shleifer, Gilens & Page and Bueno de Mesquita/Morrow/Siverson/Smith; they cover **who actually holds effective control and whose preferences actually move an institution's outcomes**, the power leg of the power/institutions/economy triad. D7 and D44 were extended rather than duplicated (Acemoglu & Robinson's formal captured-democracy derivation onto D7; Tsebelis's empirical corroboration and qualified-majority inversion onto D44). D128 is scoreable tomorrow from a firm's own filings, in the D110 mould; D129 and D130 are flagged as regime/national-level as tested, with extension to individual institutions marked explicitly as this wiki's own untested inference. ****D135–D137 were added by the `informal-institutions` ingest (2026-08-21)** from Helmke & Levitsky, Bicchieri, and Greif; they cover **how informal rules interact with formal ones, what makes a rule bind at all, and a structural precondition for reputation-based enforcement**. D2, D5 and D32 were extended rather than duplicated: Roland sharpens D2's criterion without closing its mechanism gap (and says so himself); Ostrom, Walker & Gardner give D5 a controlled self-chosen-vs-imposed comparison; Glaeser et al. give D32 a validated behavioural benchmark that sharpens, rather than closes, its "theory outruns the measure" problem. **D138 was added by the `schooling-norms-and-institutional-formation` ingest (2026-08-21)** from Nunn & Wantchekon — a mover/non-mover method for separating internal (cultural) from external (institutional) persistence of a historical shock, flagged with the authors' own stated limit (it proxies, not tests, the cultural/institutional distinction). This batch's larger contribution is not to the register but to [[schooling-and-institutional-quality]], substantially rewritten with two direct rebuttals, a reverse-causality alternative, a sub-national design and a content-dependency complication to the mechanism — see that page and [[open-questions]] Q94. **D134 was added by the `institution-case-profiles` ingest (2026-08-21)** — the wiki's first case-study job, scoring the FDA and Meta (plus Meta's Oversight Board) against this register from their own primary documents rather than from literature. D134 (leadership-vacancy share) is the register's first row with no literature naming source at all — surfaced directly by reading FDA's own org chart. Three existing rows were **promoted** on the strength of a real-institution run: D128 (ultimate ownership/control-type — Zuckerberg's 60.8% voting power against a 13–14% economic stake, a clean, current, quantified control/cash-flow wedge) and D112 (formal/real authority gap — Meta's Oversight Board holds textbook binding authority qualified at four distinct points by Meta's own continuing judgment, a genuinely mixed rather than one-sided case). D127 (absorption) and D44/D120/D35 all got real-institution confirmations recorded on their own rows rather than promoted, since they were `promoted` or in D35's case already well-attested. D131–D133 were added by the `comparative-governing-philosophies` ingest (2026-08-21)** from Xu, Colignon & Usui, and Bouckaert; this batch's purpose was to test whether the register's existing axes are universal or Anglosphere artefacts. The verdict is mixed rather than clean: the M-form/U-form decision-rights vocabulary travels (Xu applies it to a nation-state directly), but three genuinely new mechanisms surfaced that no prior batch had named — a personnel-control tournament that substitutes for market/electoral accountability (D131), an institutionalised (not individual) personnel-pipeline capture channel (D132), and — the sharpest universality finding — a legal-tradition moderator (D133) without which the register's own reform-trajectory rows (D60–D65, D70–D77, D100–D104) should not be compared cross-nationally at all.

## How to read this

**Status values.** `candidate` — named by a source and operationalisable, but not yet doing predictive work in this wiki. `promoted` — meets the promotion criterion below. `rejected` — cannot currently be measured as stated, with the reason recorded; the row stays so the problem is visible rather than silently dropped. `superseded by Dnn` — the row was found to be the same construct as an existing row and has been merged into it; the row is never deleted, it points at the survivor, and the survivor is the one to score.

**Promotion criterion [wiki synthesis]. Revised 2026-08-20 by the user's ruling.** An axis is promoted when a measurement procedure can be stated that could be run against an institution's primary documents, budget, personnel records, or observable behaviour, without needing access the analyst would not plausibly have. **A single naming source is sufficient.**

*What changed and why.* The criterion previously also required that at least two independent sources name the axis — "both conditions, not either". It was dropped because a two-source rule privileges well-trodden ground over well-operationalised ground, which is backwards for a framework meant to be *applied* to institutions rather than to summarise a literature. An axis nobody else has named but which yields a number from an institution's own records is more useful here than an axis three authors invoke and none can measure. Measurability is now the whole test.

*Consequence.* Promotion no longer carries any claim about how well-attested an axis is — only that it can be measured. Weight of naming is still recorded in the Named-by column; read it there, and do not read `promoted` as consensus.

*History.* Seven rows (D3, D4, D10, D30, D70, D71, D72) were promoted on a single source under the old rule, by two batches that independently read the "and" as an "or". The 2026-08-19 lint flagged them as breaches. Under the revised criterion they are compliant as written, and were not demoted.

**Admissibility [lint check 11].** A dimension with no operationalisation is not admissible as part of the framework. Where none can be stated, the row says so explicitly and is marked `rejected`, with a note on what restatement would readmit it.

**Evidence tier.** Every row is (b) a theoretical proposal, except where the Named-by column marks Ostrom's experimental/meta-analytic findings or Libecap's own case work, which are (a) empirical. Among the scale-effects additions, only Klimek/Hanel/Thurner's headcount data and Rajan & Wulf's firm panel are (a) empirical; Coase, Niskanen, Simon and Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg are (b) models their own authors describe as untested. Among the risk-aversion additions the tier distinction is the whole point and is unusually stark: **not one of D42–D48 has been measured against a behavioural outcome by the source that named it.** Bozeman's propositions are explicitly "potentially amenable to test" and untested; Hammond's composite is a theorem whose result is that the count alone predicts nothing; Hood's monitoring-design axis comes from an argumentative chapter; Kahneman & Lovallo's cadence result is derived inside a toy model; Holmström's and Manso's axes are parameters of formal models with no empirical content at all. Treat all seven as (b), and see [[risk-aversion-in-large-institutions]] for why that matters here more than elsewhere in the register. The operationalisation column is (c) wiki synthesis throughout — the sources rarely state how to measure what they name, and where they do it is noted.

### Rules and enforcement

| # | Dimension | Named by | Operationalisation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | **Formalisation** — formal written rules vs. informal constraints (norms, conventions, self-imposed codes) | North (JEP; framework); Ostrom (rules-in-use vs. formal organisation — 25% of coded fishery subgroups had no formal organisation at all) | Take a sample of consequential decisions; for each, code whether the binding constraint is documented and citable or unwritten practice. Report the ratio. **Rule rigidity, named by the OECD (Brazil's "heavy focus on rules, procedures and a rigid constitution") and by Bozeman, is registered here rather than as a new row** — Bozeman's whole point is that formalisation is a neutral trait and only its inefficacious subset is red tape (D42), so rigidity is this axis plus an efficacy judgement, not a separate axis. | promoted |
| D2 | **Formal/informal change-speed differential** | North (framework): "formal rules may change over night, but informal constraints do not" | Elapsed time between a formal rule change and observed change in the corresponding behaviour. Requires a before/after behavioural measure, so only applicable where behaviour is observable. **Extended [`informal-institutions`, 2026-08-21]**: Roland independently names the same distinction with a sharper, three-tier operationalisation — political institutions changeable "nearly overnight," legal systems intermediate, social norms **"which can never change by fiat"** — and supplies an interaction mechanism (continuous slow-moving pressure builds inconsistencies with fast-moving institutions until the latter snap, via an explicit earthquake analogy). **He does not supply a mechanism for why norms are slow, and says so himself**: "It is not my purpose here to analyze why social norms or values change slowly, but simply to state the fact." The gap stays open after a second naming source. See [[fast-and-slow-moving-institutions]] and, for the closest candidate mechanism this wiki holds, **D136**. | promoted |
| D3 | **Enforcement party** — first party (self-imposed), second party (retaliation), third party (community sanction, state coercion) | North (framework, explicit three-way; JEP: personal/kin vs. impersonal state) | For a sample of rule violations, code who actually imposed the cost. Note the distinction from *who is nominally responsible for enforcement* — the gap between the two is itself the finding. **Extended [`informal-institutions`, 2026-08-21]** with a worked instance of layered private enforcement: [[diamond-industry-private-ordering]] documents four distinct, stacked enforcement parties operating on the same institution simultaneously — diffuse market reputation, bourse-internal quasi-judicial bodies (graduated: fines/exclusion → suspension → expulsion), a reciprocal cross-bourse international network, and religious-court referral — a richer real case than any prior source in this row. | promoted |
| D135 | **Formal/informal interaction typology** — classify an informal practice against a formal institution by (i) whether the formal institution is currently effective, and (ii) whether the informal practice's goals converge or diverge from what the formal rule is meant to produce | Helmke & Levitsky (**model**: complementary, accommodating, competing, substitutive — a 2×2, each cell with a precise defining criterion rather than a family resemblance) | Code (i) from enforcement/compliance data on the formal rule itself, and (ii) from whether the actors using the informal practice are trying to achieve the same outcome the formal rule targets or a different one. **A documented limit**: [[diamond-industry-private-ordering]] does not fit any cell cleanly — formal law there is both effective and goal-compatible, yet still displaced by preference/efficiency rather than necessity, which the typology's effective/ineffective × compatible/conflicting cross has no cell for. See [[informal-institutions-typology]]. | candidate |
| D136 | **Convention vs. social norm** — whether an informal constraint's stability rests on empirical expectations alone (a convention) or on empirical *and* normative expectations, with possible sanction (a true social norm) | Bicchieri (**model**: a convention is "always a *followed* descriptive norm" in a pure coordination game; a social norm instead *transforms* an underlying mixed-motive game into a coordination game via the added normative-expectation layer) | For a given informal constraint, test whether people would still conform if they believed others expected nothing of them (isolates the normative-expectation component) versus whether they would still conform if they believed almost nobody else complied (isolates the empirical-expectation component). A convention should collapse under the second test alone; a social norm should survive it as long as the normative expectation holds. **Predicts different intervention points**: shifting a convention requires only changing observed behaviour; shifting a social norm requires changing beliefs about what others think *ought* to happen. Untested by any source this wiki holds — Bicchieri's chapter states the distinction formally but does not itself run the diagnostic on a real case. See [[social-norms-and-conditional-preferences]]. | candidate |
| D137 | **Network-density threshold for multilateral reputation** — the minimum connectivity of an information-transmission network required for reputation-based enforcement to extend beyond bilateral relationships | Greif (**model**, implied by the mechanism but not itself quantified: multilateral punishment requires that potential future employers can learn of an agent's past conduct, so the maximum sustainable "coalition" is bounded by the network's reach) | No source held here states or tests a minimum-connectivity threshold. In principle: for a given enforcement network, measure the probability that information about a given member's conduct reaches a randomly selected other member within some time window, and test whether enforcement remains multilateral (network-wide ostracism) below some density or degrades to bilateral (only the directly wronged party retaliates). See [[maghribi-traders-reputation-mechanism]]. | candidate |
| D4 | **Direction of monitor accountability** — monitors accountable to the users vs. to an external principal | Ostrom (design principles 4A/4B; **empirical**: local monitoring is the strongest single correlate of good resource condition) | Who appoints, pays, and can remove the monitor. Answerable from primary documents in most institutions. | promoted |
| D5 | **Sanction design** — externally imposed vs. participant-designed; graduated vs. fixed severity | Ostrom (design principle 5; **empirical**: self-designed sanctioning reached ~90% of optimal returns in lab CPR games, the best of any tested condition) | Read the sanction schedule: does severity escalate with repeat violation, and did the sanctioned population participate in setting it? **Extended [`informal-institutions`, 2026-08-21]** with a controlled causal isolation Ostrom's own field/meta-analytic work cannot supply: Ostrom, Walker & Gardner's lab experiment directly compares *self-chosen* against *externally imposed* sanctioning of the identical mechanism — chosen sanctioning (93% gross/90% net yield, 4% defection) outperforms imposed sanctioning (85%/67%) even when both follow one-shot communication, and imposed sanctioning **without** communication is actively counterproductive net of its own costs (37% gross falls to 9% net). See [[covenants-with-and-without-a-sword]]. | promoted |
| D6 | **Contract law regime** — court-enforced legal rules vs. private ordering/forbearance | Williamson | Will an external court hear a dispute arising inside this relationship? Measure as the share of disputes resolved by an external adjudicator vs. internally by fiat. | promoted |

### Decision rights and accountability

| # | Dimension | Named by | Operationalisation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D7 | **Locus of rule-making authority** — are those affected by a rule authorised to change it? | Ostrom (design principle 3). **Named again, at national scale, by the `institutions-and-growth` batch [2026-08-20]**: Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson's "breadth of the group holding political power" (narrow elite vs. broad cross-section, with franchise breadth as the worked measure across the 1832–1918 British extensions) and Besley & Persson's θ ∈ [0, ½], "the representativeness of political institutions through checks and balances or electoral systems", formalised as the weight an incumbent places on the excluded group's welfare | Fraction of individuals materially affected by a rule who hold a formal vote or veto over changing it. Compute from the constitutive documents. **D92 was reserved by the `institutions-and-growth` ingest for "breadth of the power-holding group / political inclusiveness" and folded here instead of being added** — the operationalisation is identical (what share of the affected population holds a formal say), and the batch adds naming weight at a new scale rather than a new axis. The number is left unused so the check stays visible. Two things the new naming sources contribute *to this row* rather than to a new one: AJR's **de jure / de facto** distinction, which warns that the formal-vote fraction this row counts may not be the operative one — a group with unconstrained de facto power can rewrite the formal allocation, as Pinochet did in 1980 — so score the formal fraction alongside a check on whether it has been rewritten by force within the observation window; and Besley & Persson's θ, which is the same construct as a *continuous incumbent preference weight* rather than a headcount, and is not independently measurable in their source. **Extended [`power-and-accountability`, 2026-08-21]**: Acemoglu & Robinson's *Persistence of Power* supplies the formal derivation the de jure/de facto naming previously lacked — a dynamic game in which elite de facto-power investment (endogenous, because the elite are few and can solve the collective-action problem) offsets citizens' de jure gains (exogenous, because citizens' numbers prevent equivalent coordination), producing an invariance result in the baseline case and, under a durable-regime extension, **"captured democracy"**: a procedurally genuine, durably democratic regime in which the elite nonetheless secures its preferred economic institutions with probability *higher* than under nondemocracy. See [[de-jure-vs-de-facto-power-and-captured-democracy]]. | promoted |
| D8 | **Residual claimancy / incentive intensity** — does the decision-maker's own income vary with the surplus their decision generates? | Libecap (**own case work**: politicians and bureaucrats "are not direct residual rent claimants"); Williamson (incentive intensity, strong vs. weak) | Read the compensation and budget rules: what fraction of the surplus (or loss) from a decision accrues to the decision-maker? Binary at minimum (residual claimant / not), continuous where pay is formula-linked. **Personal payoff asymmetry, the central mechanism in both the OECD paper ("no expectation of appropriating financial gains" against real career and legal downside) and Hood (blame weighs more than credit; promotion, bonuses and tenure ride on failure only), is a special case of this row and is registered here rather than duplicated** — it is residual claimancy measured on the *individual officeholder* and asymmetrically, upside and downside scored separately. Score them separately: an officeholder can be a zero claimant on the upside and a full claimant on the downside, which is the configuration both sources describe. | promoted |
| D9 | **Discretion vs. rule-boundedness** — how much of the decision space is unreviewable judgement | Libecap (**own case work**: regulators preserve discretion by choosing revocable permits over transferable rights); North (framework: institutions "lower the price of acting on one's ideas" — voting systems, lifetime judicial tenure) | Share of consequential allocations made by discretionary determination vs. by formula, auction, or entitlement. Also: count decisions with no reviewable standard. **Discretion-limiting rules, named by Bozeman (Kaufman: "Had we more trust in our public officers, we would feel less impelled to limit discretion by means of minutely detailed directions and prescriptions") and implied by Hammond's core (bureaucratic discretion is whatever the veto structure fails to reach), are registered here rather than as a new row.** Bozeman's contribution is the *cause* of discretion limits (distrust, external control — D42), Hammond's is a formal derivation of the residual (D44); the measured quantity is this one. **Tension recorded [incentives-and-institutional-form, 2026-08-19] — a tension, not a conflict.** This row's Libecap entry reads rule-boundedness through a self-serving lens: regulators *preserve* their own discretion, choosing revocable permits over transferable rights because secure rights would weaken it. Dixit and Holmstrom read the opposite move — an agent's discretion being *reduced* by rules — as rational efficient design rather than pathology. Dixit: micromanagement is "an unavoidable consequence of, or a less costly way of coping with, the asymmetric observability of multiple outcomes affecting multiple principals," and a principal whose own dimension is unobservable "may resort to imposing crude constraints on the agent's actions." Holmstrom & Milgrom: "the rigid rules and limits that characterize bureaucracy... constitute an optimal response to difficulties in measuring and rewarding performance." **Preserving and reducing discretion are opposite moves, so this is not a strict contradiction** — Libecap's actor is a rule-maker protecting his own decision space; Dixit's and Holmstrom's are principals constraining someone else's. But the two put opposite priors on the same measured quantity, so **a score on D9 is not self-interpreting**. Read it jointly with D50 (action × outcome observability) and D42 (external-control density, which since the 2026-08-20 merge carries D51's principal multiplicity): rule-boundedness under many principals with unobservable outcomes is what Dixit predicts; rule-boundedness where the rule-maker keeps the discretion and the agent loses it is what Libecap describes. Not filed as a conflict — see [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]] and [[multitask-incentive-theory]]. | promoted |
| D10 | **Constraint on the top decision-maker** — arbitrary vs. structurally shackled | North (JEP: ruler shackling, Glorious Revolution; self-restraint "seldom successful for very long") | Count of independent actors able to block an expropriative act, and whether any has ever exercised it. Market-priced alternative in D11. **Cross-referenced to D44, not merged [lint remediation, 2026-08-20].** The first limb here is a bare veto count, which is exactly what D44's composite exists to prevent: Hammond's result is that a count alone "predicts nothing — anything can happen" until the veto-holders' preferences are placed. **Carry that warning onto this row.** The two are kept separate because they count different things over different act classes: D10 counts who can block *an expropriative act by the top decision-maker* and asks whether they ever have, which is North's ruler-shackling question; D44 counts who must assent to *any* change to the status quo, and pairs the count with the preference profile. The second limb here — has any blocker ever exercised the power — is a behavioural check D44 does not have, and it is what stops D10 collapsing into a paper count. **When scoring D10, score D44's preference limb alongside it**: a high blocker count whose holders share the ruler's interest in expropriating is not a constraint, and only the preference profile distinguishes the two cases. | promoted |
| D11 | **Property-right / entitlement security** — full transferable right vs. revocable use right | Libecap (**own case work**, with the sharpest measure in these sources); Ostrom (five-right bundle: access, withdrawal, management, exclusion, alienation); North | Two methods. (i) Code which of the five rights the holder actually has. (ii) **Market-priced**: the lease-to-sale price ratio of the entitlement estimates the discount for expected future erosion — US ITQ ratio ≈ 2× New Zealand's, implying ~50% probability of erosion. Method (ii) is the only operationalisation in these sources that yields a number from market data. | promoted |
| D12 | **Exit / voice** — can the governed leave, and can they contract differently? | Ostrom (citizens "vote with their feet"; incorporated communities can renegotiate contracts with producers; neighbourhoods inside a large city can do neither) | Count of substitutable providers reachable at acceptable switching cost, and whether the governed unit holds a renegotiable contract or is administratively assigned. | promoted |
| D13 | **Recognition by higher authority** — does the surrounding state recognise this body's right to make its own rules? | Ostrom (design principle 7) | Is the rule-making power recognised in statute or higher-court doctrine, and how often has it been overridden in the last N years? Override frequency is the better measure — formal recognition alone is cheap. | promoted |

### Reform: what it is made of, and whether anyone can tell if it worked

*Added by the `enabling-institutional-change` ingest, 2026-08-19, from Finan/Olken/Pande, the NAO, Bunse & Fritz, True/Jones/Baumgartner, O'Reilly & Tushman and Andrews/Pritchett/Woolcock. ### Institution-level measurement instruments, and what each one cannot reach

*Added by the `theory-of-the-firm` ingest, 2026-08-20, from Gompers/Ishii/Metrick, Bloom/Sadun/Van Reenen, Aghion & Tirole and Alchian & Demsetz.* **Read this note before using D110 or D111.** This block is different in kind from every block above it. The register's standing problem, stated at Q90 and repeated across six batches, is that the framework operates at institution level while nearly all the evidence behind it is a **single scalar per country**. D110 and D111 are the first two rows in this register backed by instruments that **score an individual organisation**, and each has been run on thousands of them — ~1,500 US public firms a year for D110, 11,383 firms in 34 countries for D111. Both are **scoreable tomorrow**, and the block note exists to be precise about what "tomorrow" costs and where each stops:

- **They fail in different ways, and the difference is the useful part.** D110 needs documents to exist and to mean something; it fails silently when the document is decoupled from practice. D111 needs a reachable, honest, correctly-placed respondent; it fails when the answers are scripted or the right person cannot be got on the phone. Neither is strictly better, and an organisation scored on only one has been measured on only one of these risks.
- **Both are gated by D90.** See the extension in that row for which limb of the D90 diagnostic binds on which instrument.
- **D112 is `candidate` and is not scoreable at all**, and it is registered anyway because the gap is the finding: Aghion & Tirole derive the drivers of the formal/real authority gap formally and then **propose** instruments for measuring it without building one. The row records what would have to be built.
- **D113 and D114 come from a 1972 theory paper with no data.** D113 is scoreable from constitutive documents. D114 is not document-scoreable in the same way — it is a classification of a production technology — and the row says so rather than pretending otherwise.
- **D115 was reserved and not used.** The check found the "competition / product-market discipline" axis Bloom et al. name as a moderator already covered by D35 (funding mechanism) and D25 (measurability) scored together for the wiki's purposes; the number is left unused rather than filled so the check stays visible.

### Measurement construction, and the instruments that cannot reach an institution

*Added by the `measuring-institutions` ingest, 2026-08-21, from Kaufmann & Kraay (WGI), Pemstein et al. (V-Dem), Munck & Verkuilen, Arndt & Oman, the WWBI codebook and methodology report, and the QoG codebook.* **Read this note first, because two of the four rows are `rejected` and the rejections are the point of the block.**

This batch was commissioned to find out whether the field's flagship measurement instruments can be pointed at a single institution. **They cannot, and all seven sources say so independently.** Every substantive score in the batch is a national aggregate. WWBI comes closest and still stops at country × sector × occupation with **no agency identifier anywhere in its schema**. What is new is not the finding — six previous batches have hit the same wall — but the **mechanism**, which is now explicit and documented in the instruments' own words:

- **V-Dem instructs its expert coders to average away sub-national and branch heterogeneity *before scoring*.** The instruction, reproduced verbatim in the QoG codebook across several public-sector-corruption items, tells coders to average out any perceived discrepancy "between branches of the public sector, between the national/federal and subnational/state level, or between the core bureaucracy and employees working with public service delivery."
- **[[world-management-survey]] establishes that 77% of the variation in a measurable dimension of institutional quality sits *within* country-and-industry.**
- Put together: **the dominant instruments are structurally built to discard exactly the variation that carries most of the signal**, at the point of data generation rather than in the aggregation step. This is not a criticism of V-Dem — for characterising a national regime, instructing coders to give one national answer removes rater-specific noise and is the correct choice — but it is decisive here, and it is recorded prominently at [[open-questions]] **Q90**.

**Two rows are registered `candidate` and both are document-based and institution-scoreable** — D120 and D121 — and neither comes from a score in this batch. They come from *methods* in it, turned on a different object. **Two rows are registered `rejected`, and recording them is the point**: it documents that the field's flagship instruments are inadmissible here, and exactly why, so the question is not re-opened every batch. **D115–D119 are unused**, reserved by the `theory-of-the-firm` numbering and not filled; the gap is spare capacity.

Both are readable from the scorecard's own hierarchy. (iii) **Justified measurement level**: is each indicator's scale (binary / ordinal / interval) argued for, or asserted? (iv) **Replicability**: are coding rules, data sources, who scores, and any inter-scorer reliability test **published inside the institution**? Munck & Verkuilen found only two of nine indices ran and reported a reliability test at all — the base rate is the interesting comparison. (v) **Aggregation rule against a stated theory**: does the rule combining components into a headline number (additive? weighted? multiplicative? a traffic light?) express an explicit claim about how the components relate — necessary, sufficient, substitutable, complementary — and has it been stress-tested against an alternative rule? **This is the component most likely to score zero, and the wiki's own instruments score badly on it too**: D110 sums 24 provisions unweighted with no strength gradation, D111 z-scores and averages 18 items with no theory of their relation. **Why this is worth a row rather than left as method.** It is fully **document-based and institution-scoreable** — the scorecard, its definitions and its aggregation formula are internal documents, not a survey and not a market price — and it measures something no other row does: not how the institution performs, but whether the institution can tell. It pairs directly with **D65**'s question (does the design specify a condition under which it would judge itself to have failed) and with **D91(iii)** (is performance actually measured, and by whom). **What would promote it: one application to a real institution's scorecard, with the five components reported separately.** Nobody has run it — Munck & Verkuilen apply it only to country indices. **A close cousin observed running [`institution-case-profiles`, 2026-08-21], not yet the full application that would promote this row**: FDA's PDUFA fee revenue is checked against allowable-activity boundaries via an "activity-based reporting system" that ties payroll dollars to time actually spent on allowable work — a real, operating construction-and-audit mechanism, on a fee system rather than a KPI scorecard proper. See [[fda-case-profile]]. Still awaiting the row's own promotion condition. | candidate |
| D121 | **External-threshold gaming exposure** — whether an institution's funding, eligibility or classification is gated by a **bright-line threshold on a single external published index**, and whether its own reported metrics show a discontinuity at that threshold | Arndt & Oman (**empirical for the mechanism's country-level instance**: the US Millennium Challenge Account used the KKZ Control of Corruption indicator as an **in/out median cutoff**, criticised by Kaufmann et al. themselves for ignoring a "non-trivial probability" of misclassification and worse for low-income countries with fewer underlying sources; and via Burnside & Dollar and Berthélemy & Tichit, a one-SD improvement in rule-of-law/democracy indices corresponded to **28% more aid and 50% more IDA finance**). **Independently named from the other side by Kaufmann & Kraay**, who exclude objective/de jure data from WGI *because* such measures are "susceptible to 'gaming' where policy makers target reforms to narrowly change specific measures simply because they happen to be included in an aggregate indicator" | **Two steps, both runnable on one institution from documents plus its own published metrics.** **Step 1 — find the binding threshold.** Read the institution's funding agreements, grant conditions, accreditation or licensing requirements, regulatory classification rules and covenant terms for any clause conditioning money, eligibility or status on a **numeric cutoff in an externally published index or rating** — a bond rating, an accreditation score, a league-table rank, a compliance index, a national target. Record for each: which index, which threshold, what is on the other side of it, and whether the consequence is continuous (more score, more money) or discontinuous (in or out). Discontinuous thresholds are the ones that bite. **Step 2 — test for the discontinuity.** Take the institution's own reported values of the gated metric over the longest available run and look for **bunching immediately above the threshold**, for a suspiciously low frequency just below it, and for a level shift dated to the year the threshold was adopted. This does: the presence or absence of a bound external threshold is a binary fact about the institution's funding documents, and the discontinuity test is a distributional check on numbers it already publishes. It also has a clean null: an institution with no bound external threshold cannot be gaming one, which makes the axis falsifiable per institution rather than only in aggregate. **Distinguish from D25 (measurability of output) and D50**: those ask whether the institution's output *can* be observed; this asks whether a specific published number has been made **load-bearing for its survival by someone outside it**. **What would promote it: one worked application, with both steps run.** Nothing in this batch does it at institution level — every instance Arndt & Oman document is a country facing a donor. | candidate |

| D123 | **Public-sector sector/occupation aggregate** (WWBI employment share, wage premium, wage compression, wage bill) as an institution-level axis | World Bank Bureaucracy Lab (WWBI codebook v3.0 and 2021 methodology report: 192–302 indicators, 144–202 economies, 909–1,050 harmonised surveys plus IMF and ICP administrative data) | **`rejected`, and this is the batch's closest approach to admissibility — which is what makes the exact stopping point worth recording.** The panel unit is country-year cross-tabulated by sector (public/private), industry (healthcare, education, public administration, **core** public administration, public safety, social services) and occupation (five ISCO major groups, plus nine named occupations for the compression variables including judges, hospital doctors and police officers). **These are functional labour-market categories aggregated across every agency in a country performing that function, and there is no agency or ministry identifier variable anywhere in the schema.** The codebook's own ISIC note fixes the boundary: division 75/84 covers "administration of the school system (regulations, checks, curricula)… but teaching itself does not" — the classification tracks *what kind of work a person does*, not *which named institution employs them*. ## What this register does not yet contain

**[wiki synthesis]** The gap is structural and worth stating, because it is the gap between this literature and what this wiki is for. The foundations-NIE sources supply axes describing *rule-sets, transactions, and resource regimes*. They supply almost nothing describing the **internal composition of a single organisation**. Specifically absent from all five sources:

- selection and promotion criteria for personnel (who ends up inside — [[open-questions]] Q4);
- mandate clarity and multiplicity of objectives;
- funding source and its stability;
- hierarchy layer count as a behavioural variable rather than a size proxy;
- age of the organisation as distinct from age of the rule-set (see [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]]);
- career incentives, tenure structures, and internal labour markets.

**Partly closed [scale-effects, 2026-08-19].** D35–D41 supply hierarchy depth and span as behavioural variables rather than size proxies, funding mechanism, coupling strength, the two information costs, and the authority/proximity gap — and Klimek/Hanel/Thurner supply promotion probability p, subordinates-per-promotion r and attrition γ as measurable parameters of a career-incentive regime (registered inside D36 and D37 rather than as their own rows, since they are parameters of the same structure). Two items on the list above are **not** closed, and one got worse:

- Personnel selection (Q4) is still named by nobody.

**Partly closed again [risk-aversion-in-large-institutions, 2026-08-19].** D42–D48 supply the control and incentive environment, and two items on the original absent-list move: **career incentives and tenure structures** are now named by Hood (tenure security and time-to-accountability-event as moderators of blame sensitivity, registered inside D45), Holmström (career-stage effects on effort, and D47) and Manso (D48). **Personnel selection (Q4) moves from "named by nobody" to "named once, badly"**: the OECD reports a Canadian "clay layer" in which "innovative ideas are not evaluated by their merit, but by the personal characteristics and social capital involved" of middle managers. That is a selection-and-gatekeeping claim, it is exactly the axis Q4 asks about — and it rests on one country scan in an advocacy document with undeveloped coding protocols. Not enough for a row. Q4 stays open.
- **Organisational age is still supplied by no source in this wiki, after three literatures.** The scale-effects batch looks like an age literature and is not one: every age-flavoured claim in it turns out to be individual career tenure (Klimek), technology vintage (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg), evolutionary formation time (Simon), or an aside the author's own footnote disowns (Niskanen). Rajan & Wulf report firm age only as a sample statistic (~85 years) and never use it as a covariate. **The `institutional-evolution` batch was commissioned as the place age evidence would appear, and does not supply it either.** Arthur's clock is adoption count *n* with an express warning against reading it as calendar years (D58, `rejected`); North's durations are post-hoc narrative with no dose-response; Skocpol's counts, the batch's only real longitudinal data, measure organisational *survival* rather than behavioural change with age; DiMaggio & Powell's age-flavoured claims are about *field* maturity (D57), which is a different variable; Meyer & Rowan supply nothing on age or size and say so; Krasner's own cited periodisation undercuts his phase framing; and Thelen argues directly that contestation begins as soon as a rule is laid down, which would remove the phase structure an age-gated model needs. **Three batches, no measurement of institutional behaviour against elapsed time since founding.** See [[open-questions]] Q7 and Q39.

**Partly closed a third time, and one item genuinely moves [incentives-and-institutional-form, 2026-08-19].** D49–D51 supply **mandate clarity and multiplicity of objectives** — the second item on the original absent-list — in two operational forms rather than one: D51 counts the principals whose objectives the mandate is trying to hold together, and D49 records whether those objectives compete for the same effort. Dixit's own limiting condition on implicit incentives makes the pair load-bearing: career concerns work best "with a narrow, clear mission," which is precisely what a high D51 with `k > 0` denies.

**Personnel selection (Q4) now has measurement, but still no axis.** Prendergast supplies the first real decomposition in this wiki of selection against incentive effects: of Lazear's 35% productivity gain from a piece-rate switch, roughly **one third is selection** (turnover data show less able workers leaving and more able ones replacing them), and Paarsch & Shearer's structural estimate isolates the incentive effect on a *given* worker at ~10% against a raw 35% gap. Prendergast states the general point himself — "the selection effects appear to be of roughly equal size to the incentive effects, despite the overwhelming focus on incentive effects in the theoretical literature" — and Weiss/Hansen show the selection can be **U-shaped** rather than monotonic under team pay. What this does **not** give the register is an *axis*: it establishes that the reward structure is itself a selection instrument, in two occupations with unusually clean output measures, but nobody names a measurable property of an institution's selection regime. Two occupations is not a dimension. **Q4 stays open**, now with evidence attached rather than none. See [[multitask-incentive-theory]].

**The personnel-selection gap closes [enabling-institutional-change, 2026-08-19] — the first item on the original absent-list to be closed by randomised evidence.** Four batches have circled this. Finan, Olken & Pande supply what the earlier ones could not: not an inference from a reward structure's side-effects, but **the selection instrument itself, randomised** — wage offers across 106 Mexican recruitment sites, job-ad framing across 48 Zambian districts, advertised expected earnings in Uganda — each changing *who applies* before any incentive operates on anyone employed, with effect sizes and an elasticity. Registered as **D70**, `promoted`, with the four components stated. Two limits keep it honest: everything is measured at **recruitment**, nothing at promotion, so the "who ends up inside" question is answered at the front door only; and the evidence is developing- and middle-income-country field experiments, 2007–2015. See [[personnel-economics-of-the-state]] and [[open-questions]] Q4.

**Organisational age: still nothing, after five literatures.** This batch does not supply it either, and one row exists to prevent it being mistaken for a supply. D73 is a property of a *decision* — how long the benefit takes relative to the authoriser's tenure — not of an institution's elapsed life. True, Jones & Baumgartner's scale finding is about level of budget aggregation, not years. Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock's nearest claim runs the other way: repeated reform cycles over 20 years do **not** accumulate into capability, which is a claim that elapsed effort predicts nothing, on two cases. Q7 and Q39 stand.

**Fourth batch, same hole [institutional-stagnation, 2026-08-19].** D60–D65 add review architecture, procedural load and the capture-coordination requirement — and **organisational age is still supplied by no source in this wiki**, now across four consecutive batches that looked for it (`scale-effects`, `risk-aversion-in-large-institutions`, `institutional-evolution`, `institutional-stagnation`). This batch is the sharpest instance because it was commissioned to find exactly that axis and its sources are the canonical decay literature. Where duration appears it is always attached to something other than the institution: Heckelman's variable across 53 studies is **years of national political stability** ([[institutional-sclerosis]]); Miller & Dudley's is **130 years of a national rule corpus** ([[regulatory-accretion]]); Schleicher & Bagley's is **a legal regime dated to the 1970s–80s and applied uniformly regardless of any agency's founding date** ([[state-capacity-and-level-of-government]]); Fukuyama's veto structure is 230 years old and he pins the dysfunction to 1980s polarisation ([[political-decay-and-vetocracy]]). Three of the batch's founding texts state their own mechanism's age-independence **unprompted** — Merton, Stigler and Selznick ([[goal-displacement-and-bureaucratic-ritualism]], [[regulatory-capture]], [[cooptation]]). Selznick supplies the register's own warning against the substitution, and it should be read as binding on any future age row: size or scope changes driven by "the availability of markets, legislative delegations, the swing of opinion" are "accidental from the point of view of the organizational process" and must not be folded into organisational theory unless restated as an internal propensity. See [[open-questions]] Q7 and Q61.

**What an age row would have to look like**, stated here so the next batch can be scored against it: years since the institution's own founding (or since its last charter-level reconstitution, recorded separately), paired with a **directly observed** accumulation variable measured on that same institution — rule count, coalition or constituency count, or a capture indicator — rather than a duration proxy standing in for the mechanism. No source in four batches has run that pairing.

**Sixth batch, and the register's worst yield per source [institutions-and-growth, 2026-08-20].** Nine sources produced **four rows, one of them `rejected` and one `candidate`**, and the reason is a level-of-analysis problem rather than a thin literature. **Every source in the batch measures "institutions" as a single national scalar and none attempts any sub-national decomposition.** AJR's variable is one foreign-investor expropriation-risk score per country; Rodrik's is one perceptions-based rule-of-law composite per country; Besley & Persson's fiscal and legal capacity are national aggregates; Glaeser et al. attack the same national indices without disputing the level. Wade alone names individual institutions and gives them causal weight — MITI, MOF, Taiwan's central bank, ITRI/ERSO, Saemaul Undong — and does so in comparative-historical narrative; his one quasi-experimental citation (Lane 2017) is at *sector* level, comparing targeted against non-targeted industries, not one agency against another. **D91 is the exception and the batch's one clear institution-level axis**, because performance-conditionality is a property of a granting body and its client set rather than of a polity. D90 transfers because it is a test of a measure. D93's institution-level form is this wiki's own untested translation and is labelled as such on the row. **Nothing in this batch supports a causal claim about an individual institution's effect on economic production** — only about national institutional environments, and contested even there. See [[open-questions]] Q90 and [[colonial-origins-and-the-settler-mortality-instrument]].

**Organisational age: sixth batch, still nothing, and this one is instructive about why.** The batch is full of duration variables and not one attaches to an institution's own tenure. AJR's persistence is a *national institutional regime* holding its type for 300+ years — and they concede in their own conclusion that "we do not as yet have a satisfactory understanding of the mechanisms through which institutions persist". Rodrik's Section IV.B writes a decay rate δ into a stock-flow equation for national institutional quality and estimates no value for it. Besley & Persson's 150 years of English fiscal-capacity accumulation is national capital formation. Wade's one change-over-time claim is a **zig-zag** — Korean, Taiwanese and Japanese innovation bureaucracies moving from centralised control to decentralised "peripheral islands" and back — which is cyclical reorganisation, predicted by neither position in [[decay-as-real-vs-decay-as-overstated]]. Q7 and Q39 stand.

**Seventh batch, and the age hole changes character rather than closing [bureaucracy-and-public-choice, 2026-08-20].** D100–D104 add the progression margin, the selection trait, reform-content identification and personnel embeddedness — and **organisational age is still supplied by no source in this wiki as a measured variable.** What changed is that it is now supplied as an *assertion*. Downs's compendium hypothesis C.11 states that as bureaus age they accumulate rules, shift goals from performing their functions toward maintaining their structures, become subject to inertia and expand their scope — the first age-dependent organisational decay mechanism asserted by any source across seven batches, and **it carries no data of any kind**, no derivation from the paper's own axioms and no falsification condition. **No row was registered for it**, and the reason should be stated so it is not re-litigated: an axis has to be a measurable property of an institution, and "elapsed years since founding" already meets that test trivially — what the register has always lacked is not the clock but the **directly observed accumulation variable to pair it with**, which Downs does not supply either. His one usable contribution is that C.11 makes five separate directional predictions (efficiency, rule count, goal displacement, inertia, scope), at least three of which are countable from an agency's own records, against Merton's flat line on all five — the sharpest discriminating test the age literature has produced in this wiki, and still unrun. Filed at [[downs-vs-merton-on-age-dependence]]. **The requirement stated after the fourth batch is unchanged and unmet.**

**The personnel-selection gap: closed at the front door, now open behind it.** D70 closed the recruitment half of Q4 with randomised evidence. This batch shows the other half was never in the register: **D100** is the progression margin — removal cost, transfer as covert removal, the de jure/de facto coverage gap — and it is `candidate`, not `promoted`, because the quantity its welfare sign turns on (whether the bureaucrat or the politician is further from the social optimum) is measured by nobody. **D101** adds what the register also lacked: not how selection is done, but **what it selects for**. Together they mean "who ends up inside" is now a two-gate question in this register rather than a one-gate one.

**A capture gap, recorded here because it now affects a `promoted` row.** Two texts this register depends on are not held by this wiki. **Weber's "Bureaucracy"** — the only open copy is a scanned image with no text layer, OCR failed and was discarded — so every "Weberian" row and page in the wiki rests on second-hand operationalisations (Evans & Rauch's ten-item survey scale; V-Dem's expert coding), one of whose authors states in a footnote that they deliberately excluded rule-governed jurisdiction from the scale because it is "a double-edged sword… producing rigidity and organizational sclerosis when carried further." **Wilson's *Bureaucracy* (1989)** — the source of D50's typology, which the wiki holds only through Dixit's citation, and which the 1967 Wilson essay the wiki *does* hold is now confirmed not to contain. Both are acquisition targets. See [[open-questions]] Q103.

D28 is measurable but currently unlinked to any tested prediction; the rest are not in the register because no source here names them. They should be the first things a later ingest of organisational-theory or bureaucratic-politics sources adds.

**Eighth batch, and the register's first institution-level measurement instruments [theory-of-the-firm, 2026-08-20].** This is the batch that closes the register's oldest structural gap — not the age gap, which it does not close either, but the **measurement-level** gap. Every batch before this one supplied axes with operationalisations the wiki had reasoned out; six of the seven supplied their evidence as one number per country. D110 and D111 are the first two rows here backed by instruments **built to score an individual organisation and actually run on thousands of them**, and both are scoreable tomorrow by one competent person: D110 from an entity's own charter, bylaws and background law, D111 from a 45-minute double-blind interview with one correctly-placed respondent. **They fail differently and that is what makes the pair worth more than either alone** — a document score survives an unreachable respondent and dies on decoupling; an interview score survives a missing charter and dies on scripted answers. Both are gated by D90. The batch's single most consequential number is not in a row at all: **77% of the variation in D111 scores sits within country-and-industry**, against 13% explained by country — which is the first direct evidence in this wiki that a measurable dimension of institutional quality is a property of the organisation rather than of the nation. Recorded at [[open-questions]] Q90, whose fork it bears on.

**What this batch does not supply, stated so the closure is not overread.** (i) **Organisational age: eighth batch, still nothing usable.** Bloom et al. do report management level rising and dispersion falling with plant age, but the mechanism they give is **selection** — badly managed young plants improve or exit — which is survivor composition, not an ageing institution changing. Their structural δM = 13.3% management depreciation rate is the closest thing in the wiki to a measured decay rate for an organisational capability, and it is model-derived, scoped to management practice, and points the opposite way from Chandler's claim that capability outlives its personnel. Gompers et al. control for firm age and report no coefficient on it. The requirement stated after the fourth batch stands. (ii) **Neither instrument has been run on a public bureau.** D110's authors are silent on whether the approach transfers; D111's design contains nothing that requires a shareholder or a market, so the transfer is *plausible and untested*, which is what the row says. (iii) **Decision rights are named by four sources in this batch and measured by none.** D112 records the gap rather than an axis. Note in particular that D111, the most portable instrument here, contains **no decentralisation or authority item at all** — so the batch's measurement strength and its decision-rights strength are in different sources and do not meet.

**Second-hand marking.** Where the Named-by column cites Libecap, the entry draws on **his own** oil, fisheries, and groundwater case work. Libecap's summaries of North elsewhere in that paper are second-hand and are not used as a naming source in this register.

**Ninth batch, and it closes nothing while making the closure condition sharper [measuring-institutions, 2026-08-21].** Seven sources on how governance and bureaucracy are measured produced **four rows, two of them `rejected`** — the register's second-worst yield per source after `institutions-and-growth`, and for the same reason, stated more precisely than before. **Every substantive score in the batch is a national aggregate, and all seven sources confirm it independently.** What is new is the **mechanism of the loss**, which two facts now fix in place. **(i)** V-Dem's coder instructions tell coders to average away discrepancy "between branches of the public sector, between the national/federal and subnational/state level, or between the core bureaucracy and employees working with public service delivery" **before scoring** — so sub-national and branch heterogeneity is destroyed at data generation, not at aggregation, and cannot be recovered by any downstream reprocessing. **(ii)** [[world-management-survey]] measured 77% of the variation in institutional quality as sitting *within* country-and-industry. Together: **the field's dominant instruments are structurally built to discard the stratum that carries most of the signal.** That is not a defect — V-Dem is characterising national regimes and the instruction is correct for that purpose — but it converts "nobody has done the disaggregation" into "the instruments cannot do it, by construction", which is a different and more useful statement, and it is recorded at [[open-questions]] Q90.

**What this batch *does* supply, and it is method rather than data.** Four transferable techniques, none requiring a country: (1) **inverse-variance precision weighting with a formally propagated, reported margin of error** (WGI) — reusable if any register row is ever built from several partial proxies for one institution, with two conditions attached on the row's page (you need per-source error variances, which WGI gets from a 214-country sample an institution-level analyst does not have; and the independence-of-errors assumption has to be defended, since Arndt & Oman show relaxing it to 0.5 correlation **doubles** WGI's Rule of Law standard error); (2) **DIF correction via anchoring vignettes, bridge coders and lateral coders** (V-Dem) — the directly applicable answer for **D111** if this wiki ever fields multiple coders scoring one institution, and the only one of the four components that works on a single unit is the vignette, since with no overlap "this coder is strict" and "this institution is bad" are the same observation; (3) **the three-stage construction audit** (Munck & Verkuilen), runnable on any register row or instrument, and registered as **D120**; (4) **the bright-line-gaming warning** (Arndt & Oman), now with a concrete mechanism — a published index plus a threshold plus money on the other side — and registered as **D121**.

**Organisational age: ninth batch, still nothing, and this one could not have supplied it.** None of the seven sources contains an institutional-age variable of any kind. WWBI's schema structurally forecloses the question — its unit of observation excludes asking how a *given* institution's behaviour changes with its own size or age, because no given institution exists in it. The requirement stated after the fourth batch stands unchanged.

**A thin spot recorded as a lead rather than a gap.** The QoG codebook was sampled at roughly **3–5%** of its ~2,000 variable entries, and one family that went unread is the most promising unexplored item in this batch: the **Comparative Constitutions Project** (`ccp_*`), which codes the binary presence of named institution-types — a corruption commission, a human-rights commission, meritocratic civil-service recruitment — **from constitutional text**. That is document-based coding of institutional presence, the evidence type this register most lacks, and it is still one flag per country rather than a score of the institution. The adjacent Global Integrity "Africa Integrity Indicators" family pairs "Law: X is guaranteed" against "Practice: X is guaranteed" for the same feature — a de jure/de facto gap operationalised, also at country level, and the closest thing in the batch to D100(iv)'s coverage-against-exercise component. Neither was read as full variable entries. **Anything attributed to either above is inferred from the codebook's table of contents and category listings, not from a full read**, and both are acquisition targets.
