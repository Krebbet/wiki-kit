# Dimensions of Institutional Variation

A standing register of every axis of institutional variation named by the sources this wiki has ingested, with — for each — who named it, how it would be measured on a real institution, and its status. This is the seed of the wiki's general analytical framework (project outcome 2); it is meant to be extended by every subsequent ingest, not rewritten. Nothing here is a finding: the register records what the literature *proposes* as an axis and what it would take to put a number on it. Entries currently come from four foundational New Institutional Economics sources (North ×2, Ostrom, Williamson) plus Libecap's NBER retrospective — a literature that is theory and comparative history, not empirical identification, with the single exception of Ostrom's experimental and meta-analytic work. D35–D41 were added by the `scale-effects` ingest (2026-08-19) from Coase, Niskanen, Parkinson/Klimek, Simon, Rajan & Wulf and Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg; they are the first axes in the register that describe the **inside of a single organisation**, which the note at the end of the register previously recorded as wholly missing. D42–D48 were added by the `risk-aversion-in-large-institutions` ingest (2026-08-19) from Bozeman, Hammond, Hood, Kahneman & Lovallo, Holmström and Manso; they describe the **control and incentive environment** an organisation and its agents sit inside. Four axes that batch also named were **not** given rows, because they are instances of axes already here — personal payoff asymmetry under D8, rule rigidity under D1, discretion-limiting rules under D9 — and the notes are recorded in those rows instead. D54–D59 were added by the `institutional-evolution` ingest (2026-08-19) from Arthur, North's Nobel lecture, Pierson, DiMaggio & Powell, Meyer & Rowan, Thelen and Krasner; they describe **how an institution changes, what its structure tracks, and the field it sits in**, and one of the six (D58) is `rejected`. That batch also declined to re-register North's adaptive-vs-allocative efficiency axis, which duplicates the already-rejected D34 and arrives with no operationalisation the second time either — the note is recorded at D34 instead. D25a and D49–D53 were added by the `incentives-and-institutional-form` ingest (2026-08-19) from Holmstrom, Holmstrom & Milgrom, Hart, Dixit, Frey and Bénabou & Tirole; they describe the **contracting parameters** that determine how strong an incentive an institution can rationally attach to anything, and **two of those six rows are `rejected`** — recorded rather than dropped, per lint check 11.

## How to read this

**Status values.** `candidate` — named by a source and operationalisable, but not yet doing predictive work in this wiki. `promoted` — meets the promotion criterion below. `rejected` — cannot currently be measured as stated, with the reason recorded; the row stays so the problem is visible rather than silently dropped.

**Promotion criterion [wiki synthesis].** An axis is promoted when (i) at least two independent sources name it, *and* (ii) a measurement procedure can be stated that could be run against an institution's primary documents, budget, personnel records, or observable behaviour, without needing access the analyst would not plausibly have. Both conditions, not either. Criterion (i) is deliberately weak evidence of importance — it is a prior, revisable as soon as an axis proves it earns its place by predicting something.

**Admissibility [lint check 11].** A dimension with no operationalisation is not admissible as part of the framework. Where none can be stated, the row says so explicitly and is marked `rejected`, with a note on what restatement would readmit it.

**Evidence tier.** Every row is (b) a theoretical proposal, except where the Named-by column marks Ostrom's experimental/meta-analytic findings or Libecap's own case work, which are (a) empirical. Among the scale-effects additions, only Klimek/Hanel/Thurner's headcount data and Rajan & Wulf's firm panel are (a) empirical; Coase, Niskanen, Simon and Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg are (b) models their own authors describe as untested. Among the risk-aversion additions the tier distinction is the whole point and is unusually stark: **not one of D42–D48 has been measured against a behavioural outcome by the source that named it.** Bozeman's propositions are explicitly "potentially amenable to test" and untested; Hammond's composite is a theorem whose result is that the count alone predicts nothing; Hood's monitoring-design axis comes from an argumentative chapter; Kahneman & Lovallo's cadence result is derived inside a toy model; Holmström's and Manso's axes are parameters of formal models with no empirical content at all. Treat all seven as (b), and see [[risk-aversion-in-large-institutions]] for why that matters here more than elsewhere in the register. The operationalisation column is (c) wiki synthesis throughout — the sources rarely state how to measure what they name, and where they do it is noted.

### Rules and enforcement

| # | Dimension | Named by | Operationalisation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | **Formalisation** — formal written rules vs. informal constraints (norms, conventions, self-imposed codes) | North (JEP; framework); Ostrom (rules-in-use vs. formal organisation — 25% of coded fishery subgroups had no formal organisation at all) | Take a sample of consequential decisions; for each, code whether the binding constraint is documented and citable or unwritten practice. Report the ratio. **Rule rigidity, named by the OECD (Brazil's "heavy focus on rules, procedures and a rigid constitution") and by Bozeman, is registered here rather than as a new row** — Bozeman's whole point is that formalisation is a neutral trait and only its inefficacious subset is red tape (D42), so rigidity is this axis plus an efficacy judgement, not a separate axis. | promoted |
| D2 | **Formal/informal change-speed differential** | North (framework): "formal rules may change over night, but informal constraints do not" | Elapsed time between a formal rule change and observed change in the corresponding behaviour. Requires a before/after behavioural measure, so only applicable where behaviour is observable. | candidate |
| D3 | **Enforcement party** — first party (self-imposed), second party (retaliation), third party (community sanction, state coercion) | North (framework, explicit three-way; JEP: personal/kin vs. impersonal state) | For a sample of rule violations, code who actually imposed the cost. Note the distinction from *who is nominally responsible for enforcement* — the gap between the two is itself the finding. | promoted |
| D4 | **Direction of monitor accountability** — monitors accountable to the users vs. to an external principal | Ostrom (design principles 4A/4B; **empirical**: local monitoring is the strongest single correlate of good resource condition) | Who appoints, pays, and can remove the monitor. Answerable from primary documents in most institutions. | promoted |
| D5 | **Sanction design** — externally imposed vs. participant-designed; graduated vs. fixed severity | Ostrom (design principle 5; **empirical**: self-designed sanctioning reached ~90% of optimal returns in lab CPR games, the best of any tested condition) | Read the sanction schedule: does severity escalate with repeat violation, and did the sanctioned population participate in setting it? | candidate |
| D6 | **Contract law regime** — court-enforced legal rules vs. private ordering/forbearance | Williamson | Will an external court hear a dispute arising inside this relationship? Measure as the share of disputes resolved by an external adjudicator vs. internally by fiat. | candidate |

### Decision rights and accountability

| # | Dimension | Named by | Operationalisation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D7 | **Locus of rule-making authority** — are those affected by a rule authorised to change it? | Ostrom (design principle 3) | Fraction of individuals materially affected by a rule who hold a formal vote or veto over changing it. Compute from the constitutive documents. | candidate |
| D8 | **Residual claimancy / incentive intensity** — does the decision-maker's own income vary with the surplus their decision generates? | Libecap (**own case work**: politicians and bureaucrats "are not direct residual rent claimants"); Williamson (incentive intensity, strong vs. weak) | Read the compensation and budget rules: what fraction of the surplus (or loss) from a decision accrues to the decision-maker? Binary at minimum (residual claimant / not), continuous where pay is formula-linked. **Personal payoff asymmetry, the central mechanism in both the OECD paper ("no expectation of appropriating financial gains" against real career and legal downside) and Hood (blame weighs more than credit; promotion, bonuses and tenure ride on failure only), is a special case of this row and is registered here rather than duplicated** — it is residual claimancy measured on the *individual officeholder* and asymmetrically, upside and downside scored separately. Score them separately: an officeholder can be a zero claimant on the upside and a full claimant on the downside, which is the configuration both sources describe. | promoted |
| D9 | **Discretion vs. rule-boundedness** — how much of the decision space is unreviewable judgement | Libecap (**own case work**: regulators preserve discretion by choosing revocable permits over transferable rights); North (framework: institutions "lower the price of acting on one's ideas" — voting systems, lifetime judicial tenure) | Share of consequential allocations made by discretionary determination vs. by formula, auction, or entitlement. Also: count decisions with no reviewable standard. **Discretion-limiting rules, named by Bozeman (Kaufman: "Had we more trust in our public officers, we would feel less impelled to limit discretion by means of minutely detailed directions and prescriptions") and implied by Hammond's core (bureaucratic discretion is whatever the veto structure fails to reach), are registered here rather than as a new row.** Bozeman's contribution is the *cause* of discretion limits (distrust, external control — D42), Hammond's is a formal derivation of the residual (D44); the measured quantity is this one. **Tension recorded [incentives-and-institutional-form, 2026-08-19] — a tension, not a conflict.** This row's Libecap entry reads rule-boundedness through a self-serving lens: regulators *preserve* their own discretion, choosing revocable permits over transferable rights because secure rights would weaken it. Dixit and Holmstrom read the opposite move — an agent's discretion being *reduced* by rules — as rational efficient design rather than pathology. Dixit: micromanagement is "an unavoidable consequence of, or a less costly way of coping with, the asymmetric observability of multiple outcomes affecting multiple principals," and a principal whose own dimension is unobservable "may resort to imposing crude constraints on the agent's actions." Holmstrom & Milgrom: "the rigid rules and limits that characterize bureaucracy... constitute an optimal response to difficulties in measuring and rewarding performance." **Preserving and reducing discretion are opposite moves, so this is not a strict contradiction** — Libecap's actor is a rule-maker protecting his own decision space; Dixit's and Holmstrom's are principals constraining someone else's. But the two put opposite priors on the same measured quantity, so **a score on D9 is not self-interpreting**. Read it jointly with D50 (action × outcome observability) and D51 (principal multiplicity): rule-boundedness under many principals with unobservable outcomes is what Dixit predicts; rule-boundedness where the rule-maker keeps the discretion and the agent loses it is what Libecap describes. Not filed as a conflict — see [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]] and [[multitask-incentive-theory]]. | promoted |
| D10 | **Constraint on the top decision-maker** — arbitrary vs. structurally shackled | North (JEP: ruler shackling, Glorious Revolution; self-restraint "seldom successful for very long") | Count of independent actors able to block an expropriative act, and whether any has ever exercised it. Market-priced alternative in D11. | promoted |
| D11 | **Property-right / entitlement security** — full transferable right vs. revocable use right | Libecap (**own case work**, with the sharpest measure in these sources); Ostrom (five-right bundle: access, withdrawal, management, exclusion, alienation); North | Two methods. (i) Code which of the five rights the holder actually has. (ii) **Market-priced**: the lease-to-sale price ratio of the entitlement estimates the discount for expected future erosion — US ITQ ratio ≈ 2× New Zealand's, implying ~50% probability of erosion. Method (ii) is the only operationalisation in these sources that yields a number from market data. | promoted |
| D12 | **Exit / voice** — can the governed leave, and can they contract differently? | Ostrom (citizens "vote with their feet"; incorporated communities can renegotiate contracts with producers; neighbourhoods inside a large city can do neither) | Count of substitutable providers reachable at acceptable switching cost, and whether the governed unit holds a renegotiable contract or is administratively assigned. | candidate |
| D13 | **Recognition by higher authority** — does the surrounding state recognise this body's right to make its own rules? | Ostrom (design principle 7) | Is the rule-making power recognised in statute or higher-court doctrine, and how often has it been overridden in the last N years? Override frequency is the better measure — formal recognition alone is cheap. | candidate |

### Structure of the field

| # | Dimension | Named by | Operationalisation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|

### Attributes of what is being transacted

| # | Dimension | Named by | Operationalisation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D19 | **Asset specificity** — physical, human, site-specific, dedicated, brand-name, episodic/temporal | Williamson (central variable; explicitly a *design* variable — specificity can be engineered down at a cost in performance) | Redeployability: the fraction of the asset's value recoverable in its next-best use if the current counterparty disappeared. Estimable from resale/secondary-market data for physical assets; considerably harder for human and site specificity, where the honest answer is that no method in these sources measures it. | candidate |
| D20 | **Uncertainty / disturbance frequency** — outlier disturbances requiring unprogrammed adaptation | Williamson | Count consequential unprogrammed adaptations per unit time over the relationship's history: contract amendments, emergency deviations, renegotiations. | candidate |
| D21 | **Frequency of transaction** | Williamson (named as one of the three canonical attributes; not elaborated) | Transactions per period between the same parties. Trivial to measure, currently doing no work in this wiki. | candidate |
| D22 | **Complexity** — drives contractual incompleteness via bounded rationality | Williamson | Weakly measurable: share of foreseeable contingencies left unspecified in the governing document, or count of decision points requiring judgement rather than lookup. Both proxies are analyst-dependent; flagged as a soft measure. | candidate |
| D23 | **Nature of the good or resource** — subtractability of use × difficulty of exclusion (both continuous), plus boundability, observability, heterogeneity | Ostrom (four-way goods typology: private / public / toll / common-pool); Libecap (**own case work**, Table 1: small/boundable/observable/homogeneous vs. large/valuable/unobservable/heterogeneous/migratory) | Score subtractability and exclusion cost on continuous scales rather than assigning a goods category. For a physical resource, additionally code boundary observability and unit heterogeneity. | promoted |
| D24 | **Risk vs. uncertainty** — is the downside actuarially priceable? | North (JEP: marine insurance, portfolio diversification as the institutions that convert uncertainty into priced risk) | Does an insurance or hedging market exist for this exposure, and at what price? ### Information and measurement

| # | Dimension | Named by | Operationalisation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D25 | **Measurability of output and information flow** | North (JEP: the Suq lacks "institutions devoted to assembling and distributing market information" — no price quotations, no standardised weights and measures); North (framework: accuracy of information feedback to decision-makers); Ostrom (aggregated vs. individualised information; ease of measuring the resource — river vs. forest); Libecap (cost of verifying heterogeneous claims) | Can a third party verify the output at acceptable cost? Score on three components: existence of standardised units, published prices or performance statistics, and independent audit. The most-cited axis in the foundations set. **Partly superseded [scale-effects, 2026-08-19].** The *information flow* half of this axis conflated two costs that Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg show move hierarchy depth in **opposite directions** — communication cost (now **D39**) and information-acquisition cost (now **D40**). The measurability-of-output half stands exactly as written and keeps this number. Do not use the information-flow half of D25 on its own; use D39 and D40, which are separately measurable. The row is kept with its number so the change is visible rather than silent. **Refined [incentives-and-institutional-form, 2026-08-19].** The measurability half of this row treats "can a third party verify the output" as one question. Hart splits it into three properties that come apart in real relationships — see **D25a**, which supersedes the single-question phrasing above while leaving the three-component score intact as the operationalisation of the *verifiable* component. | promoted (measurability, as refined by D25a); information-flow half superseded by D39/D40 |
| D25a | **Contractibility vs. verifiability vs. mere observability** — three properties, not one. *Contractible*: can be specified in a binding agreement **ex ante**. *Verifiable*: can be proven to a third party (a court) **ex post**. *Merely observable*: both parties can see it, but no third party can be convinced. | Hart (Nobel lecture; Grossman & Hart 1986 for the underlying ex-ante-noncontractible/ex-post-contractible assumption) | Take a sample of the relationship's consequential decisions and score each on all three properties independently. Concrete probes: (i) *contractibility* — is the contingency named in the governing document, or would naming it have required foreseeing a state of the world nobody could enumerate? (ii) *verifiability* — has a dispute on this dimension ever been taken to an external adjudicator, and did the adjudicator rule on the substance? (iii) *observability* — do the parties act as though they both know, e.g. is it discussed in correspondence without either side disputing the facts? Hart's worked cases give the pattern: coal **ash content** is hard to specify ex ante but easy to observe and verify once realised; the power plant's **location** is contractible while whether it installs a boiler suited to *this* mine's coal is not; **cost `c`** in the Maskin-Tirole mechanism is observable but not verifiable. **The three do not move together, and which one binds determines the institutional form**: Aghion-Bolton assume returns are verifiable and transferable and get state-contingent equity-like control; Hart-Moore drop that (the manager can walk off with the cash) and get a debt contract instead. In the private-prison case, cost-cutting is specifiable and rewardable while use-of-force quality is neither. | candidate |
| D26 | **Information rules** — mandated channels and disclosure | Ostrom (rules-in-use type 4) | Enumerate what must be disclosed, to whom, on what cadence; then measure compliance rate rather than the rule alone. | candidate |

### Scale, time and duration

| # | Dimension | Named by | Operationalisation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D27 | **Scale of the transacting domain** — village → regional bazaar → long-distance → national → international | North (JEP; also names rising trade volume as a *cause* of institutional investment) | Geographic and counterparty reach of the typical transaction; share of transactions with non-repeat, non-kin counterparties. | candidate |
| D28 | **Organisational size** | Williamson (limits to firm size: bureaucratic ploys and political positioning rise with size); Ostrom (producer scale; small-to-medium cities monitor better; **empirical**: scale effects *invert* by service type — direct services benefit from many producers, indirect/support services from few) | Headcount, budget, and number of hierarchical layers — trivially measurable. ### Legitimacy and beliefs

| # | Dimension | Named by | Operationalisation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D31 | **Perceived legitimacy and equity** | Ostrom ("whether users consider the system to be legitimate and equitable" is named as one of the three things that actually explain forest condition) | Direct: survey the governed. Revealed: voluntary compliance rate in the absence of monitoring — the cleaner measure, since it is behavioural. | candidate |
| D32 | **Trust / expected reciprocity** | Ostrom (posited as the central mediating variable between situational structure and cooperation) | Behavioural elicitation via standardised cooperation games, or observed voluntary contribution rates. | D33 | **Mental models of decision-makers** | North (framework: institutional change is filtered through subjective and possibly incorrect mental models) | Partially measurable: code the stated rationales in decision documents against the actual payoff structure and score divergence. | D34 | **Adaptive efficiency** | North (framework, named in passing) | **No operationalisation available.** As stated, "the capacity of an institution to adapt well" is defined by the outcome it is meant to explain, and North supplies no independent indicator. Would be readmissible if restated as an observable — e.g. rate of rule revision per unit of measured performance shortfall, or elapsed time from a detected performance failure to a rule change. Recorded rather than dropped, per lint check 11. **Named a second time and still not operationalised [institutional-evolution, 2026-08-19].** North's 1993 Nobel lecture names the adaptive/allocative distinction again and makes it the stated payoff of his whole framework — "the key to long run growth" — then disclaims the lever in his own words: "we do not know how to create adaptive efficiency in the short run." No indicator, no measurement procedure, no threshold. **Deliberately not re-registered as a new row**: a second naming by the same author is not a second piece of evidence, and duplicating it would inflate the register while the admissibility problem is unchanged. The status stays `rejected` and the readmission condition above stands. | rejected |

### Internal structure of the organisation

*Added by the `scale-effects` ingest, 2026-08-19. These are the first axes here that describe the inside of one organisation rather than a rule-set, a transaction, or a field.*

| # | Dimension | Named by | Operationalisation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D35 | **Funding mechanism** — output exchanged for a lump-sum appropriation vs. sold at a per-unit price | Niskanen (the pivotal axis of his model, and his definition of a "bureau"; he notes post offices, universities and hospitals are *mixed* regardless of public or private ownership) | Share of the institution's revenue arriving as a periodic block appropriation or grant, against the share arriving per unit of output sold. Readable from any published budget. Score the mix, not the category — the interesting cases are mixed. | candidate |
| D36 | **Span of control** — direct reports per supervisor at a given level | Rajan & Wulf (**empirical**: CEO span 4.46 → 6.79 across 300+ US firms, 1986–1998; 4.39 → 8.16 in the balanced sub-panel); Simon (bounded by serial information processing — one conversation at a time); Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg (bounded by the manager's helping-time constraint, `1 ≥ h·n·(1−F(z))`); Klimek/Hanel/Thurner (r, subordinates granted per promotion) | Count direct reports per supervisor from the org chart, at a named level; report the distribution, not the mean, since it is heavy-tailed. Specify the level — CEO span and shop-floor span are different quantities. | promoted |
| D37 | **Hierarchy depth** — number of layers between the apex and the lowest independently-accountable unit | Rajan & Wulf (**empirical**: depth 1.58 → 1.18, 1986–1998; and, holding year fixed, positive in log employees — bigger firms are deeper); Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg (layer count L, endogenous, U-shaped cost function, minimum efficient scale rising in L); Simon (levels, the quantity assembly time scales with); Klimek/Hanel/Thurner (L, plus **flatness ΔL**, the number of levels below the promotion cutoff) | Count reporting layers between the apex decision-maker and the lowest position with its own budget or profit-and-loss. Also record **ΔL** (levels below the promotion ceiling): Klimek et al. find that past moderate depth, achievable average efficiency depends on ΔL and *not* on L or absolute size. Separate from D36 — span and depth are negatively correlated (−0.27 in Rajan & Wulf) but are independently manipulable. | promoted |
| D38 | **Coupling strength / decomposability** — ratio of between-subunit to within-subunit interaction | Simon (near-decomposability; the theorem in Simon & Ando 1961: short-run independence, long-run dependence only through aggregates) | Sample consequential interactions (meetings, approvals, transfers, escalations, shared dependencies) over a period and compute the ratio of cross-unit to within-unit interaction per subunit. Continuous, not binary; Simon's claim is that real systems cluster near — not at — full decomposability. Practical proxy: what fraction of a subunit's decisions require sign-off outside it. | candidate |
| D39 | **Communication cost** — the time or cost a knowledgeable agent spends fielding a question, incurred whether or not she can answer it | Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg (the "helping cost" h; a fall in h *increases* the number of layers) | Time per escalation, measured as the responder's hours consumed per escalated problem, including the ones where the escalation was fruitless. Proxy for organisations where this is not logged: latency and handling time on internal ticketing or approval queues. **Supersedes, with D40, the information-flow half of D25.** | candidate |
| D40 | **Information-acquisition cost** — the cost of an agent learning to solve a given class of problem herself | Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg (knowledge-acquisition cost c; a fall in c *reduces* layers, *widens* span, and shifts the mix toward production workers) | Cost and time to bring a worker to competence over a defined problem class — training hours, qualification time, or documented cost per certification. **Opposite sign to D39 on hierarchy depth**, which is the whole reason the two must be recorded separately. Supporting evidence: Bloom, Sadun, Garicano & Van Reenen's 1000-firm ICT survey, where ERP and CAD-CAM systems decentralise and network technologies centralise. | candidate |
| D41 | **Formal authority vs. reporting proximity** — is delegated authority the same thing as sitting close to the apex? | Rajan & Wulf (**empirical**: division managers who moved closer to the CEO also gained officer designation and more incentive-weighted pay, while their units *shrank* — ~6,000 to ~4,700 employees) | Two separate reads. (i) Reporting distance: layers between the position and the apex. (ii) Formal authority: does the position hold a designation (officer, statutory delegation, signing limit) that confers decision rights independent of the reporting line? Record the pair. The gap between them is the finding — Rajan & Wulf's reading is Aghion & Tirole's: a wide span is a credible commitment to *light monitoring*, paid for with formal authority and incentive pay instead of supervision. | candidate |

### The control and incentive environment

*Added by the `risk-aversion-in-large-institutions` ingest, 2026-08-19. These axes describe the web of external controllers, monitors, reviewers and contracts an organisation and its agents sit inside — as distinct from the organisation's own internal structure (D35–D41) or the rule-set it operates under (D1–D6). Every row here is (b): named by a source, tested against behaviour by none of them.*

| # | Dimension | Named by | Operationalisation | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D42 | **External-control density / layering** — how many entities outside the organisation promulgate rules binding on it, across how many jurisdictional layers | Bozeman (Propositions 1.0/1.1 and the rule-entropy mechanism: "the more organizations, organizational levels, and jurisdictions involved in rule promulgation and application, the more likely the meaning will be lost through entropy"); adjacent in Hood (density of the external scrutiny ecology — journalists, lobbyists, compensation lawyers, oversight bodies) | Two counts, reported as a pair. (i) **Entity count**: distinct external bodies with authority to issue rules the organisation must comply with — parent agency, political superiors, central management agencies (OMB-equivalents), auditors, regulators, interorganisational partners, funders with conditions. Enumerable from the compliance register or the organisation's own list of reporting obligations. (ii) **Layer count**: distinct jurisdictional levels those bodies sit at (municipal / state / national / supranational). Bozeman's mechanism runs on both separately, so do not collapse them into one number. Applies to private organisations too — Bozeman states the model is "as applicable to private as it is to government organizations", and his own cited data show government-dependent private bodies scoring high. | candidate |
| D43 | **Feedback-mechanism quality** — does a channel exist from the parties a rule burdens back to the parties who wrote it, and is it used? | Bozeman (Proposition 2.0: red tape decreases with the quality and use of feedback mechanisms; the private-sector market analogue is that red-tape complaints risk "reduced sales and market share", where in government "feedback is more likely to be anecdotal and episodic") | Existence is cheap and near-universal; **usage rate is the measure**. Count formal channels by which rule-affected parties can reach rule-makers, then measure the rate at which they are used and, better, the rate at which use produces a rule change — complaints filed per rule per year, and the share resulting in amendment or repeal. Market exit is the private-sector limiting case and should be scored on the same axis, not treated as a different thing. | candidate |
| D44 | **Veto-point count × preference-profile divergence** — the number of institutional actors able to block a policy change, *jointly with* how far apart their preferences are | Hammond (the paper's central result: comparing six systems pairwise, all 720 rank-orderings of the resulting discretion set are logically achievable, so count alone predicts nothing — "anything can happen") | **Deliberately registered as one composite axis, not two.** Hammond's point is that veto-point count is non-predictive on its own and that empirical work omitting the preference profile misattributes its results "erroneously, to just the impact of the institutional variables"; a count-only row here would reproduce exactly the error the source exists to warn against. | D46 | **Evaluation/review cadence** — frequency of performance review relative to the volume of decisions taken between reviews | Kahneman & Lovallo (formally: deferring evaluation across two plays rather than one drops the probability of an unfavourable review at the next checkpoint from 0.50 to 0.25, which changes whether a positive-EV gamble is accepted) | Ratio, not a raw frequency: count consequential decisions made per review cycle for a given role. ## What this register does not yet contain

**[wiki synthesis]** The gap is structural and worth stating, because it is the gap between this literature and what this wiki is for. The foundations-NIE sources supply axes describing *rule-sets, transactions, and resource regimes*. They supply almost nothing describing the **internal composition of a single organisation**. Specifically absent from all five sources:

- selection and promotion criteria for personnel (who ends up inside — [[open-questions]] Q4);
- mandate clarity and multiplicity of objectives;
- funding source and its stability;
- hierarchy layer count as a behavioural variable rather than a size proxy;
- age of the organisation as distinct from age of the rule-set (see [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]]);
- career incentives, tenure structures, and internal labour markets.

**Partly closed [scale-effects, 2026-08-19].** D35–D41 supply hierarchy depth and span as behavioural variables rather than size proxies, funding mechanism, coupling strength, the two information costs, and the authority/proximity gap — and Klimek/Hanel/Thurner supply promotion probability p, subordinates-per-promotion r and attrition γ as measurable parameters of a career-incentive regime (registered inside D36 and D37 rather than as their own rows, since they are parameters of the same structure). Two items on the list above are **not** closed, and one got worse:

- Personnel selection (Q4) is still named by nobody.

**Partly closed again [risk-aversion-in-large-institutions, 2026-08-19].** D42–D48 supply the control and incentive environment, and two items on the original absent-list move: **career incentives and tenure structures** are now named by Hood (tenure security and time-to-accountability-event as moderators of blame sensitivity, registered inside D45), Holmström (career-stage effects on effort, and D47) and Manso (D48). **Personnel selection (Q4) moves from "named by nobody" to "named once, badly"**: the OECD reports a Canadian "clay layer" in which "innovative ideas are not evaluated by their merit, but by the personal characteristics and social capital involved" of middle managers. That is a selection-and-gatekeeping claim, it is exactly the axis Q4 asks about — and it rests on one country scan in an advocacy document with undeveloped coding protocols. Not enough for a row. Q4 stays open.
- **Organisational age is still supplied by no source in this wiki, after three literatures.** The scale-effects batch looks like an age literature and is not one: every age-flavoured claim in it turns out to be individual career tenure (Klimek), technology vintage (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg), evolutionary formation time (Simon), or an aside the author's own footnote disowns (Niskanen). Rajan & Wulf report firm age only as a sample statistic (~85 years) and never use it as a covariate. **The `institutional-evolution` batch was commissioned as the place age evidence would appear, and does not supply it either.** Arthur's clock is adoption count *n* with an express warning against reading it as calendar years (D58, `rejected`); North's durations are post-hoc narrative with no dose-response; Skocpol's counts, the batch's only real longitudinal data, measure organisational *survival* rather than behavioural change with age; DiMaggio & Powell's age-flavoured claims are about *field* maturity (D57), which is a different variable; Meyer & Rowan supply nothing on age or size and say so; Krasner's own cited periodisation undercuts his phase framing; and Thelen argues directly that contestation begins as soon as a rule is laid down, which would remove the phase structure an age-gated model needs. **Three batches, no measurement of institutional behaviour against elapsed time since founding.** See [[open-questions]] Q7 and Q39.

**Partly closed a third time, and one item genuinely moves [incentives-and-institutional-form, 2026-08-19].** D49–D51 supply **mandate clarity and multiplicity of objectives** — the second item on the original absent-list — in two operational forms rather than one: D51 counts the principals whose objectives the mandate is trying to hold together, and D49 records whether those objectives compete for the same effort. Dixit's own limiting condition on implicit incentives makes the pair load-bearing: career concerns work best "with a narrow, clear mission," which is precisely what a high D51 with `k > 0` denies.

**Personnel selection (Q4) now has measurement, but still no axis.** Prendergast supplies the first real decomposition in this wiki of selection against incentive effects: of Lazear's 35% productivity gain from a piece-rate switch, roughly **one third is selection** (turnover data show less able workers leaving and more able ones replacing them), and Paarsch & Shearer's structural estimate isolates the incentive effect on a *given* worker at ~10% against a raw 35% gap. Prendergast states the general point himself — "the selection effects appear to be of roughly equal size to the incentive effects, despite the overwhelming focus on incentive effects in the theoretical literature" — and Weiss/Hansen show the selection can be **U-shaped** rather than monotonic under team pay. What this does **not** give the register is an *axis*: it establishes that the reward structure is itself a selection instrument, in two occupations with unusually clean output measures, but nobody names a measurable property of an institution's selection regime. Two occupations is not a dimension. **Q4 stays open**, now with evidence attached rather than none. See [[multitask-incentive-theory]].

D28 is measurable but currently unlinked to any tested prediction; the rest are not in the register because no source here names them. They should be the first things a later ingest of organisational-theory or bureaucratic-politics sources adds.

**Second-hand marking.** Where the Named-by column cites Libecap, the entry draws on **his own** oil, fisheries, and groundwater case work. Libecap's summaries of North elsewhere in that paper are second-hand and are not used as a naming source in this register.
