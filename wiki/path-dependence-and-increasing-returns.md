# Path Dependence and Increasing Returns

North's claim is that institutional matrices exhibit increasing returns, so early institutional choices self-reinforce and lock in trajectories: "yesterday's institutional framework provides the opportunity set for today's organizations." The mechanism has four named components (setup costs, learning effects, coordination effects, adaptive expectations) plus a political feedback loop that is the load-bearing part for this wiki: organisations that arose under the existing rules have a stake in perpetuating them, and the ideologies of the people running them rationalise the matrix that produced them. The direct implication is that an institutional matrix becomes *less* flexible the longer it has been in place, because organisations and mental models have had more time to co-adapt to it. Change is therefore "overwhelmingly incremental"; the exception is gridlock, where the absence of mediating institutions makes accumulated pressure break out as abrupt formal-rule change — but informal constraints lag, and the settled outcome is "far less revolutionary than the rhetoric."

**That last implication is now contested inside this wiki, not merely unevidenced.** The `institutional-evolution` batch adds the formal ancestor of the mechanism (Arthur, on technology markets), North's own cognitive microfoundations and duration catalogue, Pierson's politics-specific mechanisms with the batch's one real dataset — and Thelen, who accepts positive feedback and denies that it implies faithful reproduction of the original design, arguing that contestation over a rule begins as soon as the rule is laid down rather than after some interval of maturity. Read the sections below in order; the page no longer concludes what its first paragraph asserts.

## The four increasing-returns mechanisms

**[model]** From the JEP essay, applied to institutional frameworks by analogy with technology:

1. **Setup / fixed costs** — the cost of establishing the rule-set is sunk, so average cost falls as it is used more.
2. **Learning effects** — actors get better at operating within a given rule-set over time.
3. **Coordination effects** — the value of following a rule rises with the number of others following it (network externality).
4. **Adaptive expectations** — the more prevalent a rule, the more actors expect it to persist and plan accordingly.

The framework paper restates these as "economies of scope, complementarities, and network externalities" that bias the costs and benefits of any prospective change toward choices consistent with the existing framework.

## The formal ancestor: Arthur's technology-adoption model

**[model — formal, with proofs; technology markets only]** The four mechanisms above are North's restatement, for institutions, of a result Arthur proved for something else. **The scope limit has to travel with the citation, because Arthur states it himself.** The words "institution", "organisation", "agency", "bureaucracy" and "governance" do not appear anywhere in his paper. His agents are consumers and developers choosing between light-water and gas-cooled reactors, QWERTY and its rivals, petrol and steam engines. There is no organisation, no firm-as-governance-structure, no political institution in the formal apparatus, and no meso level at all between the individual choice and the market-share statistic. He is cited here **for the stochastic-process machinery only. The institutional extension is North's move and Pierson's, and it needs their sourcing, not his.**

What the model establishes, and it is proved rather than asserted (Theorems I–III, with full proofs):

- **Three regimes.** Under constant or diminishing returns to adoption, the adoption-count difference between two technologies is a free or reflecting random walk that reverts to a 50/50 split with probability one, whatever the history. Under **increasing** returns it is a random walk with **absorbing barriers**: once the lead crosses a threshold, new agents of *both* natural-preference types switch to the leader — including agents who intrinsically prefer the loser — because accumulated improvement outweighs innate preference. Predictability, flexibility, ergodicity and path-efficiency all fail in that regime and hold in the other two.
- **Increasing returns is necessary but not sufficient.** If the per-adoption improvement function is *bounded* — learning effects exhaust — Theorem III's dominance guarantee fails: some histories still produce dominance, others produce a permanently shared market, because two technologies can hit the improvement ceiling together.
- **Expectations are self-fulfilling.** If agents believe the market will lock in, the switching barriers narrow or collapse and lock-in arrives faster. Expected inevitability makes inevitability arrive sooner.
- **Reversal cost rises without bound.** Arthur's own operational definition of "flexibility" is the size of the subsidy needed to shift the absorbing barriers back, and it "increases without bound" as adoption accumulates.
- **Strategic sponsorship can reopen sharing.** Citing Hanson, sponsored (firm-owned) technologies under strong discounting may prefer immediate sales to predatory exclusion, so market sharing can reappear even under increasing returns. Firm-level strategy partially offsets the pure technical lock-in tendency.

**The clock is *n*, the count of adoption events — not calendar time.** Agents arrive at real times, but the random walk, the barriers and all three theorems are indexed by the number of prior choices. Arthur's one concrete quantified threshold is in those units: in the illustrative homogeneous-agent example, after **30 of 100** adoption events have gone to technology A, equivalent adoption of B would have delivered higher returns, but a given bounded subsidy to B "can no longer close the gap". He is equally explicit that this is not a moment — flexibility degrades **continuously and asymptotically**, and the barrier is crossed with probability one only as *n* → ∞. There is no instant of lock-in in the model. Where he uses calendar years at all (the Nautilus reactor in 1954, petrol beating steam "by 1920", British rail gauge) they are narrative colour drawn from secondary case histories he did not write, and he reports the counterfactuals as **contested**: "whether steam might have been superior given equal development is still in dispute among engineers", and much of the engineering literature holds that the gas-cooled reactor would have been superior given equal development. The wiki should not flatten those into settled lock-in cases.

**[wiki synthesis]** So the most-cited formal source behind "institutions get harder to change over time" contains **no dose-response between elapsed calendar time and degree of lock-in**, and no institution. A claim of the form "institutions lock in after N years" cannot be sourced here; at most "after a large enough fraction of adoption events", in a technology-choice setting. The reframing — reversal cost rising in *cumulative adoption cycles* rather than in years — is registered at [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D58** and **rejected**, because institutions supply no counter analogous to *n*: rules are continuously re-enacted by overlapping cohorts of actors with renegotiable authority, where Arthur's agents choose once and are frozen thereafter. The readmission condition is recorded there. See also [[open-questions]] Q7 and Q45.

**Falsifiability, precisely.** The model yields a closed-form ex ante *probability* that A rather than B wins, as a function of the returns parameters — so it is predictive about aggregate statistical structure. Arthur then says outright that it cannot predict which technology wins in a given history: "the observer... cannot predict the actual market-share outcome with any accuracy — in spite of his knowledge of supply and demand conditions. Predictability is lost." **A single observed lock-in therefore neither confirms nor falsifies the model** — this is exactly the "any persistence can be called lock-in" vulnerability, and Arthur's model does not escape it at the level of individual histories. What *is* testable, independent of any particular outcome: (i) Theorem III's structural condition on measured learning curves — does the improvement function grow at least at rate 1/*n*? (ii) the regime signature — an observed lock-in under provably constant or diminishing returns would falsify; (iii) monotonicity of reversal cost — a late-stage subsidy trivially reversing an entrenched technology would be evidence against the flexibility claim. This distinction between *structurally predictive* and *outcome-explanatory* is the sharpest tool this batch supplies for reading path-dependence claims generally, and it applies to every other source on this page.

**Also worth taking, and also not institutional:** Arthur names a **missing-market** failure — "early adopters impose externalities on later ones by rationally choosing technologies to suit only themselves; missing is an inter-agent market to induce them to explore promising but costly infant technologies" — and a **bandit-abandonment** failure, in which a central authority backing a promising-but-unpopular option "faces a classic multi-arm bandit problem" and may rationally quit after an early run of bad results. Both are premature-exploitation failures at policy level, structurally similar to and distinct from the individual-contract version in [[exploration-exploitation-incentive-contracts]].

## The political feedback loop

**[model]** This is the part with no technology analogue, and it is the mechanism that matters for institutional decay:

> Organisations that came into existence under the existing rules have a stake in perpetuating them → they invest in political influence to defend them → entrepreneurs' ideologies rationalise the existing matrix → the matrix persists → it continues to select for organisations adapted to it.

North is explicit that this is mutually reinforcing feedback, not correlation. He is equally explicit that the resulting bias is *distributional*, not efficiency-seeking: change is biased "in favor of the interests of the existing organizations" regardless of aggregate efficiency. In the framework paper he names the victims directly — institutions "exploited individuals and groups — Indians and slaves, and not infrequently immigrants, workers, and farmers — to the benefit of those with superior bargaining power."

**[wiki synthesis]** Note what this does to the efficiency question. Path dependence is not a friction that slows convergence to a good equilibrium; on North's account it is a *selection mechanism* that can hold a bad equilibrium in place indefinitely, because the actors with the most influence over the rules are precisely those the rules have already advantaged. This is the strongest form of North's rejection of the efficiency presumption — see [[efficiency-of-institutions-north-vs-williamson]].

## How change happens anyway

**[model]** North's model of institutional change: entrepreneurs — decision-makers inside organisations — perceive an opportunity, either from an external shift in relative prices or tastes, or from learning that alters their *mental models*. They compare the gains from recontracting within the existing framework against the gains from altering the framework. If altering it is worth the cost, they invest resources in changing formal rules (statutory, judicial, regulatory, constitutional) or, more slowly, informal constraints, which shift gradually and "subconsciously."

Three features of this model are worth keeping:

- **Change is filtered through subjective and possibly incorrect mental models.** North relaxes perfect information and unlimited cognitive capability; institutional change need not track real payoffs, only perceived ones.
- **Formal and informal rules change at different speeds.** "Formal rules may change over night, but informal constraints do not." The tension between a new formal rule and an unchanged informal constraint is resolved by a new equilibrium that is less radical than either.
- **Gridlock is the trigger for non-incremental change.** North defines it as "a lack of mediating institutions that enable conflicting parties to reach compromises." Where such institutions exist, pressure is discharged incrementally; where they do not, it accumulates and ruptures.

**[wiki synthesis]** The gridlock mechanism is functionally adjacent to veto-point proliferation, but North does not use that term and does not measure it. Flagged as a mapping the wiki is making, not a claim the source supports.

## North's cognitive mechanism: mental models and collective learning

**[framework — the 1993 Nobel lecture; tests nothing]** The lecture supplies the machinery underneath the JEP essay's model. What generates path dependence, on this account, is not the rule-set alone but the **mental models** through which actors read it, and the cultural transmission of those models across generations. North adopts Hayek's "collective learning" — culture as "the transmission in time of our accumulated stock of knowledge" — and names it **"the key to path dependence"**. Belief systems filter new experience through existing categories, so a society facing a genuinely novel problem of complexity may fail to recognise it and gets **"stuck"**. North's own summary is that most societies throughout history got stuck: stability is the modal outcome and growth is the exception, requiring a specific competitive, fragmented, belief-evolving context.

Two further pieces are worth keeping:

- **Where new institutional ideas come from.** "Representational redescription" (Clark & Karmiloff-Smith) — the cognitive capacity to abstract from particular experience to general, analogical models — is named as the source of both creative thought and new ideologies. It is the most novel machinery in the lecture and it is asserted, not evidenced.
- **Competition is the change mechanism, monopoly the stasis mechanism.** "The greater the degree of monopoly power the lower the incentive to learn." Organisational learning is driven by competition among organisations; where a matrix suppresses that competition it also suppresses the perception on which change depends.

**[wiki synthesis]** Note what routing the mechanism through beliefs does to testability. The causal variable is now unobserved. [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D33 registers mental models as an axis and can only offer a stated-rationale proxy — coding decision documents against the actual payoff structure — which measures stated belief, not held belief.

Note also what the lecture does **not** add. Its two organisation-level statements — that a matrix rewarding piracy produces piratical organisations, and that "political institutions will be stable only if undergirded by organizations with a stake in their perpetuation" — are bare assertions with no case, citation or mechanism beyond the claim itself. They restate the political feedback loop above; they supply no independent evidence for it. The wiki has one source for that loop, not two.

## The duration catalogue — narrative, not thresholds

**[narrative — post-hoc historical illustration, explicitly not measurement]** The lecture names several intervals, and they are the closest thing to age evidence anywhere in this literature. They are listed so the wiki is not tempted to reuse them as parameters.

| Interval | North's claim | What it actually is |
|---|---|---|
| ~500 years | The hiatus between the end of the Roman Empire in the west and the revival of Western Europe | The one *actually measured* interval in the piece, offered as proof that institutional change "has not been unidirectional" |
| ~400 years | Habsburg Spain's sixteenth-century height to "its sorry state under Franco" — used to show that rulers *knowing* performance is bad ("endless self-appraisals and frequently bizarre proposed solutions") does not reverse it | A continuous-decline narrative. **No point of no return is identified**; it does not read as critical-juncture-then-lock |
| ~800 years | Western Europe "from relative backwardness in the tenth century to world economic hegemony by the eighteenth", attributed to a gradually evolving belief system under fragmented, competitive polities | No account of why 800 years, and no marker for when the trajectory became irreversible |
| ~250 years | Modern economic growth, of which "largely restricted to Western Europe and the overseas extensions of Britain for 200 of those 250 years" | A named sub-interval, and the most specific claim in the lecture |
| ~10,000 years | Trial-and-error societal learning since the first economic revolution, which by 1993 "has still escaped the grasp of almost half of the world's population" | No interval boundaries at all |

**The finding, stated plainly: North converts none of these into a causal threshold.** There is no claim anywhere of the form "after N years, reversal probability falls to X". They are descriptions of what happened in cases selected to fit the thesis, with no counterfactual and no comparison case that failed to fit. Nor is there a dose-response — Spain declines continuously rather than crossing a lock-in point, and the European rise has no irreversibility marker.

**North names no falsification condition for lock-in, anywhere.** No condition is stated under which path dependence would fail to occur, and no ex ante predictor is given for which societies escape a stuck matrix (Netherlands, England) and which decline inside one from a similar regional starting position (Spain, Portugal). The difference is attributed to "diverse external environmental experiences" — a relabelling of the outcome, not an independent predictor. This is precisely the unfalsifiability charge against path dependence, and this source does not answer it. Pierson, below, concedes the same point about his own framework; Krasner's version of the problem is on [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]]. **Three of this batch's sources carry the same defect, independently.**

**The lecture's one hard number is not about path dependence.** Wallis & North (1986) measured the US "transaction sector" at **45% of GNP in 1970**. It is a real measurement of transaction-cost magnitude and it belongs to [[transaction-costs]]; it evidences nothing about lock-in. It is noted here because it is one of only two genuine figures in the whole batch, the other being Skocpol's counts below.

**Adaptive efficiency, and why no new axis follows.** North names adaptive efficiency — a system's flexibility and survivability under shocks — against allocative efficiency, and calls it "the key to long run growth", then disclaims the lever: **"we do not know how to create adaptive efficiency in the short run."** The wiki already registered this axis from the framework paper as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D34** and rejected it, because as stated it is defined by the outcome it is meant to explain. The lecture names it a second time and supplies no indicator, so **D34 is not re-registered**; the note there records that a second North source named the axis and still offered no operationalisation. A second naming is not a second piece of evidence.

## The worked case: US land policy

**[empirical — single narrative case, traceable]** The framework paper's illustration is the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 and the century of land policy after it, with named actors (Rev. Manassah Cutler, Nathan Dane, Rufus King). The traceable sequence: the Ordinance's disposal provisions → claims clubs emerged "to thwart competitive bidding" → squatters won a general preemption act → the minimum saleable land unit fell repeatedly → the Homestead Act gave land away free. Each step is a rent-seeking adaptation that then constrains the next step. This is the essay's strongest evidence; the accompanying general claims about "third world countries" and socialist economies are conclusions asserted from the framework rather than tested.

## Lock-in measured: how long agreement takes

**[empirical — Libecap's own case work, not second-hand]** Libecap supplies a quantified analogue at the level of a single resource regime. His repeated pattern: agreement on efficient property rights becomes likely only *after* the number and heterogeneity of parties has shrunk through attrition — that is, only after most of the rents have already been dissipated. Evidence: oil-field unitization deals took 4–9 years to negotiate across seven Texas fields; full ITQ systems in fisheries were adopted only "when fish stocks are near collapse"; the same wait-for-attrition dynamic appears in California groundwater basins.

**[wiki synthesis]** This is the same lock-in claim as North's, measured with a different instrument: not "the matrix persists" but "the correction arrives N years late and after X% of the value is gone." It is a considerably more useful form of the claim, because *time-to-agreement* and *rent dissipated before agreement* are both measurable. See [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]].

## Pierson: why politics should lock in harder than markets

**[framework — theoretical/review essay, one dataset]** Pierson (2000) transposes the increasing-returns argument from economics to politics. His contribution is that he refuses to let "history matters" stand as self-explanatory: he decomposes it into four distinct **mechanisms of reproduction** (the term is Collier & Collier's), three of which have no clean market analogue.

1. **Collective action.** Jointness of supply, non-excludability, and lumpy winner-take-all payoffs, plus high organising start-up costs, mean that once a coalition, party or movement crosses a mobilisation threshold it self-reinforces — "pick the right horse" adaptive expectations do the rest. Lipset & Rokkan's "frozen" party systems; the Skocpol data below.
2. **Institutional density.** Formal institutions and public policies are legally binding, apply even to those who did not consent, and — unlike markets — offer **no exit option**. Skills, investments and identities built around them raise exit costs over time. This is North's argument extended.
3. **Political authority as a power amplifier.** Actors who hold rule-making authority can rewrite the rules to entrench their own advantage (Mahoney). Small initial resource asymmetries widen through feedback, and the exercise of power becomes simultaneously **more unequal and less visible**, as raw imposition gives way to anticipated reactions and ideological framing (his route through Bachrach & Baratz, Lukes and Gaventa). **This mechanism has no analogue in Arthur or North.**
4. **Complexity and opacity.** Politics has no price-like performance metric and long, ambiguous causal chains from action to outcome, so actors filter evidence into path-dependent mental maps — confirming information kept, disconfirming filtered out. Learning is possible but unreliable as a corrective.

His net claim is **same mechanism family, systematically higher intensity in politics**, and it is argued rather than asserted. Three correctives that ameliorate path dependence in markets are weak or absent: **competition** (political institutions "rarely confront a dense environment of competing institutions" ready to exploit inefficiency), **learning** (blocked by the opacity above), and **far-sighted capital and property-rights markets** that let private actors capture long-run returns and so correct myopic choices. Two intensifiers have no clean market counterpart: **short time horizons** tied to election cycles, and **deliberately engineered status-quo bias** — supermajority and unanimity requirements (US constitutional amendment, EU unanimity) adopted precisely to bind rivals and successors. Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D54.

**[wiki synthesis]** This is a **public/private divergence** claim with a stated mechanism, which is rare in this wiki — most of the register's public/private material is either silence or assertion. It is also untested: the whole comparison is argued by plausibility, not measured. See [[open-questions]] Q3.

### The Skocpol dataset

**[empirical — the batch's only systematic longitudinal dataset, reported secondhand]** Skocpol (1999) tracked every US voluntary association that ever enrolled more than 1% of the population (0.5% for single-gender groups): **58 groups since the 1830s**. Of the **40 founded before 1900**:

- **19** stayed above the 1% threshold for at least five decades;
- **10** were still above it **a century or more after founding.**

Pierson's gloss: "despite massive social, economic, and political changes over time, self-reinforcing dynamics associated with collective action processes mean that organizations have a strong tendency to persist once they are institutionalized."

**[wiki synthesis — this is the wiki's best age datum and it does not measure what the wiki wants]** It is **survival data**. It records whether an organisation still cleared a membership threshold; it records nothing about whether the organisation *behaved* differently at year 100 than at year 10. There is no rigidity measure, no structural measure, no behavioural measure, and no base rate for young associations over a comparable interval to compare against. It establishes persistence, which nobody doubted. It does not establish rigidification, which is what the age hypothesis claims. Ten of forty is also, read the other way, a **75% attrition rate** over a century — a fact the source does not dwell on.

Pierson makes no headcount, budget or hierarchy claim anywhere; his essay is silent on organisational *size* and speaks only to temporal *persistence*. His own theoretical claim is systemic — "path dependent processes will often be most powerful not at the level of individual organizations or institutions but at a more macro level" — while his illustrations slide between levels: Skocpol at organisation level, central-bank design at single-institution level, varieties of capitalism at polity level. Do not read the organisation-level illustrations as validating an organisation-level general claim.

### Pierson's own concession that the framework is hard to falsify

**[the source's own words — record this prominently]** Pierson supplies a **scope** condition and not a **falsification** condition, and he says so.

- The scope condition is Arthur's four generative conditions imported wholesale — large set-up costs, learning effects, coordination effects, adaptive expectations. Where none applies, increasing returns should not be expected. He never operationalises this into a per-case checklist; it stays at "these features are common in politics", argued by analogy.
- He concedes the method problem directly, **citing Geddes (1997)**: the "many variables, few cases" problem is *worsened* for path-dependent claims, because one must evaluate sequences of several variables over time rather than a single cross-section. His proposed fixes are methodological rather than evidentiary — work in domains with many cases, lean on counterfactual analysis (Tetlock & Belkin), focus on observable *intermediate* stages rather than only start and end states — and he grants that even careful designs "may well be inadequate for anything but fairly simple increasing returns arguments."
- On critical junctures he comes close to conceding the post-hoc charge outright: such junctures "are usually attributed, often ex post, to 'exogenous shocks'".
- He does reject the permanent-freeze strawman, quoting North: "It is not a story of inevitability in which the past neatly predicts the future." Change continues but is bounded until something "erodes or swamps" the mechanisms of reproduction — with no measurable threshold for either verb.
- He also denies universal applicability inside politics — "Not all aspects of political life are subject to increasing returns" — without giving a checklist for identifying the exempt parts.

**Net: the theory predicts persistence-until-disrupted without predicting when disruption occurs.** No statement of the form "if X is observed, increasing-returns theory is wrong" appears anywhere in the essay. **This is the third source in this batch, independently, to have no falsification condition** — after North above and before Krasner on [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]]. He is the only one of the three who admits it.

### Three routes out of lock-in, per Pierson

1. **Critical junctures / exogenous shocks** that disrupt or overwhelm the mechanisms of reproduction — the standard historical-institutionalist route, and identified only in retrospect.
2. **Coercive governmental "jumps".** Politics uniquely concentrates coercive authority, so a government can occasionally orchestrate a coordinated simultaneous switch that a decentralised market could not — his example is the UK's metric conversion. He scopes it hard: this works where the underlying problem is *coordination* with **low switching costs**, not where switching costs are genuinely high, and "cases of fundamental or revolutionary reform in well-institutionalized political systems attract our attention precisely because they are so rare."
3. **Erosion or swamping over time** — the reproduction mechanisms weaken or are overwhelmed by accumulating conditions with no single identifiable shock. The vaguest of the three, and given no operational definition.

**[wiki synthesis]** Pierson's frame is binary: reproduce the path, or have it broken by a juncture. He gives **no account of gradual, endogenous change** of the layering/drift/conversion kind. That absence is what the next section, and [[gradual-institutional-change]], are about.

## Reconciliation: positive feedback does not imply faithful reproduction

**[framework — a direct challenge to this page's own framing, and it is sustained]** Thelen accepts positive feedback and denies the inference this page opened with. Her statement is explicit: feedback effects "are not associated with the faithful reproduction of the system as originally constituted"; institutions "created for one set of purposes and resting on the shoulders of one set of actors could be carried forward on the shoulders of another coalition altogether." Positive feedback keeps a rule alive. It does not keep the rule doing what it was built to do, and it does not entail that the rule becomes harder to change.

Her worked instance: the German vocational training system, legislated in **1897** by an authoritarian government together with reactionary artisan guilds *specifically to weaken organised labour*, and by the late twentieth century the institutional core of union–management social partnership. A century of formal continuity, with a complete reversal of whose interests it serves. The full account is on [[gradual-institutional-change]].

**The age-gating premise is denied directly.** Conflicts over the interpretation and enforcement of a rule "often begin **as soon as a rule is laid down**". On that account contestation runs continuously from founding, not after an interval of maturity — so there is no ossification curve to measure, because the process that would generate one is running at full strength from year zero. She also supplies a mechanism running the *other* way on elapsed time: as designers die or lose power while the rule persists, and the context around a fixed rule changes, **interpretive space that did not exist at founding opens up**. Time makes rules more available for capture and reinterpretation, not less.

**What this page changes as a result:**

- The claim that "an institutional matrix becomes *less* flexible the longer it has been in place" remains on the page as **North's position, attributed** — not as the page's conclusion. It is now contested by a source in the wiki rather than merely unevidenced.
- **Formal persistence is not evidence of functional persistence.** Skocpol's survival counts, above, measure the wrong thing on this account. The two are separately measurable and routinely decouple; registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D55**.

**[wiki synthesis]** North and Thelen are not contradicting each other on evidence, because neither measures the quantity in dispute. North asserts declining flexibility from historical narrative; Thelen asserts continuous contestation from case narrative. What separates them is a testable claim — **does the success rate of reversal attempts fall with elapsed time since founding?** — that no source in this wiki has measured. Filed as [[rival-models-of-institutional-change]] and [[open-questions]] Q39.

## What these sources do *not* establish

**[wiki synthesis — gap]** Every age and lock-in claim on this page is at the level of the **institutional matrix** — a whole society's rule-set — or of a resource regime. None of these sources makes any claim about a *single organisation* becoming more rigid as it ages. North's JEP essay is explicitly silent on headcount, hierarchy layers, or bureaucratic growth stages; the framework paper likewise. Ostrom makes no ossification-with-age claim at all: her design principles were derived cross-sectionally, contrasting long-surviving institutions against failed ones, with longevity as the *dependent* variable rather than a treatment. Williamson's scale argument (see [[governance-structures]]) is about size, not age, and is structural rather than empirical.

So the intuition that institutions ossify with age is, on this evidence, an inference by analogy from the matrix level to the organisational level. It is a good hypothesis and it is not yet supported. Logged as [[open-questions]] Q20.

**[empirical — soft counter-datapoint, added by the `scale-effects` ingest]** The first evidence bearing on the organisational reading points *against* it, mildly. Rajan & Wulf's panel is 300+ large US public firms, 1986–1998, averaging ~85 years since founding and ~47,500 employees — old, large and, in the authors' words, "not one whose organizational structure is likely to be in flux." Over thirteen years these firms restructured substantially: CEO span of control rose 50% in the full panel and 86% in the balanced sub-panel, hierarchy depth fell more than 25%, the probability that a division manager reported directly to the CEO went from 0.05 to 0.31, and the COO role was eliminated in about a fifth of the firms that had one. This happened while employment was flat or falling, so it is not growth forcing the change.

Weigh it carefully in both directions. It is a real observation of old organisations changing their own structure without an external shock large enough to be named — which bears on [[open-questions]] Q8 as well as Q20. But: it is senior management structure only, not rules, culture or mission; it is a period (post-takeover-wave US corporates) where external pressure was unusually high, and Rajan & Wulf explicitly do not identify the cause; the sample is old by construction, so there is no young-firm comparison and **age is never a covariate** in their regressions. It does not refute the ossification hypothesis. It does mean the hypothesis cannot be treated as obvious.

**[model — a parallel mechanism, different clock]** Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's dynamic extension supplies the closest thing in this wiki to a firm-level lock-in mechanism: an organisation that has thoroughly exploited its current technology may rationally postpone adopting a superior one indefinitely, because the new technology renders the accumulated hierarchy obsolete and a replacement must be built from scratch. Sunk organisational capital, not sunk political interest — the same shape as North's increasing returns at a different unit of analysis. It runs on **time since the current technology was adopted**, not time since founding, and it is a modelled result with no empirical test. Do not read it as age evidence.

**[wiki synthesis — the second batch, and the position is worse than "unsupported"]** The `institutional-evolution` batch was commissioned as the place age evidence would finally appear. It largely does not, and one source argues against the premise.

- **Arthur's clock is adoption count**, with an express warning that no dose-response connects elapsed calendar time to degree of lock-in. His only quantified threshold, 30 of 100 adoptions, is a technology-market result.
- **North's durations are post-hoc narrative** — 500 years, 400 years, 800 years — with no threshold, no dose-response, and no falsification condition anywhere in the lecture.
- **Skocpol's dataset measures survival, not behavioural change with age.** It is the batch's only real longitudinal data and it answers a different question.
- **Pierson concedes his own framework is hard to falsify**, citing Geddes, and gives no account of gradual endogenous change at all.
- **Thelen argues contestation is continuous from founding**, which if true removes the phase structure an age-gated model requires.
- **DiMaggio & Powell** supply a repeated cross-study claim that predictors of structure shift from technical to legitimacy-based as a *field* matures — but zero measured intervals originate in their own text, and it is field lifetime, not organisational age. See [[institutional-isomorphism]].
- **Meyer & Rowan** supply nothing on age or size, and say so themselves. See [[institutional-myths-and-decoupling]].
- **Krasner's own cited case data undercut his framing**: Skowronek's "punctuation" (1900–1920, twenty years) is not shorter than his "stasis" (1877–1900, twenty-three). See [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]].

**Three separate literatures have now been searched for age evidence** — the size literature, the risk-aversion literature, and this one — and none has supplied a measurement of institutional behaviour against elapsed time since founding. That is a finding about the field, recorded at [[open-questions]] Q7 with the study design that would settle it.

**An unresolved tension worth flagging, not adjudicating.** Pierson's and Skocpol's century-old voluntary associations persist largely unchanged; Rajan & Wulf's old, large firms restructured substantially over thirteen years. Both are real observations of old organisations. The obvious reconciliation is sector — membership associations against public corporations under takeover pressure — and no source says so. The two also measure different things: Skocpol measures whether the organisation still exists at threshold size, Rajan & Wulf measure whether its internal structure changed. An organisation can score "persisted" on the first and "transformed" on the second simultaneously, which is exactly Thelen's point about form and function decoupling. Logged as [[open-questions]] Q44.
