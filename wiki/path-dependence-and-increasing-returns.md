# Path Dependence and Increasing Returns

North's claim is that institutional matrices exhibit increasing returns, so early institutional choices self-reinforce and lock in trajectories: "yesterday's institutional framework provides the opportunity set for today's organizations." The mechanism has four named components (setup costs, learning effects, coordination effects, adaptive expectations) plus a political feedback loop that is the load-bearing part for this wiki: organisations that arose under the existing rules have a stake in perpetuating them, and the ideologies of the people running them rationalise the matrix that produced them. The direct implication is that an institutional matrix becomes *less* flexible the longer it has been in place, because organisations and mental models have had more time to co-adapt to it. Change is therefore "overwhelmingly incremental"; the exception is gridlock, where the absence of mediating institutions makes accumulated pressure break out as abrupt formal-rule change — but informal constraints lag, and the settled outcome is "far less revolutionary than the rhetoric."

**Evidence tier note.** (b) theoretical framework, illustrated by institutional history. North's increasing-returns mechanism is imported from Arthur and David on technology and asserted for institutions; the essay tests nothing. The one substantial case is the Northwest Ordinance sequence, which is specific and traceable. Libecap's delay findings, cited below, are (a) empirical case evidence and are his own primary work, not second-hand North. The comparative claims about Spain and Latin America are asserted in the JEP essay and offloaded to North & Thomas (1973) — treat them as North's framing, not as demonstrated.

## The four increasing-returns mechanisms

**[framework]** From the JEP essay, applied to institutional frameworks by analogy with technology:

1. **Setup / fixed costs** — the cost of establishing the rule-set is sunk, so average cost falls as it is used more.
2. **Learning effects** — actors get better at operating within a given rule-set over time.
3. **Coordination effects** — the value of following a rule rises with the number of others following it (network externality).
4. **Adaptive expectations** — the more prevalent a rule, the more actors expect it to persist and plan accordingly.

The framework paper restates these as "economies of scope, complementarities, and network externalities" that bias the costs and benefits of any prospective change toward choices consistent with the existing framework.

## The political feedback loop

**[framework]** This is the part with no technology analogue, and it is the mechanism that matters for institutional decay:

> Organisations that came into existence under the existing rules have a stake in perpetuating them → they invest in political influence to defend them → entrepreneurs' ideologies rationalise the existing matrix → the matrix persists → it continues to select for organisations adapted to it.

North is explicit that this is mutually reinforcing feedback, not correlation. He is equally explicit that the resulting bias is *distributional*, not efficiency-seeking: change is biased "in favor of the interests of the existing organizations" regardless of aggregate efficiency. In the framework paper he names the victims directly — institutions "exploited individuals and groups — Indians and slaves, and not infrequently immigrants, workers, and farmers — to the benefit of those with superior bargaining power."

**[wiki synthesis]** Note what this does to the efficiency question. Path dependence is not a friction that slows convergence to a good equilibrium; on North's account it is a *selection mechanism* that can hold a bad equilibrium in place indefinitely, because the actors with the most influence over the rules are precisely those the rules have already advantaged. This is the strongest form of North's rejection of the efficiency presumption — see [[efficiency-of-institutions-north-vs-williamson]].

## How change happens anyway

**[framework]** North's model of institutional change: entrepreneurs — decision-makers inside organisations — perceive an opportunity, either from an external shift in relative prices or tastes, or from learning that alters their *mental models*. They compare the gains from recontracting within the existing framework against the gains from altering the framework. If altering it is worth the cost, they invest resources in changing formal rules (statutory, judicial, regulatory, constitutional) or, more slowly, informal constraints, which shift gradually and "subconsciously."

Three features of this model are worth keeping:

- **Change is filtered through subjective and possibly incorrect mental models.** North relaxes perfect information and unlimited cognitive capability; institutional change need not track real payoffs, only perceived ones.
- **Formal and informal rules change at different speeds.** "Formal rules may change over night, but informal constraints do not." The tension between a new formal rule and an unchanged informal constraint is resolved by a new equilibrium that is less radical than either.
- **Gridlock is the trigger for non-incremental change.** North defines it as "a lack of mediating institutions that enable conflicting parties to reach compromises." Where such institutions exist, pressure is discharged incrementally; where they do not, it accumulates and ruptures.

**[wiki synthesis]** The gridlock mechanism is functionally adjacent to veto-point proliferation, but North does not use that term and does not measure it. Flagged as a mapping the wiki is making, not a claim the source supports.

## The worked case: US land policy

**[empirical — single narrative case, traceable]** The framework paper's illustration is the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 and the century of land policy after it, with named actors (Rev. Manassah Cutler, Nathan Dane, Rufus King). The traceable sequence: the Ordinance's disposal provisions → claims clubs emerged "to thwart competitive bidding" → squatters won a general preemption act → the minimum saleable land unit fell repeatedly → the Homestead Act gave land away free. Each step is a rent-seeking adaptation that then constrains the next step. This is the essay's strongest evidence; the accompanying general claims about "third world countries" and socialist economies are conclusions asserted from the framework rather than tested.

## Lock-in measured: how long agreement takes

**[empirical — Libecap's own case work, not second-hand]** Libecap supplies a quantified analogue at the level of a single resource regime. His repeated pattern: agreement on efficient property rights becomes likely only *after* the number and heterogeneity of parties has shrunk through attrition — that is, only after most of the rents have already been dissipated. Evidence: oil-field unitization deals took 4–9 years to negotiate across seven Texas fields; full ITQ systems in fisheries were adopted only "when fish stocks are near collapse"; the same wait-for-attrition dynamic appears in California groundwater basins.

**[wiki synthesis]** This is the same lock-in claim as North's, measured with a different instrument: not "the matrix persists" but "the correction arrives N years late and after X% of the value is gone." It is a considerably more useful form of the claim, because *time-to-agreement* and *rent dissipated before agreement* are both measurable. See [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]].

## What these sources do *not* establish

**[wiki synthesis — gap]** Every age and lock-in claim on this page is at the level of the **institutional matrix** — a whole society's rule-set — or of a resource regime. None of these sources makes any claim about a *single organisation* becoming more rigid as it ages. North's JEP essay is explicitly silent on headcount, hierarchy layers, or bureaucratic growth stages; the framework paper likewise. Ostrom makes no ossification-with-age claim at all: her design principles were derived cross-sectionally, contrasting long-surviving institutions against failed ones, with longevity as the *dependent* variable rather than a treatment. Williamson's scale argument (see [[governance-structures]]) is about size, not age, and is structural rather than empirical.

So the intuition that institutions ossify with age is, on this evidence, an inference by analogy from the matrix level to the organisational level. It is a good hypothesis and it is not yet supported. Logged as [[open-questions]] Q20.

**[empirical — soft counter-datapoint, added by the `scale-effects` ingest]** The first evidence bearing on the organisational reading points *against* it, mildly. Rajan & Wulf's panel is 300+ large US public firms, 1986–1998, averaging ~85 years since founding and ~47,500 employees — old, large and, in the authors' words, "not one whose organizational structure is likely to be in flux." Over thirteen years these firms restructured substantially: CEO span of control rose 50% in the full panel and 86% in the balanced sub-panel, hierarchy depth fell more than 25%, the probability that a division manager reported directly to the CEO went from 0.05 to 0.31, and the COO role was eliminated in about a fifth of the firms that had one. This happened while employment was flat or falling, so it is not growth forcing the change.

Weigh it carefully in both directions. It is a real observation of old organisations changing their own structure without an external shock large enough to be named — which bears on [[open-questions]] Q8 as well as Q20. But: it is senior management structure only, not rules, culture or mission; it is a period (post-takeover-wave US corporates) where external pressure was unusually high, and Rajan & Wulf explicitly do not identify the cause; the sample is old by construction, so there is no young-firm comparison and **age is never a covariate** in their regressions. It does not refute the ossification hypothesis. It does mean the hypothesis cannot be treated as obvious.

**[model — a parallel mechanism, different clock]** Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's dynamic extension supplies the closest thing in this wiki to a firm-level lock-in mechanism: an organisation that has thoroughly exploited its current technology may rationally postpone adopting a superior one indefinitely, because the new technology renders the accumulated hierarchy obsolete and a replacement must be built from scratch. Sunk organisational capital, not sunk political interest — the same shape as North's increasing returns at a different unit of analysis. It runs on **time since the current technology was adopted**, not time since founding, and it is a modelled result with no empirical test. Do not read it as age evidence.
