# Whitehall Monitor 2026: The UK Civil Service (IfG Case Profile)

The Institute for Government's annual audit of the UK central civil service (Whitehall Monitor 2026, Part 2) is a descriptive case profile of a large, mature bureaucracy: about 520,440 staff (Q3 2025), a post-2016 growth ratchet that has not unwound despite headcount caps, incentive-driven churn, unreformed senior pay, flat arm's-length-body numbers, and major-project costs that have more than doubled in real terms. It sits alongside [[defra-food-supply-chain-resilience-case-profile]] as a UK administrative-state case, but it is a think-tank synthesis of official series, not a primary audit.

**Evidence tier note.** Mixed and attributed to a camp. The underlying series are **[empirical]** administrative data (civil service statistics, People Survey, NAO exit-scheme review, NISTA/GMPP annual report, Treasury spending documents, departmental accounts), long time series with department-level breakdowns. The interpretations are IfG judgement: **[model]** in the weak sense of an argumentative essay, with no identification. Example: turnover falling "because of" recruitment freezes, and the claim that DSIT has "correctly diagnosed" its problems. The AI-savings ambitions rest on ministerial speeches and press releases (a DSIT trial claiming about two weeks a year saved per civil servant), unproven. IfG is a centrist, pro-professional-civil-service, pro-capability think tank, sceptical of political headcount targets and "bonfire of the quangos" rhetoric; it frames reform as a leadership and capability problem rather than a structural or incentive-decay one. This is an insider-technocratic view and should be read as such.

## The growth ratchet

**[empirical]** Growth since 2016 was, in IfG's phrasing, "largely a choice" (Brexit, the pandemic) and has not unwound despite ministerial headcount caps (2023). The newest departments grew fastest: DSIT (established 2023) grew 17.7%, largely via a transfer of about 930 staff. **[wiki synthesis]** Read against [[bureaucratic-growth-and-parkinsons-law]] and the open conflict [[parkinson-vs-knowledge-hierarchy-growth]], this is a datapoint for shock-driven growth that persists after its cause ends, not for workload-independent Parkinsonian drift; it does not test either directly.

## Churn, pay and incentives

**[empirical]** Staff cost is 82% of administration budgets, so the announced administration-budget cuts (11% by 2028/29, 16% by 2029/30) require headcount cuts. Senior Civil Service real pay is 24% below 2010 after a 26% cut, and the pay review body (SSRB) calls it "broken"; the government has set senior pay against SSRB advice. **[model]** IfG argues excess churn is incentive-driven: staff must change job to climb the ladder or get a raise, and cites pay progression in the digital and data profession and FCDO minimum service terms as fixes. The fall in turnover is attributed to recruitment freezes rather than fewer exits, an inference not tested. Exit schemes carried "regrettable losses" (NAO: 8,586 exits across 33 bodies, over £0.5bn redundancy cost). See [[personnel-economics-of-the-state]] for the evidence base on selection and pay.

## Structure, delivery and projects

- **[empirical]** Arm's-length bodies: 57.7% of resource departmental spending flows through them. Public-body reviews since 2010: 104 reviewed, 75 completed, £172m claimed savings, ALB count flat. IfG's conclusion is that cutting ALB numbers is "far from a silver bullet".
- **[empirical]** Government Major Projects Portfolio: 213 projects, whole-life cost £998bn, more than double in real terms since 2013 with project count up only 12% (191 to 213); cost per project drives the increase. Only 8% of portfolio spend had a suitable evaluation plan in 2019; the "unfeasible" share is the highest since 2020. About 80 legacy IT systems are at critical risk.
- **[empirical]** Efficiency targets of at least 5% (£21.9bn by 2028/29), of which under £13.0bn is technical efficiencies and several billion is unspecified. Consultancy spend peaked at £1.7bn (2021/22) and fell to £1.1bn (2024/25); temporary-staff spend fell from £8.8bn to £5.9bn, both mostly pandemic-driven in DHSC/NHS.
- **[model]** Digital transformation repeatedly fails, IfG argues, because it needs whole-process and team change across departments rather than a central strategy; the Government Digital Service is 15 years old with repeated strategies and mixed results. Compare [[isomorphic-mimicry-and-capability-traps]].

## Levers IfG proposes (not evaluated here)

Publish and audit departmental efficiency plans; pay progression within grade and minimum service terms; reform SCS pay; use exit schemes only where they improve capability; budget rather than numeric headcount targets; shared AI learning infrastructure; avoid supplier lock-in; NISTA oversight of the major-project portfolio; a £100m Test, Learn and Grow fund for locally driven reform. **[wiki synthesis]** These are recommendations, not tested levers, and belong at tier (iii) in the vocabulary of [[reform-levers]].

## Power and scope

Executive-centred: Treasury and Cabinet Office set headcount, administration budgets and efficiency targets; ministers announce ALB abolitions (for example NHS England) and use them as political signalling. UK central government only, 2024/25 to Q3 2025 data, Westminster-style permanent civil service; NHS is largely excluded except in spend figures. Generalisation to other levels or countries is not attempted. The report does not test whether these dynamics are public-specific.
