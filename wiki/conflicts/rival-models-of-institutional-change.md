# Conflict: Rival Models of Institutional Change

**Status: OPEN — not adjudicated. This is the headline conflict of the `institutional-evolution` batch.** Three incompatible accounts of how institutions change over time, each held by a serious source, none tested against either of the others. They differ on what the default state is, on where the impetus for change comes from, and on whether elapsed time makes change harder. **They also fail differently on falsifiability, which is the most useful thing about the comparison.**

| | Position A — lock-in deepens | Position B — punctuated / exogenous | Position C — gradual / endogenous |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Default state** | Reproduction, becoming more entrenched | Stasis | Continuous low-level contestation |
| **Source of change** | Rare rupture when pressure has nowhere to go | Exogenous shock: crisis | Endogenous: actors exploiting gaps in interpretation and enforcement |
| **Role of elapsed time** | Raises switching costs monotonically | Raises switching costs between crises; irrelevant during them | Opens interpretive space; contestation runs from year zero |
| **Held by** | North; Pierson; Krasner's stability half | Krasner | Thelen (with Streeck and Mahoney) |
| **Ex ante predictor** | None | None | Predicts the *form* of change, not whether change occurs |

## Position A — positive feedback makes the path progressively harder to leave

**Sources:** `raw/research/institutional-evolution/01-north-nobel-lecture.md`; `raw/research/institutional-evolution/03-pierson-increasing-returns.md`; the earlier North JEP essay and framework paper on [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]].
**Evidence tier:** (b) throughout. North's Nobel lecture is a programmatic essay; Pierson is a review essay. The single systematic dataset on this side is **Skocpol's**, reported secondhand by Pierson: of 40 US voluntary associations founded before 1900, 19 stayed above a 1%-of-population membership threshold for at least five decades and 10 for a century or more.

- Four increasing-returns mechanisms (setup costs, learning, coordination, adaptive expectations) plus a political feedback loop in which rules select the organisations that then defend them.
- Pierson adds four politics-specific mechanisms and argues politics locks in *harder* than markets, because competition, learning and far-sighted capital markets are all weaker there.
- Krasner's stability half belongs on this side too, reached independently: accumulated information, trust and shared expectations are sunk, so continuing with a disliked institution can be rational because its variable cost is below the new institution's fixed plus variable cost.
- **Falsifiability: absent, and conceded on one side.** North states no condition under which lock-in would fail to occur and no ex ante predictor of which societies escape. Pierson concedes the problem explicitly, citing Geddes on "many variables, few cases", and grants that critical junctures "are usually attributed, often ex post".

## Position B — long stasis, punctuated by crisis-driven reorganisation

**Source:** `raw/research/institutional-evolution/07-krasner-approaches-to-the-state.md` (Krasner 1984).
**Evidence tier:** (b) review essay contributing a metaphor, over other scholars' case histories. No formal model, no regression, no natural experiment.

- "Institutional change is episodic and dramatic rather than continuous and incremental" — stated flatly and generally, across regime types.
- All change is exogenous: Pattern I (domestic society outrunning state institutions) and Pattern II (external military or fiscal threat), with external pressures ranked "almost certainly more compelling".
- Functional explanations predicting smooth adjustment are explicitly rejected: "The state as administrative apparatus and legal order will not smoothly adjust to changes in its domestic environment."
- **Krasner presents this as a metaphor borrowed from evolutionary biology**, in his own words, and does not engage the falsifiability debate that surrounded the biological original.
- **Falsifiability: absent.** No threshold of magnitude, duration or intensity distinguishes a crisis that produces a punctuation from a disturbance that gets absorbed. Whether "the boundaries of legitimate coercion" moved is itself typically only assessable by observing whether institutions changed — the outcome being explained.
- **His own strongest cited case cuts against the "short burst" framing.** Skowronek's periodisation gives a "stasis" of 1877–1900 (23 years) and a "punctuation" of 1900–1920 (20 years). Krasner does not reconcile this.

## Position C — gradual, endogenous transformation is the modal mode

**Source:** `raw/research/institutional-evolution/06-streeck-thelen-institutional-change.md` (Thelen 2009, presenting Streeck & Thelen 2005 and Mahoney & Thelen 2010).
**Evidence tier:** (b) framework plus qualitative case narrative — one century-long case (German VET from 1897) and three parallel German case narratives, plus one cross-national quantitative claim that is referenced but not shown.

- Institutions are rules that must always be interpreted, enforced and enacted by actors with conflicting interests, so gaps between design and effect are permanent and continuously exploited. Four sources of gaps: designers' cognitive limits; ambiguity built in at founding by political compromise; the losers of the design fight persisting; and time, which lets designers die while the rule survives.
- Four mechanisms — layering, drift, conversion, displacement — predicted by veto possibilities × interpretive discretion.
- **Positive feedback is accepted and its usual implication denied**: feedback effects "are not associated with the faithful reproduction of the system as originally constituted"; an institution "created for one set of purposes... could be carried forward on the shoulders of another coalition altogether". German VET, built in 1897 to weaken organised labour, became the core of union–management partnership.
- **The age-gating premise is denied directly**: conflicts over a rule "often begin as soon as a rule is laid down".
- **Falsifiability: better on one axis, no better on the other.** The typology predicts which *form* gradual change takes, from two in-principle-measurable variables — more than either rival offers. It supplies **no** condition predicting whether a given shock produces discontinuity or gets absorbed. The 2×2 is exhaustive by construction, so any observed change can be redescribed post hoc as one of the four cells; discretion is sometimes inferred *from* the fact that drift occurred; and the displacement cell has **no worked example** in the source.

## Where the disagreement actually is

Three separable questions are being answered differently, and conflating them is what makes this look like one big argument rather than three tractable ones.

1. **Is the default reproduction or contestation?** A and B say reproduction or stasis; C says continuous contestation. This is directly testable and nobody has tested it: it asks whether attempts to reinterpret, under-enforce or layer around a rule are running at a steady rate in periods that Positions A and B would classify as stasis.
2. **Is the impetus exogenous or endogenous?** B says exogenous, categorically; C says endogenous is modal and exogenous is real but less common; A says exogenous rupture happens where gridlock prevents incremental discharge (North's mediating-institutions condition). Note that A and B agree here more than either agrees with C.
3. **Does elapsed time make reversal harder?** A and B's stability halves say yes, monotonically; C says time *opens* interpretive space. **This is the wiki's priority question and no source measures it.**

**The three positions do not disagree about any observation.** They disagree about how to classify observations that all three can accommodate. That is the deep problem: an institution that formally persists for a century while its function changes completely is "lock-in" to A (the form survived), "stasis" to B (no crisis, no punctuation), and "conversion" to C (function transformed without formal change). German VET is exactly that case and all three frameworks can absorb it.

## What would settle it

1. **Measure reversal-attempt success against institutional age.** The discriminating quantity is whether the success rate of attempts to change a rule falls with elapsed time since the rule was laid down. A predicts a declining rate; C predicts approximately flat, with the *form* of successful change shifting from formal amendment toward drift and conversion as veto structures harden. Requires a population of institutions with dated founding, dated change attempts, and coded outcomes — legislative amendment records, regulatory dockets, and corporate charter amendments all supply this and nobody has used them for it. [[open-questions]] Q39.
2. **Count contestation attempts during declared stasis periods.** Positions A and B both require that periods they classify as stable are quiet. C requires they are not. Litigation counts, enforcement-discretion variance, and formal exemption grants inside a Krasner-style "stasis" window would separate them directly.
3. **State an ex ante crisis criterion and pre-register it.** Neither B nor C supplies one. A criterion applied prospectively to a set of shocks, with punctuation predicted in advance, is what would move Position B from classification scheme to theory. [[open-questions]] Q40.
4. **Pair a formal-change measure with a functional-coverage measure** on the same institutions over the same window ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D55). If the two routinely diverge, Position A's habit of reading persistence as lock-in is unsafe regardless of which model wins.
5. **Find an institutional analogue of Arthur's adoption count.** Arthur's formal result — reversal cost rising without bound — runs on cumulative adoptions, not years. If some institutional counter (budget rounds, election cycles, staff cohorts socialised into the rule) predicts reversal difficulty better than calendar time does, Position A gains a mechanism it currently lacks and D58 becomes readmissible. [[open-questions]] Q45.

## A datum the wiki cannot yet place

**[flagged, not resolved]** Pierson's and Skocpol's century-old US voluntary associations persist largely unchanged. Rajan & Wulf's old, large US firms restructured substantially over thirteen years — CEO span of control up 50%, hierarchy depth down more than 25%, the COO role eliminated in about a fifth of firms that had one, with headcount flat or falling. Both are real observations of old organisations, and they point opposite ways.

Two candidate reconciliations, neither stated by any source: **sector** (membership associations against public corporations under takeover-market pressure), and **different dependent variables** (Skocpol measures whether the organisation still exists at threshold size; Rajan & Wulf measure whether its internal structure changed — an organisation can score "persisted" and "transformed" simultaneously, which is Position C's whole point). Logged as [[open-questions]] Q44.
