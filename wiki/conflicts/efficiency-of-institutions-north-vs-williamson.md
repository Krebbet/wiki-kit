# Conflict: Efficiency of Institutions — North vs. Williamson

**Status: OPEN — not adjudicated.** Williamson's discriminating alignment hypothesis holds that transactions are matched to governance structures so as to economise on transaction costs, and his remediableness criterion presumes an extant arrangement efficient until a feasible superior alternative is demonstrated. North explicitly rejects the efficiency presumption: institutional evolution can and often does produce monopoly, restricted entry, and redistributive property rights, and path dependence holds inefficient matrices in place because the organisations advantaged by them control the process of changing them. Both authors are writing inside the same tradition and each cites the other approvingly, which makes the disagreement easy to miss — but it is a disagreement about what the theory predicts, not about emphasis.

## Position A — Williamson: presume cost-economising, and apply the presumption symmetrically

**Source:** `raw/research/foundations-nie/05-williamson-nobel-transaction-cost.md` (Nobel lecture, 2009).
**Evidence tier:** (b) theoretical framework. The lecture presents no data; it asserts "over 800" corroborative empirical tests by 2006, citing meta-analyses (Macher & Richman 2008; Geyskens, Steenkamp & Kumar 2006) that this wiki has not read.

- The **discriminating alignment hypothesis**: transactions, which differ in their attributes, are aligned with governance structures, which differ in their costs and competences, "so as to effect a (mainly) transaction cost economizing outcome."
- The **remediableness criterion**: an extant mode of organisation is presumed efficient unless a *feasible and implementable* superior alternative can be shown, with net implementation costs included. Applied "in any sector whatsoever – public, private, nonprofit, etc."
- The motivating complaint is asymmetry, not efficiency-optimism: policy analysis treated private-sector shortfalls as "market failures" from positive transaction costs while implicitly assuming public-sector transaction costs were zero — which Williamson calls "nonsense."
- Williamson does hedge — the outcome is "*mainly*" cost-economising — and he names political-purpose-driven design as real (the post-9/11 Department of Homeland Security).

## Position B — North: institutional evolution has no efficiency default

**Sources:** `raw/research/foundations-nie/01-north-institutions-jep.md`; `raw/research/foundations-nie/02-north-institutional-change-framework.md`.
**Evidence tier:** (b) theoretical framework illustrated by institutional history. The JEP essay offloads its major comparative claims to North & Thomas (1973) and North & Weingast (1989).

- Direct statement: "private profitability has been enhanced by creating monopolies, by restricting entry and factor mobility, and by political organizations that established property rights that redistributed rather than increased income."
- The mechanism that blocks correction: increasing returns (setup costs, learning effects, coordination effects, adaptive expectations) plus a political feedback loop — organisations that arose under the existing rules have a stake in perpetuating them, and the ideologies of those running them rationalise the matrix. Change is biased "in favor of the interests of the existing organizations" regardless of aggregate efficiency.
- Distributional statement, framework paper: institutions "exploited individuals and groups — Indians and slaves, and not infrequently immigrants, workers, and farmers — to the benefit of those with superior bargaining power."
- North positions himself against Williamson by name: Williamson's efficient-solutions framework "assumes away" the question of why economies vary in performance, which North takes to be the whole point.
- Corroborating from a third source: Libecap (**his own case work**, not his second-hand North material) documents regulators preferring revocable permits to transferable rights specifically because secure rights would weaken their discretion — a persistent inefficient arrangement chosen by the party with the power to change it.

### Added by the `scale-effects` ingest (2026-08-19)

Two further items on the North side of the ledger. Neither resolves anything; both narrow what the efficiency default can be resting on.

- **Coase's efficiency claim is asserted, not proven by his own argument.** *Source:* `raw/research/scale-effects/01-coase-nature-of-the-firm.md`. **Evidence tier:** (b) formal argument, no data. Coase writes that firms arise "because they represent a more efficient method of organizing production" (note 14) — the historical seed of the presumption Williamson later formalises. But the marginal argument establishes only that a firm absorbs a transaction where marginal internal organising cost is locally below marginal market cost. It contains no mechanism that selects against a locally stable but globally inferior boundary; the sole disciplining device named is "it is always possible to revert to the open market if he fails," itself asserted. Coase further concedes that firm size is pushed *away* from the organising-cost optimum by two forces he names: tax and quota asymmetries between market and intra-firm transactions make firms **artificially larger** than efficiency alone would produce, and monopoly gain "offers a powerful incentive to continuous and unlimited expansion." Both are the founder of the tradition naming non-efficiency determinants of institutional boundaries.
- **Niskanen's model produces allocatively wrong output with zero internal waste.** *Source:* `raw/research/scale-effects/03-niskanen-peculiar-economics-bureaucracy.md`. **Evidence tier:** (b) deductive model with invented numbers; the author's own Section V lists the empirical tests that had not been run. In the budget-constrained equilibrium the bureau's budget exactly covers minimum cost — no slack, nothing a cost-effectiveness audit would find — and output still exceeds the Pareto-optimal level by roughly two-thirds, or twice over where the bureau also discriminates among factor suppliers. This is a specific, formal counter-example to the inference Williamson's remediableness criterion invites: **absence of demonstrable waste is not evidence of efficiency**, because the pathology can be allocative rather than operational. It also sharpens the falsification problem in the criterion — a demand-constrained bureau's true minimum-cost function is, by Niskanen's argument, unrecoverable from any behaviour an outside analyst can observe, so the "feasible superior alternative" the criterion demands may be undemonstrable in principle rather than absent in fact.

**Not adjudicated.** Both items are models, not measurements, and both come from authors sympathetic to the efficiency tradition rather than from critics of it. They add to Position B's ledger without settling anything, and the register remains unbalanced in the way point 4 below describes.

### Added by the `incentives-and-institutional-form` ingest (2026-08-19)

One item for each side. Neither resolves anything, and the two are not symmetric in kind — which is itself the most useful thing about them.

**On the North side — Prendergast's verdict on agency theory's design predictions.** *Source:* `raw/research/incentives-and-institutional-form/05-prendergast-incentives-firms.md`. **Evidence tier:** (a) empirical survey — and this is the first item on either side of this ledger that is a **reading of measured data** rather than a model. Prendergast separates two claims that are usually run together and grades them differently. "Do incentives matter" is well supported. "Are contracts designed as agency theory predicts" is not: relative performance evaluation has mixed-to-absent support (Gibbons & Murphy confirm it for executives but against the whole market rather than industry peers; Aggarwal & Samwick, Barro & Barro, Blackwell et al. and Janakiraman et al. find little), he calls the executive-pay-levels literature "a poor method of testing agency theory," and he flags the efficiency-wage tests as possibly unfalsifiable with available methods. His summary: **"the available empirical evidence on contracts does not yet provide a ringing endorsement of the theory."** He adds a standing puzzle against his own paradigm — company-wide profit sharing produces ~4–5% productivity gains in firms with thousands of workers, where the N−1 free-riding logic says the individual incentive should have vanished.

*Why this leans North.* Williamson's discriminating alignment is a claim that observed arrangements are the cost-economising ones. Prendergast's finding is that at the level where alignment is most directly checkable — the actual shape of compensation contracts, in the private sector, in the setting with the best data and the most competitive pressure — the theoretically efficient design is **not reliably what is observed**. If the efficiency presumption underperforms there, it is weaker still where competitive discipline is absent.

*The hedge that must travel with it.* Prendergast attributes much of the shortfall to **data limitation, not to the theory being wrong** — contracts are frequently unobserved and must be inferred from the pay-performance relationship, "which is tainted with many confounding effects," and there are no easily accessible personnel databases. He explicitly declines to make this a criticism of the literature. So this is evidence that the efficiency default is **undemonstrated at the contract level**, not evidence that it is false. Preserve the distinction; the value of the item is precisely that a sympathetic insider reports the record as unpersuasive.

**On the Williamson side — Hart's "efficiency, not ideology."** *Source:* `raw/research/incentives-and-institutional-form/02-hart-nobel-lecture.md`. **Evidence tier:** (b) formal model with one weak correlational citation on outcomes. Hart's property-rights theory assumes parties converge on the efficient allocation of residual control rights, and he extends that logic to the public/private ownership choice, closing: "Perhaps the most valuable lesson of the analysis is that it suggests that the public-private choice should be seen as a matter of efficiency, not ideology." Structurally this is the same move as remediableness — treat the observed arrangement as the solution to an optimisation problem and make the burden fall on whoever claims otherwise — and it is applied to exactly the public/private domain where Williamson says his own application is aspirational.

*Why it is scope-limited, and how far.* Three limits, all from the source itself. (i) The claim is about **bilateral ownership choice** — should this asset sit with this party or that one — not about whole institutional matrices persisting inefficiently over decades, which is North's domain. It does not touch the political-feedback mechanism in Position B. (ii) The *answer* is explicitly **not** invariant: it turns on which tasks are contractible, whether competition is available, and how innovation trades against harm prevention. Hart concludes the case for private provision is weak in maximum-security prisons and plausible in half-way houses — an efficiency criterion that returns different answers, not an efficiency presumption that ratifies whatever exists. (iii) The empirical anchor is one correlational citation ("Hart et al. (1997) cite some evidence that the level of violence is indeed higher in private prisons") plus a 2016 DOJ report, with no causal design described, and the "competition strengthens the case for privatization" step is asserted with no citation at all.

*And a self-undercutting note the wiki should keep.* Hart's own lecture supplies the strongest reason to doubt the efficiency default in his framework: the Maskin-Tirole mechanism would, if used, make unverifiable variables effectively contractible and eliminate the need for ownership allocation entirely — and Hart reports that **he knows of essentially no real cases where it is used**. Parties are demonstrably not reaching for an available efficiency-improving instrument. He treats this as evidence against the fully-rational baseline and abandons full rationality in response. That is a founder of the efficiency tradition reporting a systematic gap between what the efficient design would be and what parties do, which is the shape of North's objection arriving from inside Position A.

**Net effect on the ledger.** Position B gains its first measured item. Position A gains a formal item whose author scope-limits it himself and whose own lecture contains a documented case of parties not adopting an available efficient mechanism. Point 4 below — that the register is unbalanced because both positions come from founders of the same school — still holds, and now has a sharper form: **every item on both sides of this conflict now comes from inside the tradition, and the tradition's own strongest empirical reviewer says the design predictions are not confirmed.**

## Where the disagreement actually is

Not on whether inefficiency exists — Williamson concedes political-purpose design, North concedes that productivity-raising rules "have overwhelmingly dominated" in the US case. The disagreement is over the **default assumption when you observe an arrangement you do not understand**:

- Williamson's default: it is probably there because it economises on transaction costs; the burden is on the critic to name a feasible alternative.
- North's default: it is there because it served the bargaining power of whoever shaped it; efficiency is one possible reason among several and needs its own evidence.

The stakes are practical. Williamson's default makes reform proposals cheap to reject (no feasible alternative demonstrated). North's default makes persistence evidence of nothing in particular.

## What would settle it

1. **A falsification test for remediableness.** State in advance what observation would show an extant arrangement is *not* remediable-efficient. If none can be stated, the criterion is a research heuristic rather than a hypothesis, which resolves the conflict by dissolving Position A's empirical content.
2. **Cases where a demonstrably feasible superior alternative existed and was not adopted.** Libecap's Texas/Oklahoma comparison is the nearest thing available in the current source set: the same problem, a demonstrated workable solution in one jurisdiction (Oklahoma relaxed unanimity), non-adoption in the other under identifiable political pressure. Position A must argue the Texas arrangement was efficient given implementation costs; Position B predicts exactly this outcome. More cases of this shape would discriminate.
3. **Reading the meta-analyses Williamson cites** (Macher & Richman 2008; Geyskens et al. 2006) to see whether the "broadly corroborative" empirical literature tests alignment *predictions* or only documents correlation between asset specificity and integration — the latter is consistent with both positions.
4. **Sources outside the NIE tradition** on the same question. Both positions are stated by founders of the same school; the register is unbalanced by construction until a critic of the tradition is ingested.
