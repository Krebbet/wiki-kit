# Conflict: Decay as Real vs. Decay as Overstated

**Status: OPEN — not adjudicated.** This is the central conflict of the `institutional-stagnation` batch and the one the wiki's own thesis turns on, so it is filed with more care than usual about what each side actually claims. It is **not** a fight between people who think institutions get worse and people who think they do not. Both camps agree that institutions exhibit rigidity, that rules and constituencies accumulate, and that concentrated interests beat diffuse ones. The disagreement is narrower and sharper: **whether an institution's own elapsed age is the driver**, and whether the evidence usually cited for that proposition survives inspection.

The wiki user has a stated stake in the affirmative answer (project outcome 6). That is a reason to file this precisely rather than to resolve it early.

## Position A — decay is real, cumulative, and driven by elapsed time

**The claim.** Institutions accumulate pathologies as they persist, the accumulation is not self-correcting, and it is reversed only by rupture. The mechanism differs by author but the shape is common: something builds up that nothing removes.

**Sources and what each contributes:**

- **Olson, as reviewed by Heckelman** (`09-crit-heckelman-explaining-the-rain.md`) — *(a) empirical, at one remove.* The only counted evidence in the batch. Distributional coalitions form more easily than encompassing ones, accumulate in stable societies, extract redistribution rather than produce growth, and are cleared only by war, coup, revolution or a discrete legal rupture. **53 studies: 57% support, 23% mixed, 21% no support**, with no significant difference by methodology, publication outlet, author location or year (28 regressions at 57/18/25%; 25 case studies at 56/28/16%). This is the strongest version of Position A available anywhere in the batch, and it is a real body of evidence, not an assertion.
- **Fukuyama** (`03-fukuyama-america-in-decay.md`) — *(b) essay.* Institutions retain mental models past the point of empirical validity, and create favoured constituencies that block reform. Mandates accrete and are never discarded — the Forest Service's timber → firefighting → conservation layering, each with its own defending interest group. Vetocracy, judicialisation, repatrimonialisation.
- **Miller & Dudley** (`05-miller-dudley-regulatory-accretion.md`) — *(b) response article, one small cross-sectional dataset.* Rules accumulate because creation is gatekept and survival is not; vested incumbents lobby against repeal harder than the diffuse public lobbies for it. ~130 years of US federal regulation, 175,000+ CFR pages.
- **Schleicher & Bagley** (`06-schleicher-bagley-state-capacity-crisis.md`) — *(b) legal synthesis with cited quantitative work.* Procedural obligations accumulate as a tax on state capacity; Liscow & Brooks on highway costs rising since the 1970s beyond what wages and materials explain is the batch's best time-series evidence that *something* got worse.
- **Huntington, Bernstein, Olney** — reported by Novak and Carpenter & Moss rather than read directly. Huntington's "marasmus" — "a gradual and continuous wasting away of the body from a morbid cause" — applied to the ICC across "its sixty-five years of existence"; Bernstein's explicit life-cycle into "a ripe 'old age'"; Olney's 1892 letter, "the older such a Commission gets to be, the more inclined it will be found to take the business and railroad view of things." These are the canonical *age* texts, and this wiki has them only second-hand.

**The strongest form of Position A**, steelmanned: something does accumulate, the accumulation is measurable in at least three currencies (coalitions, rules, procedural obligations), a majority of a 53-study literature finds the predicted growth correlation, and the one place a real cost trend has been isolated against obvious confounds — Liscow & Brooks — finds it.

## Position B — decay is real but the age story is overstated and mis-evidenced

**The claim.** Rigidity, capture and goal displacement are real and mechanistically well described, but they are **structural properties of institutional form, not functions of elapsed time**; the age framing has been imported from metaphor and anecdote rather than measurement, and where duration genuinely does appear it belongs to a polity or a legal corpus, not to an institution.

**Sources, and note that the first three are founders of Position A's own literature:**

- **Merton** (`01-merton-bureaucratic-personality.md`) — *(b) conceptual essay.* Goal displacement and ritualism attach to rational-legal bureaucratic form as such — rule-generality, career-incentive design, esprit de corps, impersonality. A brand-new bureau and a century-old one are, on his own logic, equally subject. **He volunteers this without being asked to address any decay thesis.** No falsification condition, no levers, no data.
- **Stigler** (`02-stigler-theory-economic-regulation.md`) — *(b) model, two tests.* Capture is a cross-sectional equilibrium of the political market, present from inception. No age variable in either regression or in the argument. Motor-carrier regulation is explained as arriving *already* shaped by railroad interest; the CAB's zero-new-entrants record is cited as a constant running "since it was created in 1938", not as a trend. **Also volunteered.**
- **Selznick** (`04-selznick-foundations-theory-organization.md`) — *(b) pure theory.* Cooptation is triggered by threat; survival-maintenance needs are "a basic need of all empirical systems", present from inception. Age is simply absent as a variable. He additionally supplies the methodological warning that changes driven by "the availability of markets, legislative delegations, the swing of opinion" are "accidental from the point of view of the organizational process" and must not be folded into organisational theory. **Also volunteered.**
- **Carpenter & Moss** (`07-crit-carpenter-moss-preventing-capture-intro.md`) — *(b) literature critique.* Capture is a matter of degree, varying with institutional checks. They decline to treat the Olney letter as evidence: it "provides no direct evidence that the Commission did in fact 'take the business and railroad view of things'... it reveals only that one potentially interested observer predicted" it — and they use it as their lead case of anecdote becoming canonical through repeated citation by Bernstein, Huntington and the Senate floor. Plus Noll's tautology charge and the functionalist-fallacy charge against the whole inferential method.
- **Novak** (`08-crit-novak-revisionist-capture.md`) — *(b) historiographical critique.* Capture theory rests on a wrong short history of American regulation. Its canonical case was anticipated at design: Charles Francis Adams in **1871**, before the ICC existed, asked "Who will guard the virtue of the tribunal? Why should the corporations not deal with [the commissions] as [they did] with the legislatures?" If the risk was theorised ex ante, there is no healthy youth to decline from.
- **Heckelman, again** — the tiebreaker sits on both sides. Against Position A's strong form: only "a handful" of 53 studies directly test coalition *accumulation*; most test stability-duration against growth, "leaving the role of special interest organizations and distributional coalitions implicitly in the background". The years-of-stable-borders proxy "does not address Olson's theory or the spirit of Olson's tests very well". And practising economists (2.53) and political scientists (2.39) both score *below* the midpoint of a 1–5 scale on the theory's signature prediction — "Apparently, there is not widespread belief in Olson's explanation for the rain."
- **Fukuyama, against himself** — the Madisonian veto structure is ~230 years old and unchanged in count, and he says it worked adequately when one party was dominant, becoming "a formula for gridlock" only "since the 1980s". He also names two earlier gridlock crises produced by the *same* structure, resolved by external shock — a cyclical pattern, not a monotonic curve.
- **Schleicher & Bagley, against themselves** — their own procedural accumulation is "the ideological residue of the 1970s and 1980s", imposed uniformly on agencies of every age; a new state agency and a century-old one face the identical little-NEPA. And Caughey & Warshaw show state policy reaching ~70% majority-alignment within three decades.

**The strongest form of Position B**, steelmanned: the mechanisms are sound and the pathologies real, but the specific proposition "institution X gets worse because X is old" has never been measured on an institution, the texts usually cited for it disclaim it, and its canonical evidence is one interested party's 1892 prediction.

## What the disagreement actually is

|  | Position A | Position B |
|---|---|---|
| Rigidity, capture, accretion are real | Yes | **Yes** |
| Concentrated interests beat diffuse ones | Yes | Yes — Carpenter & Moss accept the entry-barrier mechanism as one real form |
| Driver | Elapsed time, absent a reset shock | Institutional *form* and the structure of the political market, present from inception |
| Unit the duration attaches to | Asserted: the institution | Shown: a regime, a legal corpus, or a polity |
| Direct measurement of the driver | "A handful" of 53 studies, none at institution level | — (the critics measure nothing either) |
| A specified falsification condition | Stigler: none. Merton: none. Selznick: none. Schleicher & Bagley: none. | Carpenter & Moss's three-part test is one, and it is the batch's only one |

**[wiki synthesis]** Three observations that neither camp makes and that the wiki should hold onto.

1. **The two positions are not symmetric in kind.** Position A is a claim about a cause. Position B is mostly a claim about the *evidentiary status* of that cause. Novak and Carpenter & Moss run no age test either — they show that the affirmative case's evidence does not support what it is cited for. **The age reading is therefore unevidenced, not refuted**, and this conflict file must not be cited as a refutation.
2. **The most striking fact in the batch is who is on Position B.** Merton, Stigler and Selznick are three founders of the decay-mechanism literature, and each states his own mechanism's age-independence without being asked and without a decay thesis in view. That is not critics scoring points; it is the affirmative tradition's own authors declining a claim later attributed to them.
3. **The level-of-analysis substitution is systematic, not incidental.** In every source where duration appears, it belongs to a political regime, a legal corpus or a national polity. Four consecutive research batches have now looked for institution-level age evidence and not found it. At this point the substitution looks like the field's central confound rather than a gap in this wiki's reading. See [[open-questions]] Q61.

## What would settle it

**[wiki synthesis]** One design, stated so that the next batch can be scored against it:

> **A study measuring a specific institution's own age against a directly observed accumulation variable — coalition count, rule count, or a capture indicator attached to that institution — rather than regime-stability duration or cross-sectional density.**

Its components, each of which some existing work has and none of which any work has together:

1. **Age of the institution itself** — years since founding, with years since any charter-level reconstitution recorded separately, because a re-founded agency is the natural control and nobody uses it.
2. **A directly observed accumulation variable on that same institution** — the count of rules it has issued and not repealed, the count of organised constituencies with standing to block its changes, or a capture indicator meeting Carpenter & Moss's three-part test. Not a proxy. This is Olson's Implication 2 at the organisational level, and it is exactly what the "handful" of 53 studies gestures at without reaching.
3. **A panel, not a cross-section** — the same institutions observed over time, so that the age coefficient is not identified off cohort differences.
4. **Controls for what this batch shows actually varies**: the vintage of the external legal regime the institution operates under (Schleicher & Bagley's 1970s–80s wave hits every agency regardless of founding date), and the polarisation or preference-divergence of its political principals (Fukuyama's own gating variable, and Hammond's on [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]]).

**No source in this batch does this.** Heckelman's review makes it possible to say how far the field is from it: a handful of 53 studies test accumulation at all, and none of those tests it on a single organisation.

Two weaker but immediately available tests, worth naming because they need no new data collection:

- **Enumerate Heckelman's "handful."** He never lists which of the 53 studies directly test Implication 2. Reading the review's Table 1 against Olson's chapter 3 would produce the subset, and that subset — not the 57% — is the evidence base for the accumulation mechanism.
- **The re-founding case.** An institution reconstituted by charter while its external legal regime and its political principals stay fixed separates institutional age from regime age with an event study rather than a panel. Nobody in this batch looks for one.
