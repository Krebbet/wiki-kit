# Conflict: Presumed vs. Measured Public-Sector Risk Aversion

**Status: OPEN — not adjudicated.** Four sources in the `risk-aversion-in-large-institutions` batch *presume or assert* that public-sector actors are systematically more risk averse than private-sector ones, and build mechanisms to explain it. **None of the four tests it.** One source tests it directly, with a randomized experiment on real managers, and finds no support — and under one condition finds a significant reversal. The conflict is therefore not between two bodies of evidence. It is between a presumption that four theoretical literatures rest on and the single measurement anyone in this batch made of it.

## Position A — the public/private risk-aversion gap, presumed

**Sources and what each actually does with the claim:**

- **Hood** (`01-hood-blame-game-ch1.md`) — *evidence tier (b), argumentative chapter.* Builds the blame-avoidance mechanism entirely on public-sector cases (French sewage sludge, English local government, US Congress, a German federal bureaucrat, EU institutional design), then asserts in one clause that the logic "often extends to private or independent sector providers of public services too" with no private-sector evidence and no attempt at the mapping onto a board and shareholders. The mechanism as specified — electoral accountability, legislative fire-alarm oversight, media scrutiny of officeholders — is public-sector-shaped. **He does not compare a public and a private organisation on any measure.**
- **Bozeman** (`07-bozeman-theory-of-red-tape.md`) — *evidence tier (b), definitional framework.* Theorises red tape's origins and never tests them; the word "risk" does not appear in the source. He is the most careful of the four: he states his general external-control model is "as applicable to private as it is to government organizations", names only two government-inherent causes (sovereignty; interconnection of policy missions), and **reports the public/private red-tape literature as genuinely mixed** — citing Buchanan (1975) finding *private* managers expressed greater rule adherence than public counterparts, against Rainey, Baldwin, Lan & Rainey and Kingsley & Bozeman finding the reverse. His reading of his own Bozeman/Reed/Scott (1992) data is that ownership is not the causal agent, external political authority is.
- **Hammond** (`06-hammond-veto-points-bureaucratic-autonomy.md`) — *evidence tier (b), formal model.* Public bureaucracies in democratic systems only; no private analogue is offered anywhere in the text. The word "risk" does not appear here either, and his central theorem is that veto-point count does not monotonically determine even the variable he *does* model (autonomy). He asserts nothing about risk aversion; he is included on this side only because he is routinely cited for the gridlock-implies-caution story that Position A rests on, and **his result does not support it**.
- **OECD** (`08-oecd-innovative-capacity-governments.md`) — *evidence tier (a) qualitative, institutional advocacy.* The strongest form of the assertion: risk aversion is "the most consistent and powerful factor limiting the consideration of innovative options" in government, with the mechanism explicitly a sector one — "in the public sector there is no expectation of appropriating financial gains... there may be less incentives to actually take risks in the public sector". Basis: a qualitative synthesis of 5 of 38 member states, interviews and workshops, heavily self-citing, with the report's own evidence protocols conceded to be undeveloped. **No private-sector comparison group exists in the study.**

**What Position A has in common:** every one of the four either asserts the gap without a comparison or theorises a mechanism whose test would require a comparison it never runs. Position A is not weakly evidenced; on this specific proposition it is **unevidenced**, and three of the four authors are explicit about the limits of what they have shown.

## Position B — measured, and not found

**Source:** `05-nicholson-crotty-public-managers-risk-averse.md` (Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty & Webeck 2019, *Journal of Behavioral Public Administration*).
**Evidence tier:** (a) empirical. Between-subjects randomized survey experiment, n = 300 real US managers (150 public, 150 private), Qualtrics panel, May 2017, presentation order randomized, randomization checks reported. **Sector itself is not randomized** — it is a screened self-reported characteristic, so the sector comparison is an observed moderator of a randomized manipulation.

- **Framing (Asian Disease Problem).** Gain frame: no significant sector difference (public 24.32% vs private 27.63% choosing the risky option). **Loss frame: public managers significantly *more* risk-tolerant — 76.62% vs 63.51%, p < .10.** Table χ² = 61.64, p = 0.000.
- **Status-quo bias, two independent experiments.** *Private* managers showed significant status-quo anchoring in all three conditions of Experiment A and in 2 of 3 of Experiment B; public managers never significantly exceeded the alternative-status-quo comparison in A and showed **no significant status-quo effect at all** in B.
- **Public service motivation** is genuinely sector-variant (public .19 vs private −.20, p < .05) — but it associates with the *risk-seeking* choice under loss framing (p < .05), the opposite of what Buurman et al. (2012) and Tepe & Prokop (2018) reported.

**The authors' own caveats, which belong on this side of the ledger:** they flag a real Type II concern in note 9 — the N "may be large enough to detect status quo bias but not large enough to confidently say that a lack of observed difference... is not a type 2 error" — and urge "caution in the interpretation of the status quo bias results because of our relatively limited sample size and the challenges of interpreting null findings". Their mitigation is that the *direction* runs consistently one way, which is an argument, not a resolution. The vignettes are previously-validated but not tailored to the sample's actual work, which they name as a live external-validity limit. Nonprofit managers are excluded entirely.

## What the disagreement actually is

|  | Position A | Position B |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | Public-sector actors are more risk averse | They are not, and under loss framing are less so |
| Evidence type | (b) theory, (b) formal models, (a) qualitative 5-country synthesis | (a) randomized experiment, n=300 real managers |
| Public/private comparison run? | **No — in any of the four** | Yes, on three manipulations |
| Behaviour measured? | No | Yes (choice under randomized framing and status-quo conditions) |
| Structural variables measured? | No (theorised only) | No |
| Size measured? | No | No |

**[wiki synthesis]** Note the two rows that are "no" on both sides. Nobody measured a structural variable, and nobody measured size. The conflict is narrower than it looks: it is entirely about the *sector* proposition, and both positions are silent on the size proposition that the wiki's framing question actually asked.

The conflict is also asymmetric in a way that matters for how it should be read. Position B does not refute Hood's mechanism, Bozeman's etiology or Hammond's theorem — those are claims about how blame, rules and coalitions work, and a null on a behavioural comparison does not touch them. What Position B removes is the **empirical warrant those literatures were borrowing**: the assumption that the phenomenon they explain has been established. A mechanism for a phenomenon that has not been demonstrated is a hypothesis about a hypothesis.

## What would settle it

**[wiki synthesis]** Three things, in ascending order of value:

1. **A higher-powered replication** of Nicholson-Crotty's design, sized to distinguish a true null from a Type II error on the status-quo results, which the authors themselves identify as the weakest limb.
2. **Occupation-matched vignettes.** The current design trades tailoring for validated instruments and the authors name the trade. Scenarios built for the subjects' actual decisions — procurement officers on procurement, clinicians on triage, budget officers on allocation — would test whether the null survives realistic stakes and domain familiarity.
3. **The decisive design, which no source in this batch attempts: measure *behavioural* risk-taking and the *structural* variables on the same subjects.** Blame exposure (tenure security, proximity to review, monitoring design — [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D45), veto-point count and preference divergence (D44), external-control density and rule density (D42), and feedback quality (D43), collected on the same organisations whose managers are then run through a behavioural risk task. Every source in the batch measures at most one side of that. Until someone measures both, the structural mechanisms and the behavioural evidence cannot be brought into contact at all, and this conflict cannot be adjudicated — only restated.

A fourth item, which would change the question rather than settle it: **add a size variable.** No source in this batch has one. A design that varied organisation size while holding sector fixed would test the proposition the research job was actually named after, and nothing here does.
