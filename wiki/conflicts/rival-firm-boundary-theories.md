# Conflict: Rival Theories of the Firm Boundary — Asset Specificity vs. Residual Control Rights vs. Measurement Cost vs. Adaptation/Authority

**Status: OPEN — not adjudicated. Four positions since 2026-08-20, not three.** Formal accounts explain the same phenomenon — why a transaction is brought inside a firm rather than bought on the market, and where the boundary of the firm falls — by three different mechanisms, and at least two of them position themselves *explicitly against* the third. Williamson's transaction cost economics makes **asset specificity** the central variable: specificity converts a large-numbers bidding situation into a bilateral dependency, and hierarchy is chosen because its fiat and forbearance regime handles the resulting maladaptation hazard. Grossman & Hart and Hart make **residual control rights over non-human assets** the central variable: ownership determines each party's ex post bargaining position, hence each party's ex ante incentive to make non-contractible investments. Holmstrom & Milgrom make **measurement cost** the central variable: ownership should track whoever bears the cost of the unmeasurable dimension of performance, and this is derived without any assumption about specific investment at all. These are not three vocabularies for one mechanism. They predict integration from different observables, and a case that is decisive for one is silent for the others.

**Checked against the wiki's actual text, 2026-08-19.** [[governance-structures]] currently states the Williamson position in full (asset specificity, the fundamental transformation, the three governance attributes, discriminating alignment, selective intervention) plus Coase's marginal limits-to-size argument and Rajan & Wulf's panel evidence. It contains **no** statement of the residual-control-rights or measurement-cost accounts, so this conflict is new territory for the page rather than a contradiction of something already recorded there. That page now carries a reconciliation section presenting all three side by side; this page carries the unresolved part.

## Position A — Asset specificity (Williamson)

**Source:** `raw/research/foundations-nie/05-williamson-nobel-transaction-cost.md`.
**Evidence tier:** (b) theoretical framework. No data in the lecture; it asserts "over 800" corroborative empirical tests by 2006, cited to meta-analyses this wiki has not read. Its own illustrations are two single cases (Schwinn; Boeing 787 and the Vought acquisition).

- Asset specificity — physical, human, site-specific, dedicated, brand-name, episodic — is "most important" along with outlier disturbances.
- Specificity produces the **fundamental transformation** into bilateral dependency; the hazard is *maladaptation* when a disturbance hits and each side can hold up the other.
- **Discriminating alignment**: transactions are matched to governance structures so as to effect a "(mainly) transaction cost economizing outcome." Hierarchy is chosen for its fiat and its forbearance-law regime — "hierarchy is its own court of ultimate appeal."
- Specificity is a **design variable**, not a given: a good can be engineered to be less specific at a cost in performance.
- The limit on integration is **selective intervention**: four promises the acquiring owner cannot self-enforce, plus the observation that "the political game is now played in a larger firm."

## Position B — Residual control rights (Grossman & Hart; Hart)

**Sources:** `raw/research/incentives-and-institutional-form/04-grossman-hart-property-rights.md`; `raw/research/incentives-and-institutional-form/02-hart-nobel-lecture.md`.
**Evidence tier:** (b) formal model. The authors say so, and are unusually disciplined about the model/reality boundary. The one applied section (1980s US insurance distribution) is cross-sectional association the authors themselves describe as interpreted with "considerable latitude." Hart's field citations (Baker & Hubbard on trucking, Woodruff on Mexican footwear) appear in footnotes and are not examined.

- Contracts are incomplete; whoever owns an asset decides whatever the contract is silent on. **Ownership *is* the residual right of control.**
- Ownership does not change the ex post outcome — renegotiation is assumed costless and always reaches the efficient action — it changes each party's **status-quo payoff** and hence the surplus split.
- Therefore the owner **overinvests** and the non-owner **underinvests**; integration trades one distortion for the other and is never a free lunch.
- Decision rule: allocate the asset to whichever party's non-contractible investment is more surplus-sensitive at the margin.
- Grossman & Hart position this **against** the transaction-cost tradition on methodological grounds: they argue repeatedly that Coase, Williamson and Klein et al. never rigorously define what limits firm size or what integration even *is*, and present their model as the missing microfoundation.
- Hart states the substantive contrast directly: Williamson's TCE addresses **ex post bargaining inefficiency** and how integration reduces it, while PRT isolates **ex ante distortion in non-contractible investment**; Coase and Williamson locate the firm in authority over *human capital*, PRT in control over *non-human assets*.

## Position C — Measurement cost (Holmstrom & Milgrom)

**Source:** `raw/research/incentives-and-institutional-form/03-holmstrom-milgrom-multitask.md`.
**Evidence tier:** (b) formal model, Propositions 1–7, no original data. The authors defer "new, testable results" to a companion working paper — i.e. they do not claim this one delivers them.

- Where the unmeasurable aspect of performance is how a productive asset's value changes over time, the optimal contract depends on who owns the asset's returns. Principal-owned → muted production incentives, to protect asset care. Agent-owned → intensified production incentives, to stop the agent over-conserving the asset.
- The conditions favouring **agent** ownership: the agent is not too risk-averse; the variance of asset returns is low; the variance of measurement error on other aspects of the agent's performance is low.
- The authors' own positioning, verbatim from §1: this analysis supports Williamson's observation that high-powered incentives are more common in markets than inside firms **"without relying on any assumptions about specific investments,"** and "it emphasizes measurement cost as an important determinant of integration **in contrast to the leading approaches, which stress asset specificity.**"
- Their footnote 5 distinguishes the claim from Alchian & Demsetz as well: they are *not* arguing owners can better monitor the workforce, but that ownership optimally tracks who bears the immeasurability cost.

## Position D — Adaptation / authority (Simon; Williamson's other theory)

*Added by the `theory-of-the-firm` ingest, 2026-08-20, from Gibbons (2004).*

**Source:** `raw/research/theory-of-the-firm/06-gibbons-four-theories-of-firm.md`.
**Evidence tier:** (b) theory synthesis. Gibbons's own third caveat is that he will "discuss theory, essentially ignoring evidence and testing." The adaptation theory is **formalised here for the first time**, by borrowing machinery from Aghion-Dewatripont-Rey (2002) and Hart-Holmstrom (2002); its original statements are Simon (1951) and Williamson (1971/73/75/91).

- Some decisions are **non-contractible even ex post**, and the opportunity to make them is fleeting, so the right to decide cannot be renegotiated once the state is revealed.
- Parties therefore **pre-allocate a pure decision right** to a "boss," who then acts self-interestedly. The loss is the gap between her preferred action and the joint-surplus-maximising one.
- The boss is chosen ex ante to maximise **expected total surplus** — authority is efficient because someone must decide unilaterally, not because anyone deserves to.
- **This requires no specific investment and no asset specificity anywhere in the derivation.** Gibbons states it directly: "there can be a coherent elemental theory of the firm without specific investments."
- The elemental model contains **no removal mechanism**: one-shot, and the subordinate has no ex post recourse.

## The finding that changes the shape of this conflict: the four are orthogonal, not nested

**[wiki synthesis of Gibbons's formal result]** Gibbons's headline is not that a fourth theory exists. It is that **the elemental theories' formal ingredient sets are disjoint**, so none formalises or subsumes another. He is blunt about the folk reading that this conflict page was implicitly built against: the claim that "Grossman and Hart (1986) formalized Williamson (1979)" is, in his words, **"just plain wrong."** The property-rights theory's ingredients are observable-non-contractible actions, observable-non-contractible states, and ex-post-contractible decisions; the rent-seeking theory's are contractible-ex-ante actions, never-contractible decisions, and inalienable decision rights. Disjoint sets.

Two consequences the wiki should carry:

1. **"Williamson's theory" is two theories.** Gibbons's textual-historical evidence: *Markets and Hierarchies* (1975) has **zero index entries** for asset-specificity terms, while *The Economic Institutions of Capitalism* (1985) has dozens. The rent-seeking theory (quasi-rents, haggling, specificity) and the adaptation theory (fleeting non-contractible decisions, authority) are both in the corpus and are not the same claim. [[governance-structures]] presents the 1985 version; the 1975 version is Position D's ancestor and the page did not have it.
2. **Position D changes what the discriminating case has to look like.** The existing test item on this page — high measurement difficulty with low asset specificity — separates A from C. It does not touch D, which predicts integration wherever a fleeting non-contractible decision must be made by *someone*, with or without specificity or measurement problems.

**Gibbons's own candidate discriminating test, with his own flag on it.** Under the property-rights theory (Position B), only the **marginal** effects of actions on the ex post threat-point differential should matter for governance choice — the intercept and state-dependent terms should not. That is a real, theory-specific prediction (Whinston 2003; Holmstrom & Roberts 1998), and it is the closest thing the literature has produced to a test that separates B from the rest. **Whinston's own note is that such margins are typically unobservable** — they are the marginal effects of non-contractible actions on non-contractible payoffs — so "direct tests... may be difficult." **Nobody has run it.** Record the test and record the flag together; a discriminating test that cannot be run is not progress toward settlement, it is a statement about why the conflict has stayed open.

**And Gibbons's own view of the horse race, which cuts against the framing of this page.** He thinks a horse race is probably the wrong frame: the four may all be true simultaneously, and each is too abstract to map onto data without domain-specific tailoring. **[wiki synthesis]** That is not the scope-condition settlement this page already rejects — it is weaker and more awkward. The scope reading says each theory owns a class of cases; Gibbons's reading says all four mechanisms may be operating in the same case, in which case the discriminating-case strategy is misconceived rather than merely unexecuted. The wiki does not adopt either; both are recorded.

## The identification critique that applies to all four

**[wiki synthesis of Masten, Meehan & Snyder 1991, as generalised by Gibbons]** This is the most consequential methodological item on this page, and it undercuts the evidentiary base the whole dispute has been resting on.

The standard empirical test in this literature is a regression of **organisational form on asset specificity** — logistic regression of integrate/don't-integrate on a transaction-difficulty variable (Monteverde & Teece 1982; Anderson & Schmittlein 1984; Masten 1984; Joskow 1985). Masten, Meehan & Snyder show that such a regression **cannot distinguish** two opposite causal readings:

- asset specificity **raises the transaction costs of non-integration**, or
- asset specificity **lowers the costs of internal organisation**.

Both produce the identical observed correlation. The standard practice assumes the cost-of-integration function is *independent* of the transaction-difficulty variable, and that assumption is unjustified and silently forecloses half the hypothesis space.

**Gibbons generalises it to any theory of his Figure-1 shape — that is, to all four positions on this page.** So the "over 800 broadly corroborative empirical tests" that [[governance-structures]] records Williamson asserting, and any equivalent body cited for the other three, are consistent with any of the four mechanisms *and* with the reverse causal direction within each. This does not show the correlations are spurious. It shows they carry **no information about mechanism or direction** without an independently derived cost-of-integration function, which no source in this wiki supplies. Test item 3 below was already asking whether the meta-analyses test alignment predictions or merely document a correlation; this sharpens the answer in advance — even a well-executed correlation would not discriminate.

## Chandler against Williamson: were four historical integration decisions "mistakes"?

*Filed by the `theory-of-the-firm` ingest, 2026-08-20. This is a sub-dispute within the conflict, and it is about the efficiency verdict rather than about the mechanism.*

**Williamson's position** ([[governance-structures]]): "Manufacturers appear sometimes to have operated on the mistaken premise that more integration is always preferable to less," and backward integration at **Pabst Brewing, Singer Sewing Machine, McCormick Harvester and Ford** "from a transaction cost point of view would appear to be mistakes."

**Chandler's position** ([[organizational-capabilities-and-the-m-form]]): those four decisions were rational **given the firm-specific capabilities and supply-market conditions actually prevailing at the time**. His stated basis: "when those companies actually made this investment, the supply network was unable to provide the steady flow of a wide variety of new highly specialized goods essential to assure the cost advantages of scale." The "mistake" reading is, on his account, an artefact of applying transaction-cost logic in hindsight rather than examining the capability constraint at the moment of the decision.

**What is actually in dispute.** Not the facts of the integration decisions — both agree they happened and roughly why. The dispute is over the **benchmark**. Williamson's **remediableness** criterion scores an arrangement against a feasible alternative; Chandler's objection is that the alternative Williamson treats as feasible (a supply network delivering specialised inputs at volume) **did not exist in the 1880s–1910s**, so the comparison is to a counterfactual, not to a remediable alternative. **[wiki synthesis]** If Chandler is right about the fact, Williamson's own criterion delivers Chandler's verdict — which makes this less a clash of frameworks than an empirical disagreement about what the supply networks of the period could do, decidable in principle from business-historical evidence. **Neither source supplies that evidence here**: Williamson's judgement is a sentence in a book, and Chandler's rebuttal is a paragraph in a compressed article summarising archives this wiki has not read. It is also a live instance of the standing dispute at [[efficiency-of-institutions-north-vs-williamson]] — whether an observed governance arrangement carries an efficiency presumption — arriving from business history rather than from institutional economics.

## Where the disagreement actually is

Not on whether integration happens, and not on the *direction* of the standard correlation — all four predict that relationships with high specificity, high hold-up exposure, poor measurement, or an unrenegotiable decision will tend to be internalised. The disagreement is over **which observable does the causal work**, and it has four sharp edges:

1. **What is the sufficient condition?** Holmstrom & Milgrom claim to reproduce Williamson's central stylised fact (high-powered incentives in markets, low-powered inside firms) with **zero** specific investment in the model. If that derivation is right, asset specificity is not necessary for the phenomenon it was introduced to explain.
2. **Ex ante or ex post?** Hart's contrast is a genuine timing disagreement about where the loss occurs. Williamson locates it in maladaptation *after* the disturbance; PRT locates it in investment foregone *before*, and assumes the ex post bargain is efficient by construction. A framework that assumes away ex post inefficiency cannot be a refinement of one whose entire content is ex post inefficiency.
3. **Is specific investment necessary at all?** Position D answers no, from a second direction and by formal derivation rather than by the substitution Position C performs. Two of the four elemental theories now reproduce the phenomenon with no specificity term. That is not a refutation of Position A, but it removes the argument that specificity is the only available mechanism.
4. **What is the firm made of?** Human capital and authority over people (Coase, Williamson) versus non-human assets (Hart: "a bundle of non-human assets... they are the glue that holds the firm together"). This is a definitional disagreement with empirical consequences: a firm whose value is entirely in indispensable people has no boundary at all on Hart's account, and Hart concedes replaceability of human capital as a load-bearing scope condition.

**Why this is not obviously reducible to a scope condition.** The tempting settlement — specificity explains one class of cases, measurement cost another, control rights a third — is not stated by any source, and the sources actively resist it: Holmstrom & Milgrom present measurement cost as a rival for the *same* cases, and Grossman & Hart present their model as the correct formalisation of the *same* phenomenon the transaction-cost tradition described informally. Adopting the scope-condition reading would be the wiki settling a dispute the authors are having on purpose.

## What would settle it

1. **A case where the three predictions diverge, with data.** The discriminating case is a relationship with **high measurement difficulty and low asset specificity**, or the reverse. Position C predicts integration in the first; Position A does not. Neither source supplies such a case.
2. **Resolve the Anderson & Schmittlein problem.** The electronics sales-force study is cited as supporting evidence by Holmstrom, by Holmstrom & Milgrom, and (through the same lineage) as consistent with the specificity account. Holmstrom & Milgrom concede it is equally consistent with **two competing explanations from inside their own theory** — asset/goodwill transfer versus exclusion of outside activities — so it discriminates between neither the internal pair nor the external three. A study designed to separate them would move this.
3. **Read the meta-analyses Williamson cites** (Macher & Richman 2008; Geyskens, Steenkamp & Kumar 2006) to see whether the "broadly corroborative" literature tests alignment *predictions* or merely documents a correlation between asset specificity and integration. A bare correlation is consistent with all three positions, since specificity, hold-up exposure and measurement difficulty co-occur in most real relationships. This is the same test item that appears on [[efficiency-of-institutions-north-vs-williamson]], and it would do double duty.
4. **Test the ex ante / ex post split.** Position B assumes ex post renegotiation is costless and efficient; Position A's entire content is that it is not. Direct evidence on renegotiation outcomes in specific-asset relationships would bear on whether B's simplifying assumption is a harmless idealisation or the thing being disputed.
5. **A fourth candidate is on the table and is currently the weakest.** Frey points at "the limits of the firm... in view of the possible limits of relying purely on extrinsic incentives" (Osterloh & Frey 2000) — a motivational account of firm boundaries. It is a pointer in a survey with no model and no test; recorded so it is not lost, not admitted as a fourth position.

**Note on what this conflict does *not* cover.** None of the three positions has been tested on a public bureau. Hart's private-prison application ([[property-rights-theory-of-the-firm]]) is the only one of the three extended to a public/private boundary decision at all, and it is one service, one country, with one correlational citation on outcomes.
