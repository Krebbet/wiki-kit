# Conflict: Functional vs. Rent-Seeking Growth in Institutional Size

**Status: OPEN — not adjudicated.** Two accounts of why institutions get bigger and deeper. Niskanen's bureau grows because its head's utility rises with total budget and the lump-sum funding form lets him extract the sponsor's whole surplus — growth is rent extraction, and it distorts output even in the equilibrium where the bureau carries no internal slack whatsoever. Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's firm adds layers because doing so minimises the cost of producing a larger output given the time constraint on knowledge, and Simon's complex system becomes hierarchic because non-hierarchic systems of that size cannot be assembled at all — growth in structure is functional, a response to scale rather than an appropriation of it. The two readings hand an observer of the same org chart opposite diagnoses.

**Extended to three mechanisms [institutional-evolution, 2026-08-19].** Meyer & Rowan and DiMaggio & Powell supply a third account that is neither: formal structure is adopted as **ceremony**, because conformity to externally legitimated models buys legitimacy, resources and survival — explicitly independent of both output and budget. Filed here rather than as a separate conflict, because it is a third answer to the same question ("what is observed structure tracking?") and the discriminating tests overlap. Note it does *not* simply reinforce Position A: legitimacy-seeking is not rent extraction by the bureau head, and it predicts growth in **private** organisations insulated from output tests just as readily as in public ones.

## Position A — growth is rent extraction, and low internal waste proves nothing

**Source:** `raw/research/scale-effects/03-niskanen-peculiar-economics-bureaucracy.md` (Niskanen 1968).
**Evidence tier:** (b) formal deductive model with an invented numerical illustration. Niskanen's own Section V proposes critical tests — Social Security Administration against insurance companies, public against private hospitals, police, refuse collection, statistics-gathering — and states they have not been run, and will be hard to run cleanly.

- The bureaucrat maximises total budget, from which salary, perquisites, reputation, power, patronage and ease of managing all flow; the constraint is only that budget covers minimum cost at the chosen output.
- The lump-sum exchange of output for budget, rather than sale at a per-unit price, gives the bureau an all-or-nothing position against its sponsor and lets it appropriate the entire consumer surplus.
- Result under the illustrative demand and cost functions: bureau output 166.7 against a competitive industry's 100, and 200 where the bureau also discriminates among factor suppliers. Surplus is shifted from consumers to owners of specific factors; under monopsony it is fully dissipated.
- **The load-bearing point for this conflict:** in the budget-constrained equilibrium the budget exactly covers minimum cost. There is no fat, no idle capacity, nothing an efficiency audit would find — and output is still well past the social optimum. Growth can be pathological with zero observable waste.
- Bureaucrats also spend "a significant part of their time on various promotional activities," steepening their own demand curve by asserting need or requirement "regardless of cost" — growth manufactured rather than met.

## Position B — added structure is a cost-minimising response to scale

**Sources:** `raw/research/scale-effects/08-garicano-rossi-hansberg-knowledge-hierarchies.md` (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg 2014); `raw/research/scale-effects/07-simon-architecture-of-complexity.md` (Simon 1962).
**Evidence tier:** (b) formal models. Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg add (a) associational firm-panel evidence — French administrative data, 553,125 firm-year observations, with firm fixed effects — which the authors describe as failing to falsify the mechanism rather than confirming it. Simon's social applications are self-disclaimed.

- Layers exist to route problems to whoever has invested in solving that class of problem; span is bounded by the manager's helping-time constraint. Both are outputs of cost minimisation, not choices.
- The cost function is U-shaped in output with minimum efficient scale rising in layer count, so a larger organisation is *cheaper per unit* with more layers. Deeper is not the same as more wasteful.
- The reorganisation result predicts a specific, checkable signature: growth by adding a layer cuts the wages and knowledge of pre-existing layers; growth without adding one raises them. Both are efficient responses to the same demand shock at different scales.
- Simon: past a complexity threshold, only hierarchic systems assemble in tractable time at all — depth is a precondition of large scale, not a symptom of it.
- Neither source contains a rent-extraction channel. There is no actor in either model whose payoff rises with structure for its own sake.

## Position C — added structure is ceremony, and tracks legitimacy rather than either output or budget

**Sources:** `raw/research/institutional-evolution/05-meyer-rowan-institutionalized-organizations.md` (Meyer & Rowan 1977); `raw/research/institutional-evolution/04-dimaggio-powell-iron-cage.md` (DiMaggio & Powell 1983).
**Evidence tier:** (b), and weaker than either of the other two positions. Meyer & Rowan is a conceptual essay with hand-picked illustrations — no model, no data, no systematic comparison. DiMaggio & Powell is a theoretical synthesis whose authors state outright that "an empirical test of such predictions is beyond the scope of this paper"; its empirical content is citation to others' work (Zucker & Tolbert on civil-service reform diffusion, Knoke's event-history analysis, Marshall Meyer on urban fiscal agencies).

- Meyer & Rowan, Proposition 1: as rationalised institutional rules arise in a domain, "formal organizations form and expand by incorporating these rules as structural elements" — **independent of the rules' effect on efficiency**. Proposition 2 states the elaboration holds "even with the complexity of immediate relational networks held constant", which is precisely the variable Position B's mechanism runs on.
- Proposition 3 gives the payoff and it is real, not imagined: legitimacy, resources and survival, conferred by external constituents who extend trust "based on conformity to legitimated form, not verified output". Legitimated organisations get easier loans, donations, investment, and quasi-automatic public funding.
- DiMaggio & Powell add the field-level engine: coercive pressure from dependence and legal mandate, mimetic copying under uncertainty, and normative diffusion through professionalisation and credentialed hiring. Their central negative claim: "None of this... insures that conformist organizations do what they do more efficiently than do their more deviant peers." They argue Weber's market-competition engine of bureaucratisation has been replaced by **the state and the professions**.
- Meyer & Rowan's economic mechanism is sharper than the rest of their paper and directly relevant here: modern accounting builds **"ceremonial production functions"** in which units with no measurable contribution to output — advertising, safety, econometrics staff — get "shadow prices" from their external market cost rather than their marginal product, and their internal power "rises with their performance on ceremonial measures".
- **Neither source contains a budget-maximisation channel or an output channel.** There is no actor whose payoff rises with budget as such, and no production function whose cost is being minimised. The payoff is survival through legitimacy.

**[wiki synthesis]** Position C's most useful property for this conflict is that its scope condition is stated by its own authors and cuts *across* the public/private line rather than along it. Meyer & Rowan's driving variable is explicitly "whether an organisation's survival depends primarily on relational or on institutional demands" — not sector. Their own examples of highly institutionalised *private* bodies are Lockheed, Penn Central and rate-regulated utilities. That is the same relocation Niskanen's footnote 3 and Bozeman both performed on their own axes, from a third direction.

## Where the disagreement actually is

Not on whether large institutions are ever wasteful — Position B simply has no term for it, and Position A does not deny that some coordination is genuinely needed. The disagreement is over **what to infer from observed size and depth**:

- Position A's default: a bureau at its observed size is at the size its budget incentive drove it to; absence of visible slack is not evidence to the contrary, because the pathology is allocative rather than operational.
- Position B's default: an organisation at its observed size and depth is at the structure that minimises the cost of its output; deeper hierarchies and lower wages at the bottom are what efficient growth looks like.
- Position C's default: an organisation at its observed size and depth has the structure its institutional environment currently confers legitimacy on. Both other defaults are wrong about what the structure is *for*, and the structure may not be coupled to the work at all.

**Where the three separate.** Each predicts structural growth under a different condition, and the conditions are independently observable:

| | Growth predicted when | Predicted relation of structure to actual work | What an efficiency audit finding no waste means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A — rent** | The bureau faces a lump-sum-funding sponsor it can extract from | Coupled; the output is real, just oversupplied | Nothing — the pathology is allocative |
| **B — knowledge cost** | Output rises, or information/communication costs shift | Tightly coupled by construction | The structure is right |
| **C — legitimacy** | The institutional environment elaborates a new rationalised myth, whatever output does | **Possibly decoupled entirely** — that is Meyer & Rowan's central claim | The audit itself is the anomaly: Position C predicts institutionalised organisations *minimise* inspection and evaluation |

The last column is the sharpest practical difference. Positions A and B both assume an audit is possible and disagree about what it would show. **Position C predicts the audit will not happen**, because evaluation publicly asserts that people are not competent or acting in good faith by default, and so threatens the legitimacy the ceremonial structure exists to produce (Meyer & Rowan, Proposition 6).

The practical stake is diagnostic. Position A says an audit finding no waste has found nothing. Position B says a firm that grew, added a layer, and downgraded its workforce did exactly the right thing.

## The likely resolution, not adopted

**[wiki synthesis — flagged, not endorsed]** The most plausible reading is that this is a **scope condition rather than a contradiction**: Position B's models are built on a competitive labour market and a market-priced output q, and Position A's is built on a lump-sum budget with a single sponsor and no market test of output. If so, the operative variable is the funding form ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D35) rather than public or private ownership, and each model is right inside its own regime.

**No source states this.** Niskanen never engages knowledge-based hierarchy (it postdates him by decades); Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg never mention bureaus; Simon asserts that firms, governments and universities are the same kind of object and tests nothing. The reconciliation is this wiki's inference, and it is exactly the kind of inference the wiki elsewhere refuses to treat as evidence. It is recorded as the hypothesis to test, not the answer.

## What would settle it

1. **Run Position B's model on an institution funded by lump-sum appropriation.** Does layer count still track output, where output is unpriced? The knowledge-hierarchy model needs a q; a bureau supplies none. If layer count tracks budget rather than output, Position A's mechanism is doing the work.
2. **Test the reorganisation signature in a bureau.** The firm-level result — layer added ⇒ pre-existing layers' pay and knowledge fall; no layer added ⇒ they rise — is a sharp prediction. Running it on public-sector personnel records would discriminate directly, and the data class exists.
3. **Find an organisation that changed funding form without changing ownership.** A body moving from appropriation to per-unit charging, or the reverse, would separate funding mechanism from public/private status. Niskanen's own footnote 3 names candidates — post offices, universities, hospitals, all "mixed" by his account.
4. **A source that models both.** Nothing in this batch does. Four sources are firm-only, two bureau-only, and the two that assert invariance across the line (Simon, Coase's 1991 lecture) test nothing.
5. **[added for Position C] Check whether the structure is coupled to the work at all** before asking which growth account explains it. [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D56 gives the procedure: sample formal structural elements and report the fraction actually invoked in decisions. A low fraction is direct evidence for C and against both A and B, which each assume the structure does something. This is the cheapest of the five tests and nobody has run it on a bureau.
6. **[added for Position C] Test whether structural elaboration tracks the institutional environment with output held constant.** Meyer & Rowan's Proposition 2 makes this exact claim. Two organisations with the same output and the same relational network complexity, in domains where the rationalised-myth environment differs in density (measurable via professional bodies, accreditation regimes, mandated reporting categories), should differ in structural elaboration if C is right and not if A or B is.
7. **[added for Position C] Note that C is itself internally split** on what happens under the ceremonial structure — DiMaggio & Powell predict substantive homogenisation, Meyer & Rowan predict loose coupling and internal variation. See [[homogenisation-vs-loose-coupling]]. Position C is a united front on why structure grows, not on what growth does to the work, so any test using it must state which limb it invokes.
