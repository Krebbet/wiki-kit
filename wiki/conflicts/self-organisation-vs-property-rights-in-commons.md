# Conflict: Self-Organisation vs. Property Rights in the Commons

**Status: OPEN — not adjudicated.** Ostrom's evidence says user self-organisation routinely outperforms both state management and privatisation in governing common-pool resources, and that imposing external rules can actively destroy the voluntary cooperation it replaces. Libecap's evidence says private bargaining over commons resources reliably fails through holdout and party heterogeneity, that the political fallback fails through capture and discretion-preservation, and that the remedy is a clear, secure, transferable property right — with New Zealand's perpetual fishing quota as the exemplar. Both are careful empirical researchers reporting cases they know well. The likely resolution is a scope condition rather than a refutation, but that resolution has to be argued and measured, not assumed, so the conflict is left open.

## Position A — Ostrom: self-organised, user-governed institutions, tailored locally

**Source:** `raw/research/foundations-nie/03-ostrom-nobel-polycentric.md` (Nobel lecture, 2009).
**Evidence tier:** (a) the strongest empirical layer in the foundations set — controlled lab experiments with clean causal identification, replicated in field experiments in Colombia and Brazil; plus correlational meta-analyses of coded cases (47, later 229, Nepal irrigation systems; 44 fishery subgroups; 152–178 forests). The design principles themselves are (b), derived inductively and confirmed in about two-thirds of 100+ reviewed studies.

- Farmer-managed Nepal irrigation systems outperform government-managed ones (72% of 25 vs. 40% of 12 achieving high performance), on physical condition, tail-end water availability, and rice yield.
- Legal protection status does not predict forest condition (Hayes 2006, 76 protected vs. 87 other forests); local monitoring does.
- Self-designed sanctioning reached ~90% of optimal returns in lab CPR games — the best of any tested condition; externally imposed sanctioning options were overused and *reduced* net returns.
- **Crowding out**: an externally imposed rule increased withdrawal relative to communication-only in a Colombian field experiment (Cardenas, Stranlund & Willis 2000).
- Explicit rejection of one-size-fits-all prescriptions, naming blanket ITQ adoption among them: even a well-regarded mechanism "involves multiple years of hard work" to tailor locally.

## Position B — Libecap: private bargaining fails; assign secure transferable rights

**Source:** `raw/research/foundations-nie/04-nber-north-transaction-costs.md` (NBER WP 24585, 2018). The material used here is **Libecap's own primary case work**, not his second-hand summaries of North.
**Evidence tier:** (a) archival and historical case evidence with quantified magnitudes, drawing on his and coauthors' peer-reviewed empirical work, plus one genuinely large quasi-experimental study (Costello, Gaines & Lynham 2008: 11,135 fisheries, 1950–2003) and a 445-basin California groundwater study (Ayres, Edwards & Libecap 2018).

- Private bargaining over oil-field unitization broke down or dragged 4–9 years across seven Texas fields; holdout by parties with favourable positions or private information is the named mechanism.
- Agreement typically arrives only after attrition has homogenised the remaining parties — i.e. after most of the rents are already gone. ITQ systems in fisheries were adopted "when fish stocks are near collapse."
- Catch-share and TAC regimes halted or reversed stock declines in the 11,135-fishery study — the strongest quasi-experimental evidence in either position.
- Right *security* is measurable and consequential: New Zealand's perpetual, collateralisable quota vs. US quota explicitly "not property rights" under the Magnuson-Stevens Act, with a lease/sale price ratio implying markets discount for ~50% probability of erosion.
- Fragmentation of governance units is named as a failure mode: groundwater basins split into multiple sub-basin management areas, reducing basin-wide effectiveness — which is close to the inverse of Ostrom's nesting principle.

## Where the disagreement actually is

Three distinguishable claims are tangled together, and the sources do not separate them:

1. **Does user self-organisation work?** Ostrom: often, under enumerable conditions. Libecap: not for the resources he studies, where it stalls for years and completes only after the value is lost.
2. **Is external imposition harmful?** Ostrom: it can crowd out voluntary cooperation and reduce net returns. Libecap: the state's involvement is harmful mainly because it takes the *wrong form* — discretionary regulation instead of secure transferable rights — not because state involvement as such destroys cooperation.
3. **Are ITQs a solution or a one-size-fits-all error?** Libecap treats New Zealand's ITQ regime as the benchmark. Ostrom names blanket ITQ prescription as an example of the fit failure she warns against. They are not obviously contradicting each other — Ostrom's objection is to blanket adoption without local tailoring, and Libecap emphasises the *security* of the right rather than the tradability alone — but the emphasis is genuinely opposed.

**The likely axis of resolution [wiki synthesis]:** the two case bases sit at opposite ends of dimensions the register already carries — D16 (number and heterogeneity of parties), D23 (resource boundability, observability, heterogeneity, value), and D14 (polycentricity). Ostrom's cases are small-to-medium, boundable, moderate-value resources with relatively homogeneous users; Libecap's are large, high-value, migratory or subsurface resources with many heterogeneous claimants. That is a scope condition, and if it holds it dissolves most of the conflict. But neither source states it, both generalise beyond their case base, and treating it as settled would be the wiki smoothing a real disagreement.

## What would settle it

1. **Locate the crossover empirically.** Both bodies of work code cases in ways that could in principle be pooled: party count, party cost heterogeneity, resource value, boundary observability. Fit self-organisation success against those variables across both case sets and find where the sign flips. Neither author has done this.
2. **Test the crowding-out result on Libecap-type resources.** Ostrom's crowding-out evidence is from small-group field and lab settings. If externally assigned transferable rights also crowd out voluntary cooperation in large heterogeneous resources, Position B's remedy carries a hidden cost it has not priced.
3. **Check whether the design principles predict outcomes in Libecap's cases.** Ostrom's principles are claimed to travel; Libecap's oil, groundwater and large-fishery cases are exactly the setting where they have not been applied. Retrospective coding of those cases against the eight principles is a direct test.
4. **Separate right-security from right-tradability.** Libecap's own strongest measure (the lease/sale ratio) is about security, not tradability. If security is what does the work, Ostrom's design principle 7 (state recognition of local rule-making) and Libecap's transferable right are the *same* prescription at different levels, and the conflict is much narrower than it appears.
