# Conflict: Constraint on the Ruler vs. Capacity Plus Conditional Discipline

**Status: OPEN — not adjudicated.** The secondary conflict of the `institutions-and-growth` batch, and independent of the identification dispute filed at [[does-institutions-growth-survive-identification]]: even if the settler-mortality result were cleanly identified, this question would still be open, because it is about *which institutional property* secures long-horizon investment, not about whether the estimate is credible.

It is also a **direct tension with an existing wiki page.** [[credible-commitment]] currently states, following North, that capital markets "will simply not evolve where political rulers can arbitrarily seize assets", that self-restraint "was seldom successful for very long", and that the durable solution is structural shackling. Wade's fastest-growing cases were executives who plainly could seize assets and were not shackled.

## Position A — investment requires that the ruler *cannot* expropriate

**The claim.** Secure long-horizon investment requires a structural constraint on the power-holder, not a policy choice by them. A promise not to expropriate is unenforceable against the only party with enforcement power, so the remedy must be external and structural.

**Sources:**

- **North**, via [[credible-commitment]] — *(b) framework, with the demonstration offloaded to North & Weingast (1989), which this wiki has not read directly.* Two candidate solutions, one dismissed: self-restraint fails "in the face of... fiscal crises"; structural shackling works, exemplified by the Glorious Revolution and parliamentary supremacy.
- **Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson** — *(b) theory, (a) contested empirics.* The whole extractive/inclusive scheme runs on constraints on power-holders and breadth of the power-holding group as the growth-producing properties. Their comparative statics list constraints first. The holdup channel is North's mechanism formalised as a commitment problem. [[institutions-as-fundamental-cause]]
- **Libecap**, via [[credible-commitment]] — *(a) empirical, his own case work, and the only measured evidence on this side.* US fishery quotas, revocable at government discretion, show roughly **double** New Zealand's lease-to-sale price ratio for perpetual rights — a market-priced estimate that participants discount for a substantial probability the entitlement will be eroded. This is direct evidence that *revocability itself* is priced, independent of any ruler's current behaviour.

**The strongest form of Position A**, steelmanned: the mechanism is logically airtight — a promise from the party who controls enforcement is not a commitment — and it is the only side of this conflict with a measured, market-generated number attached.

## Position B — capacity plus *discretionary* conditionality secures investment, and constraint is neither necessary nor the operative variable

**The claim.** What produces investment is a state with the capacity to direct credit, protection and R&D toward targeted sectors *and* the discretion to withdraw that support when firms fail to perform. The discipline runs from the state onto capital, not from a constitution onto the state. On this account, generic constraint is at best orthogonal and at worst obstructive, because it removes the selectivity the mechanism depends on.

**Sources:**

- **Wade** — *(a) one quasi-experimental result inside (b) comparative-historical argument.* South Korea, Taiwan and Singapore achieved the fastest sustained non-Western catch-up on record under one-party rule and military dictatorship, with labour actively excluded from the "embedded" relationship. His discriminating variable is **performance-conditionality**: East Asian assistance withdrawn on failure to close price and quality gaps, against India's and the Philippines' unconditional protection, which he calls "the route to large-scale corruption and slow economic development". Lane (2017) on Korea's 1973–79 HCI drive is the one quasi-experimental anchor: targeted sectors grew faster in output and productivity, with the differential persisting after the policy ended. [[developmental-state-and-embedded-autonomy]]
- **Glaeser, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer** — *(a) empirical, arriving from the opposite ideological direction.* Their measurement finding *is* this claim: the standard indices score a ruler who chooses not to expropriate identically to one who cannot, and their high-growth cases — Singapore, South Korea, post-1978 China — are the self-restraint cases North dismissed. Pinochet's Chile scores the worst possible Polity constraint score with relatively secure property.
- **Rodrik, Subramanian & Trebbi** — *(a) empirical, from inside the affirmative camp.* Their China/Russia contrast: Chinese entrepreneurs invested heavily with no formal private property under a socialist legal system; Russian investors stayed wary under a formal Western-style property-rights regime. Their own reading is that *credible* commitment matters more than nominal legal form — which concedes that the formal-constraint variable is not the operative one.
- **Besley & Persson** — *(b) formal model.* Supplies the other limb: capacity is a chosen investment, and the thing suppressing it is a ruler's expectation of *losing power*, not the absence of constraint on the ruler. Their investment trap predicts that raising turnover risk lowers state capacity — which, read across, predicts that the stable authoritarian cases Wade describes should build more capacity than unstable constrained ones. [[endogenous-state-capacity]]

**The strongest form of Position B**, steelmanned: the cases where investment actually surged fastest are the cases scoring lowest on the constraint variable, this is observed by four sources with incompatible priors — a heterodox industrial-policy advocate, a legal-origins market-liberal team, and two mainstream affirmative-camp papers — and none of them was looking for it.

## What the disagreement actually is

|  | Position A | Position B |
|---|---|---|
| What secures investment | The power-holder's inability to expropriate | The power-holder's demonstrated pattern of conditional discipline plus capacity to deliver |
| Where the discipline points | Constitution → ruler | State → firms |
| Status of discretion | The problem; must be removed | The instrument; must be preserved and used |
| Best evidence | Libecap's lease-to-sale ratio (measured, priced, cross-national on one asset class) | Lane's Korean sector panel (quasi-experimental, one country, one policy episode) |
| Cases it cannot explain | Taiwan, South Korea, Singapore, post-1978 China | Why revocable US fishing quotas price at half the value of perpetual New Zealand ones |
| Failure mode when it goes wrong | Expropriation; capital flight | Unconditional rent capture; India's Ambassador car; Philippine patronage |

**[wiki synthesis]** The two positions are not symmetric in scope, and that is the most promising route to a resolution nobody has argued. Position A's evidence is about **whether a specific entitlement will be honoured** — an asset-level question, and Libecap measures it at asset level. Position B's evidence is about **whether a state can direct and discipline an investment programme** — a sectoral question, and Lane measures it at sector level. It is entirely possible that both hold: an East Asian firm receiving conditional credit faces high policy risk in the sense Position A measures and still invests, because the state is simultaneously supplying capital, demand and protection that alter the calculation. Nobody in this batch says this, so it is a hypothesis to test, not an adopted resolution.

**[wiki synthesis]** A second candidate resolution, also unargued: **selectivity is itself a commitment device.** Wade's conditionality only disciplines if the withdrawal threat is credible, which requires the state to have withdrawn support before, visibly. That is a reputational commitment mechanism built from repeated behaviour rather than from structural constraint — which is exactly the "self-restraint" North dismissed, but with an enforcement mechanism (reputation across repeated interactions with a small set of large firms) that North's fiscally pressed monarch did not have. If that is right, the disagreement is about *which* commitment technologies are available at what scale, not about whether commitment is needed.

## A third case, added without resolving anything [`comparative-governing-philosophies`, 2026-08-21]

**[wiki synthesis]** [[koreas-economic-planning-board]] describes a mechanism that is neither Position A's structural shackling nor cleanly Position B's state-disciplines-firms conditionality: Korea's Economic Planning Board fused planning, budget and foreign-capital authority in one ministry, with almost no external legal constraint on the EPB itself, and was disciplined instead by a single external veto point — President Park, personally, via a Monthly Economic Trend Report Meeting he chaired himself. The source's own comparative case (India's Planning Commission, which held planning authority *without* budget control and, per the source, failed to coordinate investment with its own plan) reads as evidence *for* concentration rather than against it — the opposite of what Position A's decision-rights-decomposition instinct would predict. This does not adjudicate Position A vs. B: it adds a third accountability channel — **concentrated authority under a single, personally-committed, informally-supervising principal** — that neither position's evidence currently measures. The source itself treats this as non-replicable and contingent (the EPB was abolished in 1994 once Korea democratised and its single-veto-point model no longer fit), which is itself informative: it suggests personalised supervision may substitute for structural constraint only under conditions (stable single-party or single-leader tenure) that Position A's own cases (US, UK) never had to rely on. See [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D131 for the related personnel-tournament mechanism this case's China counterpart ([[regionally-decentralized-authoritarianism]]) uses instead of personal presidential supervision.

## Consequences for existing pages

**[wiki synthesis]** [[credible-commitment]] has been annotated rather than rewritten. Its North material is unchanged and its Libecap measurement stands; a section now records that its central claim faces a case set it does not address, and points here. The page's own stated settlement criterion — cases where ruler-constraint was imposed *without* the accompanying commercial and legal changes, and cases of constraint being *removed* — is unaffected and still the right test for the North limb. Wade's cases supply a third test the page did not name: **sustained investment under an unconstrained executive**, which the North mechanism predicts should not happen and which happened at record scale.

## What would settle it

**[wiki synthesis]** Nothing in this batch can, and the reason is worth stating: Position A's evidence and Position B's evidence are measured on different objects, in different currencies, at different levels. The discriminating designs:

1. **Run Libecap's price-ratio measure inside a developmental state.** If East Asian firms' state-granted entitlements — directed credit lines, import protection, licence allocations — were tradable, their lease-to-sale ratio would be a market estimate of how credible the commitment behind them was. High revocability *by design* (conditionality) plus high investment would be the sharp result. This is the cheapest test either side could run and neither has.
2. **Separate the two limbs of conditionality.** Wade's mechanism requires both information density with the client and observable disciplining instances. Score them separately, before the growth outcome is known, across a set of industrial-policy episodes including failures — the readmission condition recorded at [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D94. Wade's own Bank of England counter-case is the test any such instrument must classify correctly in advance.
3. **A timing test.** Did the constraint precede the investment surge or follow it? Glaeser et al.'s sequencing design (Table 12) is the right shape and finds institutions following, not leading — but it is run on schooling, not on conditionality, and on national indices, not on either side's actual mechanism.
