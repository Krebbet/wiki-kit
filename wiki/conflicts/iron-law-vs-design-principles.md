# Conflict: Michels vs. Ostrom on Whether Participatory Design Prevents Oligarchic Drift

**Status: OPEN.** Not a strict contradiction — the two bodies of evidence measure different things — but a genuine unfilled gap the wiki should not paper over by treating either source as settling the other's question.

## Position A — organisation itself produces oligarchy, participatory design notwithstanding (Michels)

**Source:** `raw/research/power-and-accountability/07-michels-iron-law-oligarchy.md` — Robert Michels, *Political Parties*, 1911/1915.
**Evidence tier: [model]**, illustrative historical case material (chiefly the German SPD), not systematic data.

"Who says organization, says oligarchy." Michels argues four cumulative mechanisms — technical necessity of delegation, mass apathy and leader-veneration, leaders' intellectual/informational advantage, and bureaucratic professionalisation of the leadership stratum — drive even the most democratically-founded, anti-oligarchic organisations (European socialist parties, trade unions) toward concentrated leadership control over time. He explicitly tests and rejects specific accountability remedies: the referendum, syndicalist structure, and anarchist organisation all reproduce the same pattern in his case material. His hedge is partial and stated once: rising mass education might offset the tendency, but never eliminates it.

## Position B — participatory design principles predict durable, functioning commons governance (Ostrom)

**Source:** captured across the `foundations-nie` batch; empirical backing from Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás (2010), reviewed on [[polycentric-governance]].
**Evidence tier: [empirical]** — 91 studies, 77 cases, mean support 3.73/5 for Ostrom's eight design principles, every principle positively correlated with commons-system survival (nesting weakest, at the 10% level).

Ostrom's design principles — clearly defined boundaries, congruence between rules and local conditions, collective-choice arrangements (those affected by a rule can participate in modifying it), monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict-resolution mechanisms, minimal recognition of rights to organise, and nesting — are associated with **survival of the commons-governance system itself**, tested across a large, real, cross-national body of cases.

## What the disagreement actually is, and is not

**Not a strict contradiction.** Cox et al. measure whether a commons-governance system *survives* over time; Michels's claim is about whether the *internal distribution of decision-making power* inside a surviving organisation concentrates toward a leadership stratum. A commons institution could score well on every design principle and still be internally oligarchic in exactly Michels's sense — collective-choice arrangements (principle 3, "those affected participate in rule modification") formally satisfy participatory accountability without ruling out the informational and organisational asymmetries Michels describes as driving real control toward a professionalised few. **No source in this wiki has measured internal power concentration inside a long-running, design-principle-compliant commons institution.**

**What would settle it.** A design comparable to Cox et al.'s survival meta-analysis, but scoring internal decision-making concentration (who actually proposes, amends, and enforces rules in practice, against who is formally entitled to) rather than system survival — run across the same or a comparable case set. Absent that, Position A and Position B are not in direct tension; they are answers to two different questions the wiki has so far only asked separately.
