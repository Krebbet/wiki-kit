# Institutional Myths and Decoupling

Meyer & Rowan's claim is that formal organisational structure is frequently adopted **not for technical coordination but as ceremony** — a reflection of "rationalized myths" circulating in the institutional environment (professions, law, public opinion, the credentialing system) — and that conforming to those myths buys legitimacy, resources and survival independent of whether it improves the work. The consequence is the paper's central concept: because ceremonial structure conflicts with the case-specific demands of actual production, organisations **decouple** formal structure from real activity, and sustain the gap with a "logic of confidence and good faith" that systematically avoids inspection and evaluation. The scope condition is stated by the authors and must travel with the claim: this describes organisations whose survival depends on **institutional** rather than **relational** demands — bodies not disciplined by a real output test. It is not a general theory of organisations.

**Evidence tier.** (b) conceptual essay with hand-picked illustrations — **the weakest tier in this wiki's hierarchy.** No model, no regression, no systematic case comparison, no original data. The evidence is citation to others' historical work (Callahan and Tyack on US school administration, Selznick on the TVA, Zald & Denton on the YMCA) plus the authors' own chosen anecdotes: Lockheed and Penn Central surviving near-bankruptcy through state entanglement, No Smoking signs, Nobel Prize hires, econometric consultants, defence contractors paid for following procedures. The paper closes by *proposing* comparative and experimental studies that had not been done. Weight it as a mechanism worth having, not as a finding.

## The six propositions

**[framework]** Each carries an identified mechanism, and every mechanism routes through **legitimacy** rather than through efficiency, output quality, or budget bargaining. This is offered explicitly as an *alternative causal channel* to the Weberian coordination-and-control account of bureaucratisation, not as a refinement of it.

1. **Rationalised institutional rules arise in a domain → formal organisations form and expand by incorporating those rules as structural elements, independent of the rules' effect on efficiency.** Mechanism: legitimacy and resource gains from being isomorphic with the myths of the institutional environment.
2. **The more modernised the society, the more extended the rationalised institutional structure and the greater the number of domains containing rationalised institutions.** Mechanism: rise of rational-legal order and centralised states. This is the one proposition operating at the environment level, and it is asserted rather than modelled — it functions as an input to the other five. The authors explicitly decline to theorise why the myth-system itself evolves: "the evolution of the whole modern institutional system... is beyond the scope of this paper."
3. **Incorporating societally legitimated rationalised elements maximises legitimacy, resources and survival.** Mechanism: external constituents — investors, funders, courts, employees — extend trust, support and protection from failure on the basis of conformity to legitimated form, **not on verified output**.
4. **Attempts to tightly coordinate and control activity in institutionalised organisations produce conflict and legitimacy loss, so structural elements are decoupled from activities and from each other.** Mechanism: categorical ceremonial rules conflict with case-specific technical efficiency, and myths arriving from a pluralistic environment conflict with each other, so tight coupling would publicly expose both the inefficiency and the inconsistency.
5. **The more structure derives from institutional myths, the more elaborate the displays of confidence, satisfaction and good faith.** Mechanism: Goffman-style face-work — avoidance, discretion, overlooking — required to sustain the fiction that decoupled structures are really working.
6. **Institutionalised organisations minimise inspection and evaluation, by internal managers and external constituents alike.** Mechanism: evaluation is itself a public assertion that people are *not* competent or acting in good faith by default, so it directly threatens the legitimacy the ceremonial structure exists to produce.

**[wiki synthesis]** Proposition 6 is the load-bearing one for this wiki, because it predicts an observable: an institutionalised organisation should suppress or fail to generate performance data about itself. The authors give the examples — hospitals suppressing cure-rate data, schools de-emphasising achievement measures — and describe the general move as substituting categorical ends for technical ends so that "data on technical performance are eliminated or rendered invisible". That behaviour is *also* predicted, from an entirely different mechanism, by [[multitask-incentive-theory]] (don't measure what distorts effort) and anticipated by [[blame-avoidance-and-negativity-bias]] (Hood, twenty years later, from individual blame risk). Three causal stories, one observable. None of them has been tested against the others.

## The scope condition: production vs. institutionalised organisations

**[framework — keep this attached to every claim above]** Meyer & Rowan state the boundary of their own theory as a continuum, not a binary, and the driving variable is explicitly *not* sector:

> whether an organisation's survival depends primarily on **relational** demands (real customers, real inspection, real output tests) or on **institutional** demands (legitimacy conferred by accrediting bodies, funders, courts, professions, public opinion).

At the production end sit organisations with clear routine technology, evaluable output, and a real market whose consumers have inspection rights — these are disciplined by "output controls" (their citation is Ouchi & McGuire) and the myth-and-ceremony mechanism is weak. At the institutionalised end sit schools, hospitals, welfare organisations, school districts — and, they insist, **private firms too** once those firms secure comparable insulation. Lockheed and Penn Central are their examples of private bodies occupying the same highly-institutionalised position as public agencies; state-regulated utilities with rates guaranteeing profit regardless of cost are another; defence contractors "paid for following agreed-on procedures, even if the product is ineffective" are a third. Business personnel, advertising and R&D departments sit there inside otherwise production-oriented firms.

**This is a public/private invariance claim with a stated mechanism**, and it is one of the better-specified ones the wiki has: sector membership is the wrong variable, exposure to output-based market tests is the right one. It is consistent with, and supplies a candidate mechanism for, the finding on [[presumed-vs-measured-public-sector-risk-aversion]] that sector alone does not predict behaviour. It is asserted, not tested. See [[open-questions]] Q3.

## Ceremonial production functions and rent by legitimacy

**[framework]** The economic-production claim is sharper than the rest of the paper. Modern accounting creates **"ceremonial production functions"** mapped onto the real one: units with no measurable contribution to output — advertising, safety, econometrics staff — are assigned "shadow prices" taken from their external market cost rather than their marginal product, and their internal power "rises with their performance on ceremonial measures" (citing Salancik & Pfeffer). Assessment criteria become externally derived — a Nobel Prize hire, a prestigious consultant — rather than output-based.

Institutionalisation also channels resources directly: legitimated organisations get easier loans, donations and investment, and quasi-automatic public funding, independent of output. "Apart from whether schools educate students, or hospitals cure patients, people and governmental agencies remain committed to these organizations, funding and using them almost automatically." This is a **rent-creation-by-legitimacy** mechanism, distinct from both market rents and classic regulatory capture.

## Accountability, suppressed by mutual agreement

**[framework]** Decision rights over formal structure sit partly with organisational leadership — who can actively *shape* the institutional environment, as automobile producers lobby over legal and public-opinion standards — and partly are ceded upward to "corporately organized agents of society": accrediting agencies, boards of trustees, government agencies, courts, professional licensing bodies. Accountability runs to the **appearance of conformity**.

The distinctive part is that external constituents *also* avoid inspecting, preferring "categorical relationships". The nominal accountability channel is suppressed by mutual agreement between the organisation and its overseers — not evaded by one party against the other. That is a sharper statement of the nominal-versus-actual accountability gap than the wiki has from anywhere else, and it bears directly on [[open-questions]] Q11.

**[framework — an explicit rejection worth recording]** Meyer & Rowan reject the "domesticated organisation" and pure-capture reading of their own material, citing and arguing *against* Carlson, Clark, Selznick and Zald & Denton: organisations are often active shapers of their institutional environment, not passive captives to it. Do not fold this page into a capture account.

## Structural inconsistency

**[framework]** A pluralistic institutional environment forces organisations to incorporate mutually incompatible elements — overlapping professional jurisdictions, competing programmes claiming authority over the same domain. Their illustration: "if one inquires who decides what curricula will be taught in schools, any number of parties... may say that they decide." This looks like veto-point gridlock and is a different mechanism — myth pluralism rather than formal veto design. Keep it separate from [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]] rather than merging the two counts.

## No age or size evidence, and the authors say so

**[wiki synthesis — the priority theme]** This source supplies **nothing** on organisational age or size, and should not be cited for either.

- "Size" appears exactly once in the paper, and only inside their summary of the *rival* Weberian theory they are arguing against ("such factors as size [Blau 1970] and technology [Woodward 1965] increase the complexity of internal relations"). It is not part of their own mechanism.
- Every elapsed-time claim is loose, secondhand, or both. The diffuse historical claim that formal-structure elaboration accompanies "modern states and societies" carries no interval. The one concrete historical claim — that American schools "evolved from producing rather specific training that was evaluated according to strict criteria of efficiency to producing ambiguously defined services that are evaluated according to criteria of certification" — is sourced entirely to Callahan, Tyack and the authors' own unpublished paper, with no dates and no rate. It is nonetheless interesting, because it is a claim about an entire field **migrating along the production/institutionalised continuum over historical time**, which is the shape of claim the wiki wants and has almost none of.
- The authors identify the gap themselves. Their closing Research Implications section flags that nobody had yet studied "variations in organizational structure... within any society across time".

See [[open-questions]] Q7.

## The authors' own posture

**[framework]** Unusually agnostic for this literature, and it changes how the page should be read. Meyer & Rowan explicitly decline to characterise ceremonial decoupling as dysfunction: "No position is taken here on the overall social effectiveness of isomorphic and loosely coupled organizations... It should not be assumed that the creation of microscopic rationalities in the daily activity of workers effects social ends more efficiently than commitment to larger institutional claims and purposes." They close by suggesting decoupling **may maximise long-run effectiveness** through good-faith commitment.

**[wiki synthesis]** That is the opposite normative posture from [[red-tape]], and the contrast is useful. Bozeman defines red tape as rules that impose burden and achieve *nothing*; Meyer & Rowan's ceremonial structure imposes efficiency costs and achieves something real — legitimacy, resources, survival. Two accounts of similar-looking rule elaboration with opposite verdicts on whether it is functionless. Neither has measured against the other.

The only lever implicit in the paper follows from the scope condition rather than from any prescription: expose an organisation to genuine market-verified output evaluation and real consumer inspection rights, and its structure should tighten toward its activity. The authors neither test nor advocate this.

## The disagreement with DiMaggio & Powell

**[the cross-source point]** Same tradition, opposite prediction from the same antecedent. Under uncertainty and weak technology, Meyer & Rowan predict **loose coupling and internal variation**; DiMaggio & Powell predict **substantive homogenisation with less internal variation**, and say so in their own text at Hypothesis A-3 ("Here our prediction diverges somewhat from Meyer and Rowan"). See [[institutional-isomorphism]] and, for the conflict as stated, [[homogenisation-vs-loose-coupling]].

They agree on the other half, which is why the disagreement is worth isolating: both attribute organisational structure increasingly to state and institutional legitimacy rather than to technical or output requirements, and DiMaggio & Powell take Meyer & Rowan's own line that as rationalised states expand, organisations become "decreasingly held together by output controls" and increasingly organised around rituals of conformity.
