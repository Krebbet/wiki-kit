# Institutions as the Fundamental Cause

Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson's Handbook of Economic Growth chapter is the theory behind the settler-mortality regressions: economic institutions, not geography, culture or ideology, are the "fundamental cause" of cross-country income differences, and the institutions a society gets are chosen by whoever holds political power — **de jure** power allocated by political institutions, and **de facto** power derived from resources and solved collective action. The load-bearing move is a **commitment problem**: power-holders cannot credibly promise not to use future power in their own interest, so the side-payments that would let losers accept an efficiency-improving change are unenforceable, and efficiency and distribution collapse into a single choice. Three failure channels follow — holdup, political losers, economic losers — of which the authors rank political losers strongest and economic losers weakest, on their own evidence. **The chapter's most important sentence for this wiki is the authors' own verdict on it:** "the framework we outlined was largely verbal rather than mathematical, and thus, by its very nature, not fully specified."

**Evidence tier note.** This is (b) — a synthesis-and-theory chapter, not a new empirical result. Its two quantitative anchors are *restated, not re-run*: the settler-mortality IV and the reversal-of-fortune pattern are reproduced as scatterplots from the authors' own earlier papers, which are audited at [[colonial-origins-and-the-settler-mortality-instrument]]. Everything else is historical case narrative used to *illustrate* rather than test, drawn overwhelmingly from the authors' own prior work plus a small number of secondary historians (Haber, Bates, Brenner). The de jure/de facto power apparatus and the three commitment channels have **no independent empirical test anywhere in the chapter**. Treat the chapter as advocacy for one research programme rather than a neutral Handbook survey, and weight it as theory.

---

## The two claims

**[model]** **Claim 1: economic institutions → performance.** Better property-rights enforcement and broader access to opportunity raise investment in physical and human capital and in technology, which raises growth. This is asserted with citation, not derived; the chapter inherits it from North & Thomas and the wider New Institutional Economics.

**[model]** **Claim 2: political power → economic institutions.** Whichever group holds power chooses the economic institutions that serve its rents. Power has two sources with different persistence properties:

- **De jure power** — allocated by political institutions (constitutions, electoral rules, franchise). Durable, because the institutions that allocate it persist.
- **De facto power** — collective-action capacity plus economic resources, including the capacity for coercion. **Intrinsically transitory.**

**[model]** The asymmetry drives the chapter's most interesting prediction: a group with transitory de facto power has a strong incentive to convert it into durable de jure power while it lasts. AJR's illustration is Pinochet rewriting Chile's constitution in 1980 to entrench military power *after* de facto force had already let the military override the prior constitution — formal rules being rewritten by whoever currently holds informal power, precisely to make that power outlast the moment. Their franchise-extension narrative runs the same mechanism in reverse: British concessions in 1832, 1867, 1884 and 1918 are read as elites granting durable de jure power to the excluded under threat of revolt, because a promise of future redistribution would not be credible and a franchise is.

## The commitment problem, and why efficiency and distribution cannot be separated

**[model]** The Coasean answer to "why don't societies adopt the institutions that maximise the pie and compensate the losers?" is that they should. AJR reject the Political Coase Theorem explicitly (their Section 5.1) and the reason is enforcement: there is no third party above the power-holders who can enforce a compensation contract against them. So the losers cannot be bought off, and the choice of institutions is simultaneously a choice of who gets what. This is the same structural problem [[credible-commitment]] states for the ruler-and-investor case, generalised to institutional choice itself.

**[model]** Three inefficiency channels follow, and the authors grade their own evidence for each:

1. **Holdup** — power-holders cannot commit not to expropriate the returns on an investment once it is sunk, so the investment is not made. The direct analogue of the credible-commitment mechanism, one level up.
2. **Political losers** — power-holders block an economically beneficial institutional change because it would erode their *political* power, even where it would raise their income, since eroded political power cannot be trusted to be compensated later. **The authors' strongest channel.** It is a genuinely distinct mechanism from anything already on this wiki: elites block change to protect *future* rent-extraction capacity, not merely because the current rules happen to favour them. Closest existing relative is the "rules select the organisations that defend them" feedback at [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]], which is a weaker, structural version.
3. **Economic losers** — groups whose current income would fall block the change. **The authors argue this is empirically the weakest of the three** and usually dominated by political losers, citing the Luddites' failure to stop mechanisation and English landowners after 1870 not blocking falling land rents once their political power let them benefit elsewhere.

**[wiki synthesis]** Preserve the ranking. AJR do not treat the three as equally supported, and a page or manifesto that cites "AJR's three sources of inefficiency" as a flat list misrepresents them.

## What "institutions" means here — two different objects, not cleanly separated

**[empirical]** **Quantitatively**, in every regression and scatterplot the chapter cites, economic institutions = one variable: protection against expropriation risk, a subjective 1985–95 average from Political Risk Services. It is the only cardinal measured variable in the chapter.

**[model]** **Theoretically**, "institutions" is a much richer narrative construct — security of property rights, equality of access before the law, constraints on power-holders, breadth of the group holding power, and the size of extractable rents — cashed out as a binary, **"institutions of private property" vs. "extractive institutions"**, which is never operationalised as a variable distinct from the expropriation-risk index. The whole causal architecture above it — de jure vs. de facto power, the "hierarchy of institutions" — has **no operational variable anywhere in the chapter.**

**[empirical]** The authors flag the tautology risk in defining "good economic institutions" as those securing broad property rights, and try to close it by fixing the definition independent of observed growth outcomes. **The fix is a stipulation, not an independent measurement**, so the risk is only partly closed.

**[wiki synthesis]** Net assessment: the *narrow* claim (property-rights index → income) is falsifiable and was tested — elsewhere, in the authors' own earlier papers, and contested there. The *broad* claim (political power determines institutions via commitment problems) is an organising narrative rather than a falsifiable model, and the authors say so themselves.

## The identification restated, and one footnote worth reading

**[empirical]** Two "natural experiments" are re-cited rather than re-derived.

- **Korea's division (1945–48).** A single case, not an instrument: geography, culture and initial industrial endowment held fixed by construction — the North was if anything more industrialised in 1945 — with post-1950 divergence attributed to institutions by elimination. The chapter flags its own insufficiency: "this is only one case... an example of an extreme case."
- **Settler mortality as an instrument.** The exclusion restriction is *stated* — "outside of its effect on economic institutions during the colonial period, historical European mortality has no impact on current income levels" — and not argued. No placebo or falsification exercise appears in the chapter.

**[empirical]** The chapter raises exactly one threat to the exclusion restriction, in footnote 8: European and indigenous mortality could be correlated through a shared disease environment, letting the instrument proxy indigenous disease burden rather than settlement incentives. The rebuttal is that "local populations have developed much greater immunity", cited to the 2001 paper rather than shown. **No other threat is raised.** A reader of this chapter alone would take the instrument's validity as settled. It is not — see [[colonial-origins-and-the-settler-mortality-instrument]], where Glaeser et al. put the correlation between log settler mortality and modern malaria risk at **.67** and find both significant when AJR's own joint regression is re-run on the full sample.

## Production, and institutions selecting against efficient producers

**[empirical]** Two concrete production-level data points survive the chapter's theoretical apparatus and are worth keeping, because both are instances of institutions actively selecting *against* efficient production rather than merely failing to encourage it:

- **Marketing boards.** Ghanaian, Nigerian and Zambian government marketing boards taxed cash-crop farmers up to **70% of crop value** (Ghana, 1970s) by paying below world prices, correlating with collapsing agricultural investment and output — against Kenya and Colombia, where farmer political power, via concentrated landownership or party pivotality, kept prices favourable. The discriminating variable AJR name is **collective-action capacity**: concentrated large farmers in Kenya solve it, dispersed smallholders in Ghana do not.
- **Haber & Maurer on Mexican textiles.** Firms with bank political connections were **less profitable and less technically efficient** than unconnected competitors, yet grew faster and survived longer because of privileged credit access.

**[wiki synthesis]** The second is the more useful of the two for this wiki, because it is measured at firm level inside one national institutional environment — which is the level at which almost nothing else in this batch operates. It is a mechanism datapoint, not an identified effect, and AJR do not build it into any estimate.

## Levers, and the one that matters

**[model]** Four comparative statics, derived from the theory and then read back onto the colonial, Kenya/Ghana and Mexico/US cases post hoc. None is independently tested:

1. Constraints and checks on power-holders → better economic institutions.
2. Political power held by a relatively **broad** group with investment opportunities → better institutions.
3. **Fewer extractable rents** available to power-holders → better institutions.
4. **Reforms that do not threaten incumbents' political power** are more likely to be adopted and to persist.

**[wiki synthesis]** Lever 4 is the one that connects to measured evidence elsewhere in this wiki, and the connection runs the right way — theory supplying a mechanism for an existing finding rather than the reverse. Bunse & Fritz's **75% operation-level success against 47% country-level transformation** ([[political-economy-of-public-sector-reform]]) is attributed by its own authors to selecting politically easy, non-rent-threatening components. AJR's commitment problem is why that selection happens. Note what this does *not* license: lever 4 explains a gap; it is not advice to pursue only non-threatening reforms, which is the same selection that produced the gap.

## Persistence, admitted unexplained

**[empirical]** The chapter claims institutions "persist for long periods of time, often centuries (and sometimes millennia)" and then states, in its own concluding section: **"we do not as yet have a satisfactory understanding of the mechanisms through which institutions persist."**

**[wiki synthesis]** Record this as a direct authorial admission on a question this wiki has now searched five literatures for. It is consistent with — and independently corroborates — the standing finding at [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] and [[open-questions]] Q7 that a persistence or decay mechanism tied to elapsed time is asserted far more often than shown. AJR's persistence, note, is at the level of a *national institutional regime*, not of a single institution's own tenure, so it does not close the age gap even if it were mechanised.

## Level of analysis

**[wiki synthesis]** Exclusively national. "Economic institutions" and "political institutions" are whole-country attributes — North's "rules of the game in a society" — or occasionally a *class* of institutions (financial-market rules, labour-market rules, franchise rules) still measured at country level. The chapter never distinguishes a single agency, bureau or firm from the national regime, and gives no basis for reading its claims down to that level. It is also silent on whether its mechanisms apply differently to public agencies and private firms inside one polity; record that as an open silence, not as generality.
