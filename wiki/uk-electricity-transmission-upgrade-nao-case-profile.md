# UK Electricity Transmission Upgrade (NAO Audit Case Profile)

A case-study page scoring the governance of Great Britain's roughly GBP 70bn transmission-grid upgrade for Clean Power 2030 against a National Audit Office value-for-money audit (HC 589, 11 September 2026) — a primary institutional document, not secondary literature. The unit of analysis is an institutional *field*: three public bodies (DESNZ as policy-setter, Ofgem as regulator, NESO as system operator and planner), three privately owned regulated monopoly Transmission Owners (NGET, SPEN, SSENT), and two coordination bodies (the Electricity Networks Programme Board and the Clean Power Unit). The audit's headline findings are that generation was allowed to outrun the grid, that nobody is accountable for the project portfolio as a whole, and that value for money "now depends on delivery". It is the second NAO fragmented-authority case in the wiki, after [[defra-food-supply-chain-resilience-case-profile]], with a private monopoly delivery tier that Defra's case lacks.

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]** for NAO's analysis of regulator, NESO and Transmission Owner administrative data (project delivery ratings at March 2026 for 56 essential projects, spend series 2010-11 to 2025-26, outage and connection-offer counts) and the documented policy timeline. **[model]** for every 2030 constraint-cost counterfactual and the household saving derived from them: these are NESO model forecasts that assume all planned generation arrives on time, reproduced rather than independently tested by NAO. **[wiki synthesis]** where this wiki reads the case across other pages. The audit's criterion is process-based (can it be shown this is the best route?), not an outcome verdict, and its recommendations are untested prescriptions.

## Actor map and decision rights

**[empirical]** Decision rights are split four ways. DESNZ sets policy and picks pathways (the Secretary of State approves the Strategic Spatial Energy Plan, SSEP); NESO plans and advises; Ofgem approves spend and sets returns; the Transmission Owners hold delivery decisions and the project information. Ofgem therefore "holds less information than Transmission Owners" and spends six to nine months on iterative challenge per business case. Costs fall on consumer bills, not the Treasury, so no spending-control body reviews value for money and NAO is the only external check. Baseline returns were set using European comparators (Spain, Italy); Citizens Advice estimates a lower-return basis would have saved GBP 256m over five years, against a Transmission Owner counter-argument about underinvestment risk (both reported views, not tested by NAO).

## Policy timeline: how the coordination gap arose

**[empirical]** The 2015 "market-led" turn removed the shared spatial plan; the Electricity Networks Strategy Group was disbanded in 2017. DESNZ's "Connect and Manage" regime lets generators connect before reinforcement is built. With no common view of investment need across DESNZ, Ofgem and the system operator, Ofgem declined to approve some upgrades that were approved later at higher cost: the East Coast subsea links, proposed in 2009/2012, were fully funded in 2024 and are due in 2029. Constraint costs rose from GBP 0.6bn (2018-19) to GBP 1.9bn (2025-26, 2024 prices), with volume up from 5 TWh to 14 TWh. The mechanism is documented with a dated sequence, not merely a correlation. Central spatial planning returns only with the SSEP (planned 2027) and the Centralised Strategic Network Plan (2028). Earlier under-delivery compounds this: Transmission Owners underspent allowances by 20% (GBP 4.8bn, 2013-21) and 12% (GBP 1.3bn, 2021-25), and DESNZ concedes "historic underinvestment".

**[wiki synthesis]** This is a path-dependence pattern ([[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]]): removing an ex ante coordination layer left only ex post management of its consequences, and the 2024-29 delays trace back to that 2015 choice.

## Portfolio accountability versus project accountability

**[empirical]** "Accountability for individual projects rests with the Transmission Owners, no single person is accountable for delivery of the portfolio as a whole." The Programme Board and Clean Power Unit have limited formal powers and "rely on influence". Transmission Owners report project status voluntarily and public datasets "cannot be reconciled". NAO's recommendation is a portfolio management function with a single performance view, agreed milestone definitions, impact-based prioritisation and escalation routes; closing the information gap "may require either changes to licensing or voluntary agreement". This is a real-institution instance of the gap between formal and real authority ([[formal-and-real-authority]]) and of multi-principal incentive collapse ([[incentives-under-multiple-principals]]).

## Regulator capacity and the scrutiny-for-speed trade

**[empirical]** Regulated asset value goes from GBP 20.3bn (2020-21) to GBP 22.8bn (2024-25) to a forecast GBP 76.7bn (2030-31); annual upgrade spend rises from GBP 2.5bn (2025-26) to over GBP 11bn (2027-28). NAO says this "will test systems not designed for delivery at this pace and scale". Ofgem's project-assessment staff grew from an estimated 3.9 FTE (2013) to 21.8 FTE (2026) while spend quadruples. Ofgem "changed its risk appetite to prioritise faster funding decisions... over more detailed scrutiny of project costs and risks", and raised the detailed cost-review threshold from GBP 100m to GBP 300m. GBP 17.7bn of up to GBP 70bn is approved upfront, with re-openers for the rest. Many projects were chosen "because they were already in development" rather than through whole-system appraisal; there is no business case or options appraisal for the programme as a whole.

**[wiki synthesis]** This pairs a quantified scale response by a regulator (headcount up about 5.6x, threshold tripled, spend up about 4x) with an explicit trade of verification for speed. NAO does not allege capture ([[regulatory-capture]]); it asks Ofgem to test for systematic bias in cost estimates. The capacity numbers bear on [[state-capacity-and-level-of-government]].

## Incentive design

**[empirical]** Rewards for early delivery are weighted above penalties for lateness (incentive range -4.6 to +6.7 percentage points of return on regulated equity). Target dates are set at the later of NESO's optimal date and the Transmission Owner's own P50 forecast, so a project can beat its regulatory target while arriving after the date the system needs it. Penalties are exercised: Ofgem clawed back GBP 158m from two companies in 2022 after a two-year-late project. NAO flags the risk that firms prioritise on-track projects over at-risk ones.

**[wiki synthesis]** Target-setting from the regulated party's own forecast is a capture-by-information channel, and the early-bias reward structure is a multitask problem of the kind in [[multitask-incentive-theory]]: the measurable task (hit the target date) is favoured over the one that matters (arrive when the system needs it).

## Delivery bottlenecks and the economic cost of delay

**[empirical]** Delivery cycles run 12-14 years against a target of 7, set against a 2030 political deadline and five-year price-control periods. Connection-offer timeliness fell from 64% (2019-20) to 16% (2023-24). Of 23,400 outage applications in 2025-26, 15,300 were cancelled or rearranged. Skills are the most commonly cited significant risk (22% of projects), and land agreements can add up to three years; these are veto-point and consenting frictions ([[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]]). The competitive-appointment regime (CATO) exists but has not been used on any Clean Power 2030 project. Reforms in train, none yet evaluated for effect: staged funding with re-openers, streamlined approval for 26 of 88 projects (replacing sequential approvals that took 11-14 years; judged "broadly positive" by the three Transmission Owners, who are interested parties), advance procurement, and the 2023 Electricity Networks Commissioner recommendations (half of DESNZ's ten workstreams rated red or amber-red). The streamlining is a counter-direction to [[regulatory-accretion]].

## 2030 forecasts: model outputs, not measurements

**[model]** The following figures are NESO model forecasts, not observed data, and all assume planned generation arrives on time: 2030 constraint costs of GBP 6.6-7.8bn without acceleration, GBP 12.7bn with no upgrades, and GBP 2.8-3.6bn with all upgrades; and Ofgem's household saving of GBP 30 a year, which NAO flags as "subject to significant uncertainty". NESO does not recalculate constraint-cost impacts for each delay because of resource cost. NAO did not assess DESNZ's claimed wider benefits, and DESNZ has no impact assessment for Clean Power 2030. Bill impact is GBP 44 to GBP 104 per typical household per year by 2030-31. Do not cite these as established savings; they are conditional projections.

**[empirical]** Observed, by contrast: constraint spending to 2025-26, and turn-up payments to gas at roughly a 30% premium over wholesale. Wind curtailment is paid at Contract-for-Difference-fixed rates, so it is mostly not a net extra cost to consumers.

## Recommendations (prescriptions, untested)

**[model]** NAO recommends: (a) a portfolio management function; (b) regular public cost, timetable and status updates; (c) a review of options for generation expected to connect before capacity exists; (d) a review of the n-2 Security and Quality of Supply Standard trade-off between resilience and speed; (e) Ofgem evaluation of its price-control approach, comparing actual costs with estimates; (f) cost-benefit appraisal at SSEP, DESNZ pathway-choice and Ofgem decision stages. The report contains no outcome evaluation of any of these. For the wiki's lever taxonomy see [[reform-levers]].

## Scope limits and open questions

**[wiki synthesis]** The audit covers Great Britain only (Northern Ireland excluded), 2009-2026 with forecasts to 2030-31, under a specific price-control regime and a specific consumer-bill funding route. NAO is statutorily barred from questioning policy merits, so it audits delivery, not whether Clean Power 2030 is the right goal. It does not argue public/private invariance and does not compare against fully public or fully private grids; whether the portfolio-ownership gap is specific to this mixed arrangement is left open. Institutional age (founding date) is not analysed as a driver; "age" in the report refers to the physical grid (built largely 1950s-70s for fossil stations near demand). Other countries' rising constraints (Germany, China, Chile, Brazil, via the IEA) are cited, not analysed.
