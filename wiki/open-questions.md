# Open Questions

The live register of questions this wiki exists to answer. Every question carries a status and points at the
research job or page that will settle it. Questions are added here whenever an ingest surfaces something the
wiki cannot yet answer; they are never left in chat history.

**Numbering gaps are deliberate.** Q46-Q59 and Q66-Q69 are unused. Seven research jobs were ingested
concurrently on 2026-08-19 and each writing pass was assigned a reserved number range up front so two agents
could not claim the same question number. Ranges that were reserved but not filled leave gaps. **No question
has been deleted** — if a later session finds a gap, it is spare capacity, not a missing entry. Reuse the gaps
freely.

Status: `open` (no evidence yet) · `partial` (some evidence, no settled answer) · `answered` (a page states the
answer and cites it) · `dissolved` (the question turned out to be malformed — say why).

| Q3 | Which of those dimensions are genuinely invariant across public and private institutions, and which only look invariant because of shared vocabulary? | open | Outcome 2 | Ostrom's empirical finding that ownership form does not predict forest condition (Hayes 2006) is real negative evidence; Williamson's remediableness symmetry is a methodological argument, not evidence. Both in commons/private settings only. **The `scale-effects` batch adds nothing: no source in it tests a firm against a bureau on the same axis.** Three are firm-only (Coase 1937; Rajan & Wulf; Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg), three are bureau-only (Niskanen; Parkinson; Klimek/Hanel/Thurner), and two *assert* invariance without testing it — Simon groups "business firms, governments, universities" as one class and never contrasts them, and Coase's 1991 lecture analogises the firm to a "little planned society" and to the Soviet economy by rhetoric alone. Niskanen's footnote 3 goes further and suggests the axis may be wrong: post offices, universities and hospitals are *mixed* — part per-unit sale, part lump-sum budget — regardless of ownership, which points at funding mechanism ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D35) rather than public/private as the operative variable. Untested. Status moved partial → open: the batch that should have addressed this did not. **The `risk-aversion-in-large-institutions` batch (2026-08-19) supplies the wiki's first direct public/private test of a behavioural axis, and it comes back null.** Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty & Webeck ran a randomized survey experiment on 150 public and 150 private US managers: no sector difference in risky choice under gain framing, public managers *significantly more* risk-tolerant under loss framing (76.62% vs 63.51%, p<.10), and status-quo anchoring concentrated in the **private** managers, not the public ones. The authors flag limited power and an explicit Type-II concern on the status-quo nulls. Against that, four sources in the same batch *presume* the gap without testing it — Hood (all-public evidence, private extension asserted in one clause), Bozeman (word "risk" never appears; he himself reports the public/private red-tape literature as mixed and cites Buchanan 1975 finding *private* managers more rule-adherent), Hammond (public-only, no private analogue offered), OECD (5 of 38 member states, no private comparison group). See [[presumed-vs-measured-public-sector-risk-aversion]]. Bozeman also relocates the axis the way Niskanen's footnote 3 did: he states his external-control model is "as applicable to private as it is to government organizations" and names only two government-inherent causes (sovereignty; interconnection of policy missions), reading his own data as showing external political authority rather than ownership is causal — which points at D42 rather than at the public/private dummy. **Moved open → partial by the `incentives-and-institutional-form` batch (2026-08-19), on one source.** Dixit is the first source in this wiki to *derive* public-sector institutional form rather than assert that firm results transfer, and he states the position directly: **"many differences between public and private sector organizations are ones of degree, not kind."** The mechanism is parameterisation, not different behavioural laws — the same moral-hazard and multitask mathematics, fed public-sector values on five parameters (more principals; more tasks, more often substitutes; worse output measurability; more risk-averse principals who cannot diversify policy-outcome risk; less competition), mechanically yields weaker optimal incentive slopes and more rule-boundedness. The formal core is `m = 1/(1+nrcv)`. He also denies the clean dichotomy from the other side: private firms are not the `n = 1` case either, once unions and consumer groups are counted. **The corroboration is thin and should be stated as such.** The only other public/private treatment in the batch is Hart's private-prison case, which reaches a compatible conclusion (some services belong inside government) by a *different* mechanism (residual control over non-contractible guard quality), for the make-or-buy boundary only, on one service in one country, with one correlational citation on outcomes. Everything else in the batch is firm-only, and four of the eight sources say nothing about public bureaus at all. Status is partial because the wiki now has a **specified, testable claim** where it had none — not because it has been tested. The discriminating test is Q38. See [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]]. **Scope cluster [lint remediation, 2026-08-20].** This is the general form of a question the wiki has asked four more times at narrower scopes, each raised by a later batch that could not see the others: **Q64** asks it of the decay mechanisms (goal displacement, cooptation, capture, accretion, procedural ossification), **Q73** asks it of the renewal mechanisms, and **Q25** and **Q38** each propose a specific variable — funding mechanism, principal count — that would replace the public/private dummy this row is framed in. Read the five as one cluster; the scope differences are real and none is merged, but an answer here constrains all four. |
| Q4 | Is institutional behaviour better predicted by formal structure (rules, decision rights) or by selection effects (who ends up inside)? | partial | Outcome 2 | Untouched by the foundations-NIE sources — none names personnel selection as an axis. This is the test that would settle Q1. **First mention, and it is thin.** The OECD's Canada scan reports a middle-management "clay layer" where "innovative ideas are not evaluated by their merit, but by the personal characteristics and social capital involved" — a selection-and-gatekeeping mechanism, and precisely this question's object. It is one country scan inside an advocacy document whose own coding protocols are conceded to be undeveloped. Hood adds a moderator on the selection side (tenure security predicts blame tolerance) without measuring who gets selected. Not enough for a register row. **Moved open → partial by the `incentives-and-institutional-form` batch (2026-08-19) — and the evidence is essentially one source.** Prendergast supplies the wiki's first decomposition of selection against incentive effects, and states the headline himself: "the selection effects appear to be of roughly equal size to the incentive effects, despite the overwhelming focus on incentive effects in the theoretical literature." Two clean cases: of Lazear's (1996) 35% productivity gain from a piece-rate switch, **roughly one third is selection** — turnover data show less able workers leaving and abler ones replacing them; Paarsch & Shearer's (1996) structural estimate isolates the incentive effect on a *given* Canadian tree planter at ~10% against a raw 35% gap. Weiss (1987) and Hansen (1997) add that selection under team pay is **U-shaped**, not monotonic — medium-ability workers stay while both the ablest and the least able leave — with a *negative-slope* incentive effect underneath it. Prendergast also names the case that cannot be decomposed (Banker, Lee & Potter's store-level data), which is a standing caution against reading firm-level productivity comparisons as tests of incentive theory. **The structural gap this exposes is the important part.** Four of the batch's eight sources are *explicitly silent* on selection and say so: Holmstrom's lecture and Holmstrom & Milgrom are moral-hazard setups with agents "identical ex ante"; Grossman & Hart and Hart's lecture hold the parties' identities fixed and vary only control rights; Bénabou & Tirole's agent is the same person before and after the belief update. Dixit *models* an adverse-selection channel into public employment but measures nothing and hedges it explicitly. Frey's one selection-flavoured claim (higher-intrinsic-motivation people self-select into public employment) sits in the same paragraph as a crowding claim without the two being disentangled. So: **the distinction is evidenced by one source, in two occupations, both with unusually clean output measures** — the least representative slice of the workforce by Prendergast's own account. It establishes that a reward structure is itself a selection instrument. It does not supply a measurable property of an institution's selection regime, so no register row follows. Stays partial, not answered. See [[multitask-incentive-theory]]. **The register row finally exists [enabling-institutional-change, 2026-08-19], and this question has its first randomised evidence.** Finan, Olken & Pande supply the selection instrument itself, randomised rather than inferred from a reward structure's side-effects: **wage offers randomised across 106 Mexican recruitment sites** (better and no less prosocial applicants, fill rate +35.2%, short-run labour-supply elasticity 2.15 — Dal Bó, Finan & Rossi 2013); **job-ad framing randomised across 48 Zambian districts** (career framing drew more qualified applicants with no loss of prosocial motivation, better on-the-job performance, lower 18-month attrition — Ashraf, Bandiera & Lee 2015); and **advertised expected earnings randomised in Uganda**, the one case finding a genuine tradeoff (30% more applicants, less experienced and 24pp less likely to donate in a dictator game, no quality gain, and low-pay-recruited agents performing better in year one — Deserranno 2015). Three distinct instruments with different signs, which is why the axis is registered with four components rather than one ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D70**, `promoted` — the first item on the register's original absent-list closed by randomised evidence). Two further items belong on this question: even inside a de jure merit system, caste affinity to the sitting Chief Minister predicts plum IAS postings as strongly as measured ability (Iyer & Mani 2012), which is the de facto override of a formal selection rule; and screening *on integrity* is not currently available as an instrument, since India's civil-service exams would not filter out dishonest applicants (Hanna & Wang 2014) and the authors classify better screening design as future research. **Still `partial`, for three reasons.** Everything is measured at **recruitment** and nothing at promotion, so "who ends up inside" is answered at the front door only. The evidence is developing- and middle-income-country field experiments, 2007–2015. And the question as posed is comparative — selection *against* formal structure — and no source scores both on the same institutions. See [[personnel-economics-of-the-state]]. |

| Q6 | Which changes with scale are near-universal (coordination cost, layer growth, risk-aversion) and which are contingent on sector or governance? | partial | Outcome 4 | Ostrom's metro-services finding that scale effects *invert* by service type (many producers better for direct services, few for indirect) is direct empirical evidence of contingency; Chhatre & Agrawal show forest size effects are non-monotonic and interact with local autonomy. All at field level, none within a single organisation. **Within-organisation evidence now exists** from `scale-effects`: hierarchy depth rises with headcount cross-sectionally in 300+ US firms with firm and year fixed effects (Rajan & Wulf), layer count rises with output in French firm panel data (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg), and Simon gives a formal reason depth must scale with complexity at all. Coordination cost with scale is therefore the best-evidenced of the three limbs named in the question; risk-aversion is still untouched. Contingency is unresolved — every one of those results is private-sector. **The risk-aversion limb was addressed head-on by a dedicated batch (2026-08-19) and came back empty on the scale question.** Zero of eight sources test institutional size against risk-taking. Holmström rejects the size-based (risk-bearing-capacity) account by name for firms "of even modest size"; Hammond, Kahneman & Lovallo and Nicholson-Crotty have no size variable; Bozeman's drivers are external-control density, not focal-organisation size; Manso's Section VIII is the only gesture at a size mechanism and it has no size parameter, one case and two practitioner books behind it. Kahneman & Lovallo's hierarchy-depth compounding hypothesis is the nearest thing to a size mechanism in the wiki and is the authors' own explicitly untested extrapolation. So: of the three limbs named in this question, coordination cost and layer growth have real within-organisation evidence and **risk-aversion has none, in either direction**. See [[risk-aversion-in-large-institutions]] and Q30. |
| Q7 | Is institutional decay a function of age, of size, of the absence of competitive pressure, or of the accumulation of veto points? These are usually conflated. | partial (size) / open (age) | Outcome 4, 6 | **The size literature does not supply age evidence, and the conflation is the field's habit rather than a finding.** The `scale-effects` batch (eight sources, 2026-08-19) reads like a lifecycle literature and is not one. Every age-flavoured claim in it is about something else: Klimek/Hanel/Thurner's "age" is the *individual official's* career tenure (age of qualification, retirement age τR), not years since the institution's founding; Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's stagnation result runs on *technology vintage* — time since the current technology was adopted; Simon's time variable is *evolutionary and assembly time*, how long a complex form takes to come into being, explicitly not post-formation ageing; Niskanen's one age claim ("older bureaus... couldn't care less") is an aside his own footnote 1 disowns, the paper being static by construction; Rajan & Wulf report firm age only as a sample statistic (~85 years) and never use it as a covariate — their sample is old by construction, so it contains no young-firm comparison. The **size** limb, by contrast, now has real content: hierarchy depth rises with firm size cross-sectionally (Rajan & Wulf, firm and year fixed effects), layer count rises with output (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg, French panel), and decision-body performance appears to break near N≈20–21 (Klimek et al., 197-country cross-section, uncontrolled). The **competitive-pressure** limb has one negative result: Rajan & Wulf tested governance pressure against hierarchy depth and found nothing. The **veto-point** limb now has one source and it is a negative: Hammond's spatial model proves veto-point count and bureaucratic discretion are not monotonically related once preference divergence is allowed to vary — all 720 rank-orderings across six systems are achievable, "anything can happen" — so a count-only veto-point account of decay is unavailable as a general law from the formal literature. Registered as one composite axis ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D44) for exactly that reason. Hammond is silent on decay, on age and on risk; this constrains the limb rather than evidencing it. Bozeman's accretion mechanism is adjacent but counts external *rule-promulgating* entities, not blocking coalitions — do not merge the two counts. Do not let any page infer an age effect from this batch. See [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] and [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]], which now carries the one soft counter-datapoint (old, large firms restructuring substantially over thirteen years). **The `institutional-evolution` batch (seven sources, 2026-08-19) was commissioned as the place age evidence would finally appear. It largely does not, and one source argues against the premise.** Source by source: **Arthur's** model — the batch's only formal one, and the formal ancestor of every lock-in claim in this wiki — runs on *n*, the count of adoption events, not calendar time, and he states there is no dose-response connecting elapsed years to degree of lock-in; his one quantified threshold (30 of 100 adoptions) is a technology-market result, and he makes no institutional claim anywhere (registered and `rejected` as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D58). **North's** Nobel lecture names real intervals — ~500 years post-Rome, ~400 years of Spanish decline, ~800 years of European rise — and converts none into a threshold, a rate or a dose-response; Spain declines continuously with no point of no return identified, and he names **no falsification condition for lock-in anywhere**. **Skocpol's dataset**, reported by Pierson and the batch's only systematic longitudinal data (58 US voluntary associations since the 1830s; of the 40 founded before 1900, 19 stayed above a 1%-of-population threshold for five decades and 10 for a century or more), **measures survival, not behavioural change with age** — no rigidity measure, no structural measure, no young-organisation base rate, and read the other way it is 75% attrition over a century. **Pierson** concedes his own framework is hard to falsify, citing Geddes 1997, and grants that critical junctures are "usually attributed, often ex post". **DiMaggio & Powell** supply a genuine repeated cross-study claim — predictors of structure shift from technical to legitimacy-based as a field matures, replicated across civil-service reform, urban fiscal agencies and newspapers — but **zero measured intervals originate in their own text**, and it is *field* maturity (D57), a different variable from organisational age. **Meyer & Rowan** supply nothing on age or size and say so themselves, their own research-implications section flagging that nobody had studied structural variation "within any society across time". **Krasner's** own cited case data undercut his framing: Skowronek's "punctuation" runs 1900–1920 (20 years) against a "stasis" of 1877–1900 (23 years). **Thelen argues the premise is wrong in principle**: contestation over a rule "often begin[s] as soon as a rule is laid down", so there is no phase structure for an age-gated model to describe, and her fourth gap-mechanism runs the *opposite* way on elapsed time — as designers die and context shifts, interpretive space *opens*. **The honest position: this wiki has now searched three separate literatures for age evidence — size, risk-aversion, institutional evolution — and found no measurement of institutional behaviour against elapsed time since founding.** That is a finding about the field, not a gap in the capture. The study that would supply it is Q39.  **Fourth literature, and the sharpest null yet [`institutional-stagnation`, 2026-08-19].** This job was commissioned to test the age limb head-on, on the canonical *decay* literature itself, and it came back with **no institution-level age evidence and three founders of that literature disowning the age reading of their own mechanisms, unprompted.** Merton states goal displacement attaches to rational-legal bureaucratic form as such, so a new bureau and a century-old one are equally subject; Stigler's capture is a cross-sectional equilibrium with no age variable in either regression, and he cites the CAB's zero-new-entrants record as a constant running "since it was created in 1938" rather than as a trend; Selznick's cooptation is triggered by threat, with survival-maintenance needs "a basic need of all empirical systems" present from inception. None of the three was addressing a decay thesis when he said so. Where duration *does* appear in the batch it attaches to something other than the institution: Heckelman's variable across 53 studies is years of national political stability; Miller & Dudley's is 130 years of a national rule corpus; Schleicher & Bagley's is a legal regime dated to the 1970s–80s and applied uniformly regardless of any agency's founding date; Fukuyama's veto structure is 230 years old while he pins the dysfunction to 1980s polarisation. The **veto-point limb** gains two sources asserting what Hammond's model denies — count or density as itself capacity-degrading — and both partly self-undercut; see the contested-cases section of [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]]. **Cumulative position across four literatures — size, risk-aversion, institutional evolution, institutional stagnation: the age limb has been looked for by four consecutive research jobs and found by none.** It stays `open` rather than moving toward `dissolved`, because unevidenced is not refuted and none of the critics runs an age test either. Successors: Q39 at the rule level, Q60 at the organisational level, and Q61 on why the field keeps substituting. See [[institutional-sclerosis]], [[regulatory-capture]] and [[decay-as-real-vs-decay-as-overstated]]. |
| Q8 | Are there documented cases of large institutions genuinely renewing themselves without an external shock? If renewal always requires a shock, the manifesto's premise changes. | partial | Outcome 3 | **Moved open → partial by the `institutional-evolution` batch (2026-08-19), and the answer depends on which of three rival models you accept — which is itself unsettled.** Thelen's whole programme is the affirmative case: layering, drift and conversion are transformations achieved with **no exogenous shock and, in two of the three, no formal rule change at all**, with worked German instances of each (Hartz-era layering of atypical-employment rules alongside untouched core protections; collective-bargaining coverage eroding while the formal multi-industry structure was strengthened by union mergers; the attempted *Günstigkeitsprinzip* court conversion). Her strongest single case runs the other way on the same point: German VET absorbed two world wars and multiple regime changes largely unchanged in form while its function reversed completely — change without shock, and shock without change, in one institution. See [[gradual-institutional-change]]. **Three caveats keep this partial rather than answered.** (i) These are *transformations*, not *renewals* — Thelen makes no claim that any of them improved performance, and her German finding is dualisation, which she treats as a warning. The question asks about renewal; the evidence is about change. (ii) Krasner states the flat negative from the same batch — all change is crisis-driven, "episodic and dramatic rather than continuous and incremental" — and neither he nor Thelen tests the other. See [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]] and [[rival-models-of-institutional-change]]. (iii) The evidence is qualitative single-country case narrative in both directions. Still the only quantitative item, and still ambiguous: Rajan & Wulf's 300+ old, large US firms restructuring substantially over thirteen years, with the cause explicitly unidentified by the authors — not obviously shock-free, given the era.  **The `institutional-stagnation` batch adds a fourth item, on the pessimistic side but at theory level rather than evidence level.** Olson's thesis, as reviewed across 53 studies, offers **exactly one lever and it is destructive**: war, coup, revolution, or a discrete non-violent rupture (Olson's own example is the 1965 Voting Rights Act ending Jim Crow). Heckelman reports **no regression evidence directly testing the reset claim** — it is evidenced by narrative case study only — and the review contains no incremental or reformist lever anywhere: no sunset clauses, no competitive entry, no structured periodic reorganisation. Fukuyama predicts decay "will probably continue until some external shock comes along", and names the 1880s Pendleton Act reform coalition as the historical counter-case while stating its collective-action problem is severe and no current equivalent exists. Schleicher & Bagley give the obstacle its general form, the Posner & Vermeule inside/outside problem: the same dysfunction diagnosed as the cause of low capacity is likely to block the reforms meant to fix it. So the affirmative decay literature has a **prescriptive gap** where a non-catastrophic renewal mechanism should be — which is an observation about that literature, not evidence against Thelen. See [[institutional-sclerosis]] and [[political-decay-and-vetocracy]]. **The `enabling-institutional-change` batch (2026-08-19) supplies the first source in this wiki whose entire subject is firm renewal, and it does not move the question.** O'Reilly & Tushman's case set — USA Today, Ciba Vision, IBM's Emerging Business Organizations, Analog Devices, NUMMI — is presented as deliberate, senior-management-driven renewal, which taken at face value is the affirmative answer. It cannot be taken at face value: the cases are success-selected roughly nine to one, and the construct **excludes by definition** successful adaptation that was not consciously designed (HP's divisional spinoffs past 1,200 employees, B.F. Goodrich's wartime pivot into chemicals) — precisely the category this question is about. The mortality statistics on the other side of their argument are real and are somebody else's — 28 of the largest 266 US manufacturers of 1917 surviving to 1997; S&P 500 life expectancy falling 90 → 30 → 15 years — and they establish that renewal is rare without establishing what produces it. See [[ambidexterity-as-a-dynamic-capability]] and Q73. |

## Raised by ingest (institutional-stagnation, 2026-08-19)

Goodrich's wartime pivot are excluded as "not dynamic capabilities" despite identical outcomes, because the process was not deliberately managed. **Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock**: hand-picked failure cases (procurement in three countries, one PFM study cited not reproduced, an anonymised education ministry with n=1, Afghanistan) with essentially no successes discussed — South Korea is named once as an "obvious spectacular success" and not analysed. Selection in the *failure* direction is the same defect: it cannot show that the diagnosed mechanism is what distinguishes failures from successes. **Only two sources largely escape it.** Finan, Olken & Pande's field experiments randomise the treatment and observe both arms; NAO's audit is a near-census of a defined population and period, capturing reorganisations regardless of outcome — and note that both are also the two sources this batch's tier-(i) levers and base-rate finding come from. **The one partial case worth recording separately**: True, Jones & Baumgartner's budget-distribution evidence is a full unconditional time series of all subfunction-years and does not select at all, while their *mechanism* narrative selects uniformly on successful punctuations. Same paper, two evidence layers, opposite verdicts — which is the cleanest illustration in the wiki of why the tier labelling has to be done per claim rather than per source. |
| Q73 | Does anything in this wiki's account of institutional renewal hold for private firms? | open | Outcome 2, 3, 4 | **Private-sector renewal evidence in this wiki is one source, and it is a draft working paper built on roughly five recurring case firms.** O'Reilly & Tushman is the batch's only private-sector source and the wiki's only source on firm-level renewal as such. | Q39 | **Does the success rate of attempts to change a rule fall with elapsed time since that rule was laid down?** | open | Outcome 4, 6 | **The study that would settle the wiki's age question, stated as a design rather than a wish.** Every position in [[rival-models-of-institutional-change]] makes a different prediction about it and none measures it. Lock-in-deepens (North, Pierson, Krasner's stability half) predicts a declining success rate. Gradual/endogenous (Thelen) predicts approximately flat, with the *form* of successful change shifting from formal amendment toward drift and conversion as veto structures harden. Punctuated (Krasner) predicts a step function conditioned on crisis, not on age. **The data class exists and is public**: legislative amendment records, regulatory dockets and rulemaking petitions, corporate charter and bylaw amendments, and collective-agreement renegotiations all carry a dated founding, dated change attempts, and a coded outcome. What has to be got right is the denominator — the *attempt*, not the success — because measuring only successful changes selects on the outcome, which is the mistake the whole path-dependence literature makes. Second requirement: control for stake size, since attempts on consequential rules are both rarer and better resourced. Until something of this shape exists, "institutions rigidify with age" is a hypothesis this wiki has looked for in three literatures and not found. |
| Q40 | Is there any **ex ante** criterion distinguishing a shock that produces institutional discontinuity from one that gets absorbed? | open | Outcome 3, 4 | Neither rival model supplies one, and this is the axis on which they could actually be compared. Krasner offers Skowronek's qualitative crisis definition — "a sporadic, disruptive event that suddenly challenges a state's capacity to maintain control and alters the boundaries defining the legitimate use of coercion" — with no threshold of magnitude, duration or intensity, and whether the boundaries moved is typically only assessable by observing whether institutions changed, which is the outcome being explained. His own examples are heterogeneous in kind (military defeat, diffuse industrialisation, elite coup), so source-of-shock cannot be the marker either. Thelen predicts which *form* gradual change takes but says nothing about whether a given shock will be absorbed — her own strongest case is German VET absorbing two world wars, offered retrospectively. Pierson's critical junctures are "usually attributed, often ex post" by his own admission. **Three frameworks, three post-hoc classification schemes.** A criterion applied prospectively to a set of shocks, with punctuation predicted in advance, is what would turn any of them into a theory. See [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]] and [[gradual-institutional-change]]. |
| Q41 | Do any of DiMaggio & Powell's twelve hypotheses actually hold? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | **Worth asking because testability is rare in this material.** The twelve are directional, stated in terms of measurable organisation and field characteristics (resource dependence and centralisation, technological uncertainty, goal ambiguity, credential reliance, professional-association participation, number of visible alternative models, extent of state transaction, field structuration), and intended by the authors for cross-sectional or longitudinal testing against standard-deviation-based homogeneity measures. The authors tested none — "an empirical test of such predictions is beyond the scope of this paper" — and did not address expected non-linearities or ceiling effects. Two are of particular interest to this wiki. **B-2** (the more a field transacts with agencies of the state, the more isomorphic the whole field) is a public/private claim in disguise and bears on Q3. **B-4** predicts, counter-intuitively, that abrupt increases in uncertainty produce *rapid* homogenisation after only brief experimentation — which contradicts the intuition that shocks open institutional variety, and bears on Q40. The inverse case the authors never state is the design brief for a homogenisation-resistant institution: decentralised resources, few state transactions, well-understood technology, unambiguous goals, low credentialism, many visible alternative models, weakly structured field. Tabulated at [[institutional-isomorphism]]. |
| Q42 | What is observed institutional structure actually tracking — output and knowledge cost, budget-maximising rent, or legitimacy? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | The three-mechanism form of [[functional-vs-rent-seeking-growth]], which this batch extended from two positions to three. Each predicts structural growth under a different, independently observable condition: rising output or shifting information costs (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg, Simon); a lump-sum-funding sponsor available for extraction (Niskanen); elaboration of a rationalised myth in the institutional environment, output held constant (Meyer & Rowan's Proposition 2, which makes exactly that ceteris paribus claim). **The cheapest discriminating test the wiki has identified in any conflict**: sample an organisation's formal structural elements and report the fraction actually invoked in real decisions ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D56). A low fraction is direct evidence for the legitimacy account and against both others, which each assume the structure does something. Nobody has run it on a bureau. Note the sharpest asymmetry: the first two positions assume an audit is possible and disagree about what it would show, while the third predicts the audit **will not happen**, because evaluation threatens the legitimacy the structure exists to produce. |
| Q43 | Under uncertainty, does an organisation homogenise *substantively* or merely ceremonially while its internal practice stays variable? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | The subject of [[homogenisation-vs-loose-coupling]], and a rare case of two founding papers of the *same* tradition making opposite predictions from the same antecedent — DiMaggio & Powell say so themselves at Hypothesis A-3 ("Here our prediction diverges somewhat from Meyer and Rowan"). Meyer & Rowan: ceremony insulates the work, so loosely coupled organisations vary internally. DiMaggio & Powell: ceremony pulls the work with it via imitation and credentialed hiring, so homogeneity is substantive and internal variation falls. **The discriminating measurement is the cross-organisation variance of a *practice* measure, not of an org chart** — both predict converging formal structure. A candidate scope condition, stated by neither: Meyer & Rowan's cases sit at zero output pressure (schools, hospitals, defence contractors, regulated utilities) while DiMaggio & Powell's include fields with some competitive discipline, so ceremony may convert into substance only where some output test operates. Consequence for the wiki: "sociological institutionalism says X" is not a usable citation about internal behaviour; a page must name which limb it is invoking. |
| Q44 | Why do century-old US voluntary associations persist largely unchanged while old, large US firms restructure substantially — sector, or different dependent variables? | open | Outcome 3, 4 | **Flagged, not adjudicated.** Skocpol via Pierson: 10 of 40 US voluntary associations founded before 1900 were still above a 1%-of-population membership threshold a century later, which Pierson reads as self-reinforcing persistence. Rajan & Wulf: 300+ old (~85 years mean), large (~47,500 employees), size-stable US public firms changed their senior structure substantially over thirteen years — CEO span of control up 50%, hierarchy depth down more than 25%, the COO role eliminated in about a fifth of firms that had one. Two candidate reconciliations, neither stated by any source. **Sector**: membership associations with no takeover market and no product-market test, against public corporations in the post-takeover-wave era. **Different dependent variables**: Skocpol measures whether the organisation still exists at threshold size; Rajan & Wulf measure whether its internal structure changed. An organisation can score "persisted" on the first and "transformed" on the second at once — which is precisely Thelen's form/function decoupling ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D55), and if that is the resolution then the persistence literature has been measuring the wrong thing for the age question all along. |
| Q45 | Is there an institutional analogue of Arthur's adoption count, and does it predict reversal difficulty better than calendar time? | open | Outcome 2, 4 | **The readmission condition for the `rejected` [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D58, and a real hypothesis rather than a bookkeeping note.** Arthur's proved result is that reversal cost rises without bound in *n*, the count of adoption events — not in years — and he warns explicitly against the calendar reading. The wiki's lock-in claims have inherited the conclusion while silently swapping the clock. If some institutional counter does the work Arthur's *n* does — budget rounds completed, election cycles survived, contract renewal rounds, or cohorts of staff socialised into the rule — then reversal difficulty should track that counter and *not* elapsed time, and two institutions of the same age with different cycle counts should differ. That is directly testable on the same data class as Q39 and would give the lock-in account a mechanism it currently lacks. If no such counter predicts better than years do, the analogy should be dropped rather than repeated. Note the structural obstacle Arthur's own model implies: his agents choose once and are frozen, whereas institutional rules are continuously re-enacted by overlapping cohorts with renegotiable authority, so any institutional *n* is a construct rather than a count. |
