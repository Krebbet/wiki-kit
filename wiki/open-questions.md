# Open Questions

The live register of questions this wiki exists to answer. Every question carries a status and points at the
research job or page that will settle it. Questions are added here whenever an ingest surfaces something the
wiki cannot yet answer; they are never left in chat history.

Status: `open` (no evidence yet) · `partial` (some evidence, no settled answer) · `answered` (a page states the
answer and cites it) · `dissolved` (the question turned out to be malformed — say why).

| Q2 | What are the *primary* dimensions of institutional variation — the smallest set that explains the most behavioural variance? | partial | Outcome 2 | [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] registers 34 named axes with operationalisations; none is yet shown to explain variance, so the *primary* set is still open. **Register status after `incentives-and-institutional-form` (2026-08-19): 53 numbered rows plus D25a, of which three are `rejected`.** Two of the three rejections come from this batch and share a failure mode worth naming: Frey's controlling-vs-supportive valence (D52) and Bénabou & Tirole's sorting condition (D53) are both **post-hoc labels rather than measurement procedures** — each is applied after the outcome it explains is known. | Q3 | Which of those dimensions are genuinely invariant across public and private institutions, and which only look invariant because of shared vocabulary? | open | Outcome 2 | Ostrom's empirical finding that ownership form does not predict forest condition (Hayes 2006) is real negative evidence; Williamson's remediableness symmetry is a methodological argument, not evidence. Both in commons/private settings only. **The `scale-effects` batch adds nothing: no source in it tests a firm against a bureau on the same axis.** Three are firm-only (Coase 1937; Rajan & Wulf; Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg), three are bureau-only (Niskanen; Parkinson; Klimek/Hanel/Thurner), and two *assert* invariance without testing it — Simon groups "business firms, governments, universities" as one class and never contrasts them, and Coase's 1991 lecture analogises the firm to a "little planned society" and to the Soviet economy by rhetoric alone. Niskanen's footnote 3 goes further and suggests the axis may be wrong: post offices, universities and hospitals are *mixed* — part per-unit sale, part lump-sum budget — regardless of ownership, which points at funding mechanism ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D35) rather than public/private as the operative variable. Untested. Status moved partial → open: the batch that should have addressed this did not. **The `risk-aversion-in-large-institutions` batch (2026-08-19) supplies the wiki's first direct public/private test of a behavioural axis, and it comes back null.** Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty & Webeck ran a randomized survey experiment on 150 public and 150 private US managers: no sector difference in risky choice under gain framing, public managers *significantly more* risk-tolerant under loss framing (76.62% vs 63.51%, p<.10), and status-quo anchoring concentrated in the **private** managers, not the public ones. The authors flag limited power and an explicit Type-II concern on the status-quo nulls. Against that, four sources in the same batch *presume* the gap without testing it — Hood (all-public evidence, private extension asserted in one clause), Bozeman (word "risk" never appears; he himself reports the public/private red-tape literature as mixed and cites Buchanan 1975 finding *private* managers more rule-adherent), Hammond (public-only, no private analogue offered), OECD (5 of 38 member states, no private comparison group). See [[presumed-vs-measured-public-sector-risk-aversion]]. Bozeman also relocates the axis the way Niskanen's footnote 3 did: he states his external-control model is "as applicable to private as it is to government organizations" and names only two government-inherent causes (sovereignty; interconnection of policy missions), reading his own data as showing external political authority rather than ownership is causal — which points at D42 rather than at the public/private dummy. **Moved open → partial by the `incentives-and-institutional-form` batch (2026-08-19), on one source.** Dixit is the first source in this wiki to *derive* public-sector institutional form rather than assert that firm results transfer, and he states the position directly: **"many differences between public and private sector organizations are ones of degree, not kind."** The mechanism is parameterisation, not different behavioural laws — the same moral-hazard and multitask mathematics, fed public-sector values on five parameters (more principals; more tasks, more often substitutes; worse output measurability; more risk-averse principals who cannot diversify policy-outcome risk; less competition), mechanically yields weaker optimal incentive slopes and more rule-boundedness. The formal core is `m = 1/(1+nrcv)`. He also denies the clean dichotomy from the other side: private firms are not the `n = 1` case either, once unions and consumer groups are counted. **The corroboration is thin and should be stated as such.** The only other public/private treatment in the batch is Hart's private-prison case, which reaches a compatible conclusion (some services belong inside government) by a *different* mechanism (residual control over non-contractible guard quality), for the make-or-buy boundary only, on one service in one country, with one correlational citation on outcomes. Everything else in the batch is firm-only, and four of the eight sources say nothing about public bureaus at all. Status is partial because the wiki now has a **specified, testable claim** where it had none — not because it has been tested. The discriminating test is Q38. See [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]]. |
| Q4 | Is institutional behaviour better predicted by formal structure (rules, decision rights) or by selection effects (who ends up inside)? | open | Outcome 2 | Untouched by the foundations-NIE sources — none names personnel selection as an axis. This is the test that would settle Q1. **First mention, and it is thin.** The OECD's Canada scan reports a middle-management "clay layer" where "innovative ideas are not evaluated by their merit, but by the personal characteristics and social capital involved" — a selection-and-gatekeeping mechanism, and precisely this question's object. It is one country scan inside an advocacy document whose own coding protocols are conceded to be undeveloped. Hood adds a moderator on the selection side (tenure security predicts blame tolerance) without measuring who gets selected. Not enough for a register row. **Moved open → partial by the `incentives-and-institutional-form` batch (2026-08-19) — and the evidence is essentially one source.** Prendergast supplies the wiki's first decomposition of selection against incentive effects, and states the headline himself: "the selection effects appear to be of roughly equal size to the incentive effects, despite the overwhelming focus on incentive effects in the theoretical literature." Two clean cases: of Lazear's (1996) 35% productivity gain from a piece-rate switch, **roughly one third is selection** — turnover data show less able workers leaving and abler ones replacing them; Paarsch & Shearer's (1996) structural estimate isolates the incentive effect on a *given* Canadian tree planter at ~10% against a raw 35% gap. Weiss (1987) and Hansen (1997) add that selection under team pay is **U-shaped**, not monotonic — medium-ability workers stay while both the ablest and the least able leave — with a *negative-slope* incentive effect underneath it. Prendergast also names the case that cannot be decomposed (Banker, Lee & Potter's store-level data), which is a standing caution against reading firm-level productivity comparisons as tests of incentive theory. **The structural gap this exposes is the important part.** Four of the batch's eight sources are *explicitly silent* on selection and say so: Holmstrom's lecture and Holmstrom & Milgrom are moral-hazard setups with agents "identical ex ante"; Grossman & Hart and Hart's lecture hold the parties' identities fixed and vary only control rights; Bénabou & Tirole's agent is the same person before and after the belief update. Dixit *models* an adverse-selection channel into public employment but measures nothing and hedges it explicitly. Frey's one selection-flavoured claim (higher-intrinsic-motivation people self-select into public employment) sits in the same paragraph as a crowding claim without the two being disentangled. So: **the distinction is evidenced by one source, in two occupations, both with unusually clean output measures** — the least representative slice of the workforce by Prendergast's own account. It establishes that a reward structure is itself a selection instrument. It does not supply a measurable property of an institution's selection regime, so no register row follows. Stays partial, not answered. See [[multitask-incentive-theory]]. |

## Lifecycle, scale and decay

| # | Question | Status | Bears on | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q6 | Which changes with scale are near-universal (coordination cost, layer growth, risk-aversion) and which are contingent on sector or governance? | partial | Outcome 4 | Ostrom's metro-services finding that scale effects *invert* by service type (many producers better for direct services, few for indirect) is direct empirical evidence of contingency; Chhatre & Agrawal show forest size effects are non-monotonic and interact with local autonomy. All at field level, none within a single organisation. **Within-organisation evidence now exists** from `scale-effects`: hierarchy depth rises with headcount cross-sectionally in 300+ US firms with firm and year fixed effects (Rajan & Wulf), layer count rises with output in French firm panel data (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg), and Simon gives a formal reason depth must scale with complexity at all. Coordination cost with scale is therefore the best-evidenced of the three limbs named in the question; risk-aversion is still untouched. Contingency is unresolved — every one of those results is private-sector. **The risk-aversion limb was addressed head-on by a dedicated batch (2026-08-19) and came back empty on the scale question.** Zero of eight sources test institutional size against risk-taking. Holmström rejects the size-based (risk-bearing-capacity) account by name for firms "of even modest size"; Hammond, Kahneman & Lovallo and Nicholson-Crotty have no size variable; Bozeman's drivers are external-control density, not focal-organisation size; Manso's Section VIII is the only gesture at a size mechanism and it has no size parameter, one case and two practitioner books behind it. Kahneman & Lovallo's hierarchy-depth compounding hypothesis is the nearest thing to a size mechanism in the wiki and is the authors' own explicitly untested extrapolation. So: of the three limbs named in this question, coordination cost and layer growth have real within-organisation evidence and **risk-aversion has none, in either direction**. See [[risk-aversion-in-large-institutions]] and Q30. |
| Q7 | Is institutional decay a function of age, of size, of the absence of competitive pressure, or of the accumulation of veto points? These are usually conflated. | partial (size) / open (age) | Outcome 4, 6 | **The size literature does not supply age evidence, and the conflation is the field's habit rather than a finding.** The `scale-effects` batch (eight sources, 2026-08-19) reads like a lifecycle literature and is not one. Every age-flavoured claim in it is about something else: Klimek/Hanel/Thurner's "age" is the *individual official's* career tenure (age of qualification, retirement age τR), not years since the institution's founding; Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's stagnation result runs on *technology vintage* — time since the current technology was adopted; Simon's time variable is *evolutionary and assembly time*, how long a complex form takes to come into being, explicitly not post-formation ageing; Niskanen's one age claim ("older bureaus... couldn't care less") is an aside his own footnote 1 disowns, the paper being static by construction; Rajan & Wulf report firm age only as a sample statistic (~85 years) and never use it as a covariate — their sample is old by construction, so it contains no young-firm comparison. The **size** limb, by contrast, now has real content: hierarchy depth rises with firm size cross-sectionally (Rajan & Wulf, firm and year fixed effects), layer count rises with output (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg, French panel), and decision-body performance appears to break near N≈20–21 (Klimek et al., 197-country cross-section, uncontrolled). The **competitive-pressure** limb has one negative result: Rajan & Wulf tested governance pressure against hierarchy depth and found nothing. The **veto-point** limb now has one source and it is a negative: Hammond's spatial model proves veto-point count and bureaucratic discretion are not monotonically related once preference divergence is allowed to vary — all 720 rank-orderings across six systems are achievable, "anything can happen" — so a count-only veto-point account of decay is unavailable as a general law from the formal literature. Registered as one composite axis ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D44) for exactly that reason. Hammond is silent on decay, on age and on risk; this constrains the limb rather than evidencing it. Bozeman's accretion mechanism is adjacent but counts external *rule-promulgating* entities, not blocking coalitions — do not merge the two counts. Do not let any page infer an age effect from this batch. See [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] and [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]], which now carries the one soft counter-datapoint (old, large firms restructuring substantially over thirteen years). |
| Q8 | Are there documented cases of large institutions genuinely renewing themselves without an external shock? If renewal always requires a shock, the manifesto's premise changes. | open | Outcome 3 | |
| Q9 | Does the decay pattern differ between institutions with measurable output (firms, hospitals) and those without (regulators, ministries)? | open | Outcome 3, 4 | **The `incentives-and-institutional-form` batch supplies the front half of this question and none of the back half.** It now gives a well-specified account of how institutional *form* differs by measurability — Dixit's reading of Wilson's typology ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D50) predicts that where actions are observable and outcomes are not, an organisation will govern by standard operating procedures and evaluate by evidence that rules were followed, and where neither is observable explicit incentives must be very weak, leaving only career concerns. He places US schooling between the two. Holmstrom & Milgrom reach the same institutional form from task multiplicity rather than principal multiplicity. **But nothing in the batch is longitudinal.** Every model is static or two-period; no source observes an institution over time, and none contains a decay claim of any kind. The question as posed asks about a *pattern over time*, so it stays open — but it is now well posed: score an institution on D50, and the prediction about its form is stated in advance and falsifiable. |

| Q24 | Does the knowledge-hierarchy mechanism — layers scaling with output, management by exception, the reorganisation-on-growth signature — survive where output is unpriced and often unmeasurable? | open | Outcome 2, 4 | Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's model minimises a wage bill to produce a market-priced quantity q and closes with a competitive labour market. | Q25 | Is budget-maximising behaviour a property of **funding mechanism** (lump-sum appropriation vs. per-unit price) rather than of public ownership? | open | Outcome 2, 5 | Niskanen defines a bureau by its funding form, not its owner, and his own footnote 3 concedes that post offices, universities and hospitals are mixed regardless of ownership. | Q26 | Is the ~N=20–21 threshold for decision-body effectiveness real, or an artefact of confounding and reverse causality — and does it apply to boards and committees inside organisations, not just national cabinets? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | Klimek et al.'s cross-section (197 countries, CIA 2007 data against HDI and political stability) has no controls for regime type, income, federalism or colonial legacy, and coalition politics inflating cabinet size is at least as plausible a causal direction. | Q27 | Did the 1986–1998 flattening of US corporate hierarchies have any counterpart in public bureaus, or does it require product-market competition and a takeover market? | open | Outcome 3, 4 | Rajan & Wulf establish the trend robustly and explicitly do not identify its cause; their one tested explanation (governance pressure) fails. | Q28 | Can "grew by adding a layer" vs. "grew without adding one" work as a general diagnostic separating functional from dysfunctional growth? | open | Outcome 3, 4 | Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's firm-fixed-effects result gives each path a distinct wage signature — adding a layer cuts pre-existing layers' pay and knowledge, growing without one raises them. | Q29 | What determines **span** — the breadth of a hierarchy at each level — as opposed to depth? | open | Outcome 2 | Three incompatible answers are on the table and none has been tested against the others: Simon's cognitive ceiling (a human is "more nearly a serial than a parallel information-processing system"), Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's manager helping-time constraint, and Klimek et al.'s promotion regime (r subordinates granted per promotion). ## Raised by ingest (risk-aversion-in-large-institutions, 2026-08-19)

*The framing question this batch was commissioned under — "why are large institutions risk averse?" — presupposed its answer. Q30 restates it as a question the evidence can be brought to bear on. The rest record what the batch left unmeasured.*

| # | Question | Status | Bears on | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q30 | **Are large institutions actually more risk averse — relative to what baseline, and measured how?** | open | Outcome 2, 3, 4 | **This is a correction to the wiki's own framing, and it is the central finding of the `risk-aversion-in-large-institutions` batch.** The affirmative case in that batch is mechanism-assertion; the single direct measurement cuts the other way. Zero of eight sources test institutional *size* against risk-taking behaviour, and Holmström explicitly rejects the size-based (risk-bearing-capacity) account for firms "of even modest size". The one source that measures risk aversion directly — Nicholson-Crotty et al., randomized survey experiment, n=300 real US managers — tests the *sector* version and finds no support, with a significant reversal under loss framing. Kahneman & Lovallo, whose paper is the most-cited source for institutional timidity, deny the general reading in terms: "we do not claim that an objective observer would describe managerial decisions as generally risk averse", and pair the timidity with an equally-evidenced bold-forecast bias. **No baseline is specified by any source except Kahneman & Lovallo** (the pooled-portfolio value), and against that baseline the net direction is indeterminate. Until a baseline and a measure are fixed, the question is not answerable rather than unanswered. See [[risk-aversion-in-large-institutions]]. |
| Q31 | Does the loss-aversion / narrow-framing mechanism survive where decision-makers hold no equity stake and face no market review? | open | Outcome 2, 4 | Kahneman & Lovallo's evidence base is corporate executives, entrepreneurs, capital investment, M&A and process plants — settings with equity ownership, market returns and board review. Their one public-sector gesture (Challenger, via Landau & Chisholm 1990) illustrates the *optimism* half and is undeveloped. Public agencies have none of the three features and a different review structure (political and media rather than board and shareholder). The mechanism is individual-psychological and might transfer intact; it might also require the payoff structure it was measured in. Neither has been tested. Cuts directly against treating that source as evidence about "large institutions" generally. |
| Q32 | Is there an aggregation from individual career concerns to institutional behaviour, and does it amplify or cancel? | open | Outcome 2, 4 | Hood, Kahneman & Lovallo, Holmström and Manso are all individual-level; each is explicit that it is. The step from many timid agents to a timid institution is taken by no source and tested by none. It is not obviously amplifying: Kahneman & Lovallo's own pooling arithmetic implies an institution holding a portfolio of many independent decisions should be *closer* to risk-neutral than any of its agents, which would make aggregate timidity evidence that the portfolio is not being pooled — a claim about internal review design (D46), not about size. Their hierarchy-depth compounding hypothesis is the opposite conjecture from the same model. The two predictions are separable and neither is tested. |
| Q33 | Can observed institutional timidity be told apart from outside — risk preference, moral hazard under a mis-specified contract, or structural constraint? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | This matters for the playbook, because the three imply different levers. Manso's result holds for risk-*neutral* agents ("the critical elements... are the likelihood ratios between the different action plans, not the agent's preferences"), and Holmström's risk-neutral manager acts risk-averse via reputational signalling. So two of the three produce the observed behaviour with no risk preference anywhere. Fixing risk attitudes would not fix either. A diagnostic that separates them on observable data — who declines what, under which contract and which review structure — does not exist in this batch and would be worth more than another mechanism. |
| Q34 | Does any structural variable (rule density, external-control layers, veto-point count, blame exposure) actually predict cautious *behaviour*, measured on the same subjects? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | **The batch's structural half and its behavioural half never touch.** Bozeman theorises rule density and never observes a choice — the word "risk" does not appear in his paper. Hammond derives the discretion set and never observes a choice, and the word "risk" does not appear in his either. Nicholson-Crotty observes choices under randomized manipulation and measures no structural variable at all. The settling design is stated on [[presumed-vs-measured-public-sector-risk-aversion]]: collect D42 (external-control density), D43 (feedback quality), D44 (veto points × preference divergence) and D45 (monitoring design) on a set of organisations, then run their managers through a behavioural risk task. Nobody has done it. |
