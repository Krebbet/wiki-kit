# Open Questions

The live register of questions this wiki exists to answer. Every question carries a status and points at the
research job or page that will settle it. Questions are added here whenever an ingest surfaces something the
wiki cannot yet answer; they are never left in chat history.

**Numbering gaps are deliberate.** Q46-Q59 and Q66-Q69 are unused. Seven research jobs were ingested
concurrently on 2026-08-19 and each writing pass was assigned a reserved number range up front so two agents
could not claim the same question number. Ranges that were reserved but not filled leave gaps. **No question
has been deleted** — if a later session finds a gap, it is spare capacity, not a missing entry. Reuse the gaps
freely. Q46–Q59, Q66–Q69 and **Q74–Q89** are unused for the same reason; the
`institutions-and-growth` ingest was assigned Q90 upward. **Q95–Q99 are unused** for the same reason; the `bureaucracy-and-public-choice` ingest (2026-08-20) was assigned Q100 upward. **Q106–Q109 are unused** for the same reason; the `theory-of-the-firm` ingest (2026-08-20) was assigned Q110 upward. **Q116–Q119 are unused** for the same reason; the `measuring-institutions` ingest (2026-08-21) was assigned Q120 upward.

| Q4 | Is institutional behaviour better predicted by formal structure (rules, decision rights) or by selection effects (who ends up inside)? | partial | Outcome 2 | Untouched by the foundations-NIE sources — none names personnel selection as an axis. This is the test that would settle Q1. **First mention, and it is thin.** The OECD's Canada scan reports a middle-management "clay layer" where "innovative ideas are not evaluated by their merit, but by the personal characteristics and social capital involved" — a selection-and-gatekeeping mechanism, and precisely this question's object. It is one country scan inside an advocacy document whose own coding protocols are conceded to be undeveloped. Hood adds a moderator on the selection side (tenure security predicts blame tolerance) without measuring who gets selected. Not enough for a register row. **Moved open → partial by the `incentives-and-institutional-form` batch (2026-08-19) — and the evidence is essentially one source.** Prendergast supplies the wiki's first decomposition of selection against incentive effects, and states the headline himself: "the selection effects appear to be of roughly equal size to the incentive effects, despite the overwhelming focus on incentive effects in the theoretical literature." Two clean cases: of Lazear's (1996) 35% productivity gain from a piece-rate switch, **roughly one third is selection** — turnover data show less able workers leaving and abler ones replacing them; Paarsch & Shearer's (1996) structural estimate isolates the incentive effect on a *given* Canadian tree planter at ~10% against a raw 35% gap. Weiss (1987) and Hansen (1997) add that selection under team pay is **U-shaped**, not monotonic — medium-ability workers stay while both the ablest and the least able leave — with a *negative-slope* incentive effect underneath it. Prendergast also names the case that cannot be decomposed (Banker, Lee & Potter's store-level data), which is a standing caution against reading firm-level productivity comparisons as tests of incentive theory. **The structural gap this exposes is the important part.** Four of the batch's eight sources are *explicitly silent* on selection and say so: Holmstrom's lecture and Holmstrom & Milgrom are moral-hazard setups with agents "identical ex ante"; Grossman & Hart and Hart's lecture hold the parties' identities fixed and vary only control rights; Bénabou & Tirole's agent is the same person before and after the belief update. Dixit *models* an adverse-selection channel into public employment but measures nothing and hedges it explicitly. Frey's one selection-flavoured claim (higher-intrinsic-motivation people self-select into public employment) sits in the same paragraph as a crowding claim without the two being disentangled. So: **the distinction is evidenced by one source, in two occupations, both with unusually clean output measures** — the least representative slice of the workforce by Prendergast's own account. It establishes that a reward structure is itself a selection instrument. It does not supply a measurable property of an institution's selection regime, so no register row follows. Stays partial, not answered. See [[multitask-incentive-theory]]. **The register row finally exists [enabling-institutional-change, 2026-08-19], and this question has its first randomised evidence.** Finan, Olken & Pande supply the selection instrument itself, randomised rather than inferred from a reward structure's side-effects: **wage offers randomised across 106 Mexican recruitment sites** (better and no less prosocial applicants, fill rate +35.2%, short-run labour-supply elasticity 2.15 — Dal Bó, Finan & Rossi 2013); **job-ad framing randomised across 48 Zambian districts** (career framing drew more qualified applicants with no loss of prosocial motivation, better on-the-job performance, lower 18-month attrition — Ashraf, Bandiera & Lee 2015); and **advertised expected earnings randomised in Uganda**, the one case finding a genuine tradeoff (30% more applicants, less experienced and 24pp less likely to donate in a dictator game, no quality gain, and low-pay-recruited agents performing better in year one — Deserranno 2015). Three distinct instruments with different signs, which is why the axis is registered with four components rather than one ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D70**, `promoted` — the first item on the register's original absent-list closed by randomised evidence). Two further items belong on this question: even inside a de jure merit system, caste affinity to the sitting Chief Minister predicts plum IAS postings as strongly as measured ability (Iyer & Mani 2012), which is the de facto override of a formal selection rule; and screening *on integrity* is not currently available as an instrument, since India's civil-service exams would not filter out dishonest applicants (Hanna & Wang 2014) and the authors classify better screening design as future research. **Still `partial`, for three reasons.** Everything is measured at **recruitment** and nothing at promotion, so "who ends up inside" is answered at the front door only. The evidence is developing- and middle-income-country field experiments, 2007–2015. And the question as posed is comparative — selection *against* formal structure — and no source scores both on the same institutions. See [[personnel-economics-of-the-state]]. **The recruitment-only limitation is partly addressed [`bureaucracy-and-public-choice`, 2026-08-20], and the fix reveals a second gate the wiki had not registered.** Guo Xu splits protection into an **entry** margin (rule-based vs. discretionary selection, which changes *who* is appointed) and a **progression** margin (removal and interference cost, which changes the appointee's behaviour once in post) — registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D100**, alongside **D101** for what a selection rule actually screens *for* (preference alignment vs. ability). Two results bear directly on this row. **Aneja & Xu's staggered DiD on the Pendleton Act attributes the performance gains to the progression margin, with only limited evidence of a selection effect from competitive exams** — i.e. on the canonical merit-reform case, "who ended up inside" may not be what mattered. And the reviewers of the wider literature state that **causal evidence on entry exams is thin**: exam scores predict performance only conditional on selection, the extensive margin is "unexplored", and Moreira & Pérez find the Pendleton Act shifted customs hiring toward higher prior-occupational-status individuals **with no impact on performance**. Selection is now measured at two gates rather than one, and the front gate looks weaker than the literature's own rhetoric implies. Stays `partial`. See [[civil-service-tenure-and-political-insulation]]. |

| Q7 | Is institutional decay a function of age, of size, of the absence of competitive pressure, or of the accumulation of veto points? These are usually conflated. | partial (size) / partial (age) | Outcome 4, 6 | **The size literature does not supply age evidence, and the conflation is the field's habit rather than a finding.** The `scale-effects` batch (eight sources, 2026-08-19) reads like a lifecycle literature and is not one. Every age-flavoured claim in it is about something else: Klimek/Hanel/Thurner's "age" is the *individual official's* career tenure (age of qualification, retirement age τR), not years since the institution's founding; Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's stagnation result runs on *technology vintage* — time since the current technology was adopted; Simon's time variable is *evolutionary and assembly time*, how long a complex form takes to come into being, explicitly not post-formation ageing; Niskanen's one age claim ("older bureaus... couldn't care less") is an aside his own footnote 1 disowns, the paper being static by construction; Rajan & Wulf report firm age only as a sample statistic (~85 years) and never use it as a covariate — their sample is old by construction, so it contains no young-firm comparison. Hammond is silent on decay, on age and on risk; this constrains the limb rather than evidencing it. Bozeman's accretion mechanism is adjacent but counts external *rule-promulgating* entities, not blocking coalitions — do not merge the two counts. Successors: Q39 at the rule level, Q60 at the organisational level, and Q61 on why the field keeps substituting. **Seventh literature, and the null holds — but its character changes [`bureaucracy-and-public-choice`, 2026-08-20].** Still no institution-level age measurement anywhere in seven sources. It would be natural to infer that institution-level measurement is what the literature is missing. **Gompers, Ishii & Metrick show that inference is wrong.** Their instrument is everything the wiki wants — hand-coded from an organisation's own constitutive documents, one score per firm-year, transparent, reproducible, no perception component, ~1,500 firms a year covering >93% of US market capitalisation — and the identification problem is **completely unresolved**. They refuse a causal reading in their own words, and their conclusion sets out three rival stories that all predict every correlation they report: provisions **cause** agency costs; provisions are **defensive foresight** by managers who foresee poor performance and protect themselves; provisions are a **signal not a source**, "like a 'beware of dog' sign", so removing them would change nothing. | Q115 | **Does the WMS instrument work on a public bureau — and what would it score?** | open | Outcome 2, 3 | **The cheapest untested extension in this batch, and the row exists because nothing in the instrument's design forbids it.** D111 needs no charter, no public listing, no market price and no public record of the organisation at all — only a trained interviewer with the 18-item grid and one respondent senior enough to see across functions but close enough to still see daily operations. **It works by construction on private firms with no disclosures (90% of the 11,383-firm sample), and the same argument carries to a bureau**, which likewise has no share price and often no usable published accounts. The authors do not run it. (iii) **The failure mode**: scripted answers are more likely, not less, where the respondent is politically exposed. **What would answer it**: run the instrument on twenty agencies alongside twenty comparably sized private firms, report the item-level distributions, and check whether the low scores concentrate in the items (ii) predicts. | Q121 | **If several coders scored one institution, would the wiki's instruments survive their disagreement — and does anyone correct for it?** | open | Outcome 2 | **Asked because the wiki's most portable institution-level instrument has a rater-disagreement problem it does not treat, and this batch holds the treatment.** [[world-management-survey]] reports re-rater reliability of **r = 0.51** (p = 0.001, n = 222) between independent interviewers scoring different plant managers blind. V-Dem's measurement model says plainly that this is not enough: **reporting raw rater means ± SD is invalid whenever raters use scales differently**, and the failure is not that the mean is noisy but that it is **biased in a way the SD does not reveal** — with cited cases (Marquardt & Pemstein 2018; Lindstädt, Proksch & Slapin 2020) where naive means produce substantively wrong conclusions.
