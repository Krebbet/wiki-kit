# Open Questions

The live register of questions this wiki exists to answer. Every question carries a status and points at the
research job or page that will settle it. Questions are added here whenever an ingest surfaces something the
wiki cannot yet answer; they are never left in chat history.

Status: `open` (no evidence yet) · `partial` (some evidence, no settled answer) · `answered` (a page states the
answer and cites it) · `dissolved` (the question turned out to be malformed — say why).

| Q2 | What are the *primary* dimensions of institutional variation — the smallest set that explains the most behavioural variance? | partial | Outcome 2 | [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] registers 34 named axes with operationalisations; none is yet shown to explain variance, so the *primary* set is still open. |
| Q3 | Which of those dimensions are genuinely invariant across public and private institutions, and which only look invariant because of shared vocabulary? | open | Outcome 2 | Ostrom's empirical finding that ownership form does not predict forest condition (Hayes 2006) is real negative evidence; Williamson's remediableness symmetry is a methodological argument, not evidence. Both in commons/private settings only. **The `scale-effects` batch adds nothing: no source in it tests a firm against a bureau on the same axis.** Three are firm-only (Coase 1937; Rajan & Wulf; Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg), three are bureau-only (Niskanen; Parkinson; Klimek/Hanel/Thurner), and two *assert* invariance without testing it — Simon groups "business firms, governments, universities" as one class and never contrasts them, and Coase's 1991 lecture analogises the firm to a "little planned society" and to the Soviet economy by rhetoric alone. Niskanen's footnote 3 goes further and suggests the axis may be wrong: post offices, universities and hospitals are *mixed* — part per-unit sale, part lump-sum budget — regardless of ownership, which points at funding mechanism ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D35) rather than public/private as the operative variable. Untested. Status moved partial → open: the batch that should have addressed this did not. |
| Q4 | Is institutional behaviour better predicted by formal structure (rules, decision rights) or by selection effects (who ends up inside)? | open | Outcome 2 | Untouched by the foundations-NIE sources — none names personnel selection as an axis. This is the test that would settle Q1. |
| Q5 | What is the correct unit of analysis — the institution, the institutional field, or the rule-set? The literature mixes all three. | partial | Outcome 1 | Confirmed and specified: North uses the societal matrix (and mixes levels unflagged in the JEP essay), Ostrom the meso-level governance regime, Williamson the transaction. [[what-is-an-institution]] tabulates the mismatch. No source adjudicates. |

## Lifecycle, scale and decay

| # | Question | Status | Bears on | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q6 | Which changes with scale are near-universal (coordination cost, layer growth, risk-aversion) and which are contingent on sector or governance? | partial | Outcome 4 | Ostrom's metro-services finding that scale effects *invert* by service type (many producers better for direct services, few for indirect) is direct empirical evidence of contingency; Chhatre & Agrawal show forest size effects are non-monotonic and interact with local autonomy. All at field level, none within a single organisation. **Within-organisation evidence now exists** from `scale-effects`: hierarchy depth rises with headcount cross-sectionally in 300+ US firms with firm and year fixed effects (Rajan & Wulf), layer count rises with output in French firm panel data (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg), and Simon gives a formal reason depth must scale with complexity at all. Coordination cost with scale is therefore the best-evidenced of the three limbs named in the question; risk-aversion is still untouched. Contingency is unresolved — every one of those results is private-sector. |
| Q7 | Is institutional decay a function of age, of size, of the absence of competitive pressure, or of the accumulation of veto points? These are usually conflated. | partial (size) / open (age) | Outcome 4, 6 | **The size literature does not supply age evidence, and the conflation is the field's habit rather than a finding.** The `scale-effects` batch (eight sources, 2026-08-19) reads like a lifecycle literature and is not one. Every age-flavoured claim in it is about something else: Klimek/Hanel/Thurner's "age" is the *individual official's* career tenure (age of qualification, retirement age τR), not years since the institution's founding; Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's stagnation result runs on *technology vintage* — time since the current technology was adopted; Simon's time variable is *evolutionary and assembly time*, how long a complex form takes to come into being, explicitly not post-formation ageing; Niskanen's one age claim ("older bureaus... couldn't care less") is an aside his own footnote 1 disowns, the paper being static by construction; Rajan & Wulf report firm age only as a sample statistic (~85 years) and never use it as a covariate — their sample is old by construction, so it contains no young-firm comparison. The **size** limb, by contrast, now has real content: hierarchy depth rises with firm size cross-sectionally (Rajan & Wulf, firm and year fixed effects), layer count rises with output (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg, French panel), and decision-body performance appears to break near N≈20–21 (Klimek et al., 197-country cross-section, uncontrolled). The **competitive-pressure** limb has one negative result: Rajan & Wulf tested governance pressure against hierarchy depth and found nothing. The **veto-point** limb remains untouched. Do not let any page infer an age effect from this batch. See [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] and [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]], which now carries the one soft counter-datapoint (old, large firms restructuring substantially over thirteen years). |
| Q8 | Are there documented cases of large institutions genuinely renewing themselves without an external shock? If renewal always requires a shock, the manifesto's premise changes. | open | Outcome 3 | |
| Q9 | Does the decay pattern differ between institutions with measurable output (firms, hospitals) and those without (regulators, ministries)? | open | Outcome 3, 4 | |

| Q19 | Do Ostrom's eight design principles transfer beyond small-to-medium common-pool resources to large formal institutions (agencies, firms) with no physical resource and no measurable yield? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | Principles 3, 4A/4B and 7 are statements about decision rights, monitor accountability and state recognition and look portable; 1A/1B and 2A/2B are bound to resources with a boundary and a yield. | Q21 | Where is the boundary between resources that user self-organisation governs well and those needing assigned transferable rights — and is it set by party count, party heterogeneity, resource value, or boundary observability? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | The likely dissolution of [[self-organisation-vs-property-rights-in-commons]]. ## Raised by ingest (scale-effects, 2026-08-19)

| # | Question | Status | Bears on | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q24 | Does the knowledge-hierarchy mechanism — layers scaling with output, management by exception, the reorganisation-on-growth signature — survive where output is unpriced and often unmeasurable? | open | Outcome 2, 4 | Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's model minimises a wage bill to produce a market-priced quantity q and closes with a competitive labour market. A bureau supplies no q. If layer count in bureaus tracks budget rather than output, the mechanism is not invariant and [[functional-vs-rent-seeking-growth]] resolves toward Niskanen for that regime. Testable on public-sector personnel records. |
| Q25 | Is budget-maximising behaviour a property of **funding mechanism** (lump-sum appropriation vs. per-unit price) rather than of public ownership? | open | Outcome 2, 5 | Niskanen defines a bureau by its funding form, not its owner, and his own footnote 3 concedes that post offices, universities and hospitals are mixed regardless of ownership. The discriminating case is an organisation that changed funding form without changing ownership. This is the most likely dissolution of Q3's framing and of [[functional-vs-rent-seeking-growth]]. |
| Q26 | Is the ~N=20–21 threshold for decision-body effectiveness real, or an artefact of confounding and reverse causality — and does it apply to boards and committees inside organisations, not just national cabinets? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | Klimek et al.'s cross-section (197 countries, CIA 2007 data against HDI and political stability) has no controls for regime type, income, federalism or colonial legacy, and coalition politics inflating cabinet size is at least as plausible a causal direction. The opinion-dynamics model reproduces a transition; it does not establish this one. If the threshold survives controls it is the most directly actionable number in the wiki. |
| Q27 | Did the 1986–1998 flattening of US corporate hierarchies have any counterpart in public bureaus, or does it require product-market competition and a takeover market? | open | Outcome 3, 4 | Rajan & Wulf establish the trend robustly and explicitly do not identify its cause; their one tested explanation (governance pressure) fails. Agency org charts over the same period would be a clean comparison and the data are public. Bears on whether delayering is a lever anyone can pull or a market artefact. |
| Q28 | Can "grew by adding a layer" vs. "grew without adding one" work as a general diagnostic separating functional from dysfunctional growth? | open | Outcome 3, 4 | Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's firm-fixed-effects result gives each path a distinct wage signature — adding a layer cuts pre-existing layers' pay and knowledge, growing without one raises them. It is the only mechanism-level discriminator this wiki has between growth that is cost minimisation and growth that is not. Demonstrated on French manufacturing and nowhere else. |
| Q29 | What determines **span** — the breadth of a hierarchy at each level — as opposed to depth? | open | Outcome 2 | Three incompatible answers are on the table and none has been tested against the others: Simon's cognitive ceiling (a human is "more nearly a serial than a parallel information-processing system"), Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's manager helping-time constraint, and Klimek et al.'s promotion regime (r subordinates granted per promotion). Simon flags the gap in his own theory. Rajan & Wulf show span moving 50–86% in thirteen years at stable firm size, which none of the three predicts on its own. |
