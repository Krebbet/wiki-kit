# Open Questions

The live register of questions this wiki exists to answer. Every question carries a status and points at the
research job or page that will settle it. Questions are added here whenever an ingest surfaces something the
wiki cannot yet answer; they are never left in chat history.

Status: `open` (no evidence yet) · `partial` (some evidence, no settled answer) · `answered` (a page states the
answer and cites it) · `dissolved` (the question turned out to be malformed — say why).

The authors flag limited power and an explicit Type-II concern on the status-quo nulls. Against that, four sources in the same batch *presume* the gap without testing it — Hood (all-public evidence, private extension asserted in one clause), Bozeman (word "risk" never appears; he himself reports the public/private red-tape literature as mixed and cites Buchanan 1975 finding *private* managers more rule-adherent), Hammond (public-only, no private analogue offered), OECD (5 of 38 member states, no private comparison group). See [[presumed-vs-measured-public-sector-risk-aversion]]. Bozeman also relocates the axis the way Niskanen's footnote 3 did: he states his external-control model is "as applicable to private as it is to government organizations" and names only two government-inherent causes (sovereignty; interconnection of policy missions), reading his own data as showing external political authority rather than ownership is causal — which points at D42 rather than at the public/private dummy. **Moved open → partial by the `incentives-and-institutional-form` batch (2026-08-19), on one source.** Dixit is the first source in this wiki to *derive* public-sector institutional form rather than assert that firm results transfer, and he states the position directly: **"many differences between public and private sector organizations are ones of degree, not kind."** The mechanism is parameterisation, not different behavioural laws — the same moral-hazard and multitask mathematics, fed public-sector values on five parameters (more principals; more tasks, more often substitutes; worse output measurability; more risk-averse principals who cannot diversify policy-outcome risk; less competition), mechanically yields weaker optimal incentive slopes and more rule-boundedness. The formal core is `m = 1/(1+nrcv)`. He also denies the clean dichotomy from the other side: private firms are not the `n = 1` case either, once unions and consumer groups are counted. **The corroboration is thin and should be stated as such.** The only other public/private treatment in the batch is Hart's private-prison case, which reaches a compatible conclusion (some services belong inside government) by a *different* mechanism (residual control over non-contractible guard quality), for the make-or-buy boundary only, on one service in one country, with one correlational citation on outcomes. Everything else in the batch is firm-only, and four of the eight sources say nothing about public bureaus at all. Status is partial because the wiki now has a **specified, testable claim** where it had none — not because it has been tested. The discriminating test is Q38. See [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]]. |
| Q4 | Is institutional behaviour better predicted by formal structure (rules, decision rights) or by selection effects (who ends up inside)? | open | Outcome 2 | Untouched by the foundations-NIE sources — none names personnel selection as an axis. This is the test that would settle Q1. **First mention, and it is thin.** The OECD's Canada scan reports a middle-management "clay layer" where "innovative ideas are not evaluated by their merit, but by the personal characteristics and social capital involved" — a selection-and-gatekeeping mechanism, and precisely this question's object. It is one country scan inside an advocacy document whose own coding protocols are conceded to be undeveloped. Hood adds a moderator on the selection side (tenure security predicts blame tolerance) without measuring who gets selected. Not enough for a register row. **Moved open → partial by the `incentives-and-institutional-form` batch (2026-08-19) — and the evidence is essentially one source.** Prendergast supplies the wiki's first decomposition of selection against incentive effects, and states the headline himself: "the selection effects appear to be of roughly equal size to the incentive effects, despite the overwhelming focus on incentive effects in the theoretical literature." Weiss (1987) and Hansen (1997) add that selection under team pay is **U-shaped**, not monotonic — medium-ability workers stay while both the ablest and the least able leave — with a *negative-slope* incentive effect underneath it. Prendergast also names the case that cannot be decomposed (Banker, Lee & Potter's store-level data), which is a standing caution against reading firm-level productivity comparisons as tests of incentive theory. **The structural gap this exposes is the important part.** Four of the batch's eight sources are *explicitly silent* on selection and say so: Holmstrom's lecture and Holmstrom & Milgrom are moral-hazard setups with agents "identical ex ante"; Grossman & Hart and Hart's lecture hold the parties' identities fixed and vary only control rights; Bénabou & Tirole's agent is the same person before and after the belief update. Dixit *models* an adverse-selection channel into public employment but measures nothing and hedges it explicitly. Frey's one selection-flavoured claim (higher-intrinsic-motivation people self-select into public employment) sits in the same paragraph as a crowding claim without the two being disentangled. So: **the distinction is evidenced by one source, in two occupations, both with unusually clean output measures** — the least representative slice of the workforce by Prendergast's own account. It establishes that a reward structure is itself a selection instrument. It does not supply a measurable property of an institution's selection regime, so no register row follows. Stays partial, not answered. See [[multitask-incentive-theory]]. |

## Lifecycle, scale and decay

| # | Question | Status | Bears on | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q6 | Which changes with scale are near-universal (coordination cost, layer growth, risk-aversion) and which are contingent on sector or governance? | partial | Outcome 4 | Ostrom's metro-services finding that scale effects *invert* by service type (many producers better for direct services, few for indirect) is direct empirical evidence of contingency; Chhatre & Agrawal show forest size effects are non-monotonic and interact with local autonomy. All at field level, none within a single organisation. **Within-organisation evidence now exists** from `scale-effects`: hierarchy depth rises with headcount cross-sectionally in 300+ US firms with firm and year fixed effects (Rajan & Wulf), layer count rises with output in French firm panel data (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg), and Simon gives a formal reason depth must scale with complexity at all. Coordination cost with scale is therefore the best-evidenced of the three limbs named in the question; risk-aversion is still untouched. Contingency is unresolved — every one of those results is private-sector. **The risk-aversion limb was addressed head-on by a dedicated batch (2026-08-19) and came back empty on the scale question.** Zero of eight sources test institutional size against risk-taking. Holmström rejects the size-based (risk-bearing-capacity) account by name for firms "of even modest size"; Hammond, Kahneman & Lovallo and Nicholson-Crotty have no size variable; Bozeman's drivers are external-control density, not focal-organisation size; Manso's Section VIII is the only gesture at a size mechanism and it has no size parameter, one case and two practitioner books behind it. Kahneman & Lovallo's hierarchy-depth compounding hypothesis is the nearest thing to a size mechanism in the wiki and is the authors' own explicitly untested extrapolation. So: of the three limbs named in this question, coordination cost and layer growth have real within-organisation evidence and **risk-aversion has none, in either direction**. See [[risk-aversion-in-large-institutions]] and Q30. |
| Q7 | Is institutional decay a function of age, of size, of the absence of competitive pressure, or of the accumulation of veto points? These are usually conflated. | partial (size) / open (age) | Outcome 4, 6 | **The size literature does not supply age evidence, and the conflation is the field's habit rather than a finding.** The `scale-effects` batch (eight sources, 2026-08-19) reads like a lifecycle literature and is not one. Every age-flavoured claim in it is about something else: Klimek/Hanel/Thurner's "age" is the *individual official's* career tenure (age of qualification, retirement age τR), not years since the institution's founding; Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg's stagnation result runs on *technology vintage* — time since the current technology was adopted; Simon's time variable is *evolutionary and assembly time*, how long a complex form takes to come into being, explicitly not post-formation ageing; Niskanen's one age claim ("older bureaus... couldn't care less") is an aside his own footnote 1 disowns, the paper being static by construction; Rajan & Wulf report firm age only as a sample statistic (~85 years) and never use it as a covariate — their sample is old by construction, so it contains no young-firm comparison. The **size** limb, by contrast, now has real content: hierarchy depth rises with firm size cross-sectionally (Rajan & Wulf, firm and year fixed effects), layer count rises with output (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg, French panel), and decision-body performance appears to break near N≈20–21 (Klimek et al., 197-country cross-section, uncontrolled). The **competitive-pressure** limb has one negative result: Rajan & Wulf tested governance pressure against hierarchy depth and found nothing. The **veto-point** limb now has one source and it is a negative: Hammond's spatial model proves veto-point count and bureaucratic discretion are not monotonically related once preference divergence is allowed to vary — all 720 rank-orderings across six systems are achievable, "anything can happen" — so a count-only veto-point account of decay is unavailable as a general law from the formal literature. Registered as one composite axis ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D44) for exactly that reason. Hammond is silent on decay, on age and on risk; this constrains the limb rather than evidencing it. Bozeman's accretion mechanism is adjacent but counts external *rule-promulgating* entities, not blocking coalitions — do not merge the two counts. Do not let any page infer an age effect from this batch. See [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] and [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]], which now carries the one soft counter-datapoint (old, large firms restructuring substantially over thirteen years). **The `institutional-evolution` batch (seven sources, 2026-08-19) was commissioned as the place age evidence would finally appear. It largely does not, and one source argues against the premise.** Source by source: **Arthur's** model — the batch's only formal one, and the formal ancestor of every lock-in claim in this wiki — runs on *n*, the count of adoption events, not calendar time, and he states there is no dose-response connecting elapsed years to degree of lock-in; his one quantified threshold (30 of 100 adoptions) is a technology-market result, and he makes no institutional claim anywhere (registered and `rejected` as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D58). **North's** Nobel lecture names real intervals — ~500 years post-Rome, ~400 years of Spanish decline, ~800 years of European rise — and converts none into a threshold, a rate or a dose-response; Spain declines continuously with no point of no return identified, and he names **no falsification condition for lock-in anywhere**. **Skocpol's dataset**, reported by Pierson and the batch's only systematic longitudinal data (58 US voluntary associations since the 1830s; of the 40 founded before 1900, 19 stayed above a 1%-of-population threshold for five decades and 10 for a century or more), **measures survival, not behavioural change with age** — no rigidity measure, no structural measure, no young-organisation base rate, and read the other way it is 75% attrition over a century. **Pierson** concedes his own framework is hard to falsify, citing Geddes 1997, and grants that critical junctures are "usually attributed, often ex post". **DiMaggio & Powell** supply a genuine repeated cross-study claim — predictors of structure shift from technical to legitimacy-based as a field matures, replicated across civil-service reform, urban fiscal agencies and newspapers — but **zero measured intervals originate in their own text**, and it is *field* maturity (D57), a different variable from organisational age. **Meyer & Rowan** supply nothing on age or size and say so themselves, their own research-implications section flagging that nobody had studied structural variation "within any society across time". **Krasner's** own cited case data undercut his framing: Skowronek's "punctuation" runs 1900–1920 (20 years) against a "stasis" of 1877–1900 (23 years). **Thelen argues the premise is wrong in principle**: contestation over a rule "often begin[s] as soon as a rule is laid down", so there is no phase structure for an age-gated model to describe, and her fourth gap-mechanism runs the *opposite* way on elapsed time — as designers die and context shifts, interpretive space *opens*. **The honest position: this wiki has now searched three separate literatures for age evidence — size, risk-aversion, institutional evolution — and found no measurement of institutional behaviour against elapsed time since founding.** That is a finding about the field, not a gap in the capture. The study that would supply it is Q39. |
| Q8 | Are there documented cases of large institutions genuinely renewing themselves without an external shock? If renewal always requires a shock, the manifesto's premise changes. | partial | Outcome 3 | **Moved open → partial by the `institutional-evolution` batch (2026-08-19), and the answer depends on which of three rival models you accept — which is itself unsettled.** Thelen's whole programme is the affirmative case: layering, drift and conversion are transformations achieved with **no exogenous shock and, in two of the three, no formal rule change at all**, with worked German instances of each (Hartz-era layering of atypical-employment rules alongside untouched core protections; collective-bargaining coverage eroding while the formal multi-industry structure was strengthened by union mergers; the attempted *Günstigkeitsprinzip* court conversion). Her strongest single case runs the other way on the same point: German VET absorbed two world wars and multiple regime changes largely unchanged in form while its function reversed completely — change without shock, and shock without change, in one institution. See [[gradual-institutional-change]]. **Three caveats keep this partial rather than answered.** (i) These are *transformations*, not *renewals* — Thelen makes no claim that any of them improved performance, and her German finding is dualisation, which she treats as a warning. The question asks about renewal; the evidence is about change. (ii) Krasner states the flat negative from the same batch — all change is crisis-driven, "episodic and dramatic rather than continuous and incremental" — and neither he nor Thelen tests the other. See [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]] and [[rival-models-of-institutional-change]]. (iii) The evidence is qualitative single-country case narrative in both directions. Still the only quantitative item, and still ambiguous: Rajan & Wulf's 300+ old, large US firms restructuring substantially over thirteen years, with the cause explicitly unidentified by the authors — not obviously shock-free, given the era. |
| Q9 | Does the decay pattern differ between institutions with measurable output (firms, hospitals) and those without (regulators, ministries)? | open | Outcome 3, 4 | **The `incentives-and-institutional-form` batch supplies the front half of this question and none of the back half.** It now gives a well-specified account of how institutional *form* differs by measurability — Dixit's reading of Wilson's typology ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D50) predicts that where actions are observable and outcomes are not, an organisation will govern by standard operating procedures and evaluate by evidence that rules were followed, and where neither is observable explicit incentives must be very weak, leaving only career concerns. He places US schooling between the two. Holmstrom & Milgrom reach the same institutional form from task multiplicity rather than principal multiplicity. **But nothing in the batch is longitudinal.** Every model is static or two-period; no source observes an institution over time, and none contains a decay claim of any kind. The question as posed asks about a *pattern over time*, so it stays open — but it is now well posed: score an institution on D50, and the prediction about its form is stated in advance and falsifiable. **The `institutional-evolution` batch adds a mechanism for the no-measurable-output half and still nothing longitudinal.** Meyer & Rowan's production/institutionalised continuum is the sharpest statement the wiki has of the variable this question is really about — an organisation's exposure to a real output test — and their Proposition 6 makes a checkable prediction on the unmeasured side: institutionalised organisations **minimise inspection and evaluation**, and suppress or fail to generate technical-performance data about themselves (hospitals not publishing cure rates, schools de-emphasising achievement measures), because evaluation itself threatens the legitimacy the ceremonial structure produces. That is a decay-adjacent prediction with an observable, registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D56. It is also (b) at the weakest tier — a conceptual essay with hand-picked illustrations — and, critically, **still not longitudinal**: no source in this batch tracks the same organisations over time on either side of the measurability line. DiMaggio & Powell's field-maturity claim (predictors of structure shifting from technical to legitimacy-based) is the closest thing to a decay trajectory and originates no measured interval. See [[institutional-myths-and-decoupling]] and [[institutional-isomorphism]]. |

## Raised by ingest (risk-aversion-in-large-institutions, 2026-08-19)

| Q30 | **Are large institutions actually more risk averse — relative to what baseline, and measured how?** | open | Outcome 2, 3, 4 | **This is a correction to the wiki's own framing, and it is the central finding of the `risk-aversion-in-large-institutions` batch.** The affirmative case in that batch is mechanism-assertion; the single direct measurement cuts the other way. | Q31 | Does the loss-aversion / narrow-framing mechanism survive where decision-makers hold no equity stake and face no market review? | open | Outcome 2, 4 | Kahneman & Lovallo's evidence base is corporate executives, entrepreneurs, capital investment, M&A and process plants — settings with equity ownership, market returns and board review. Public agencies have none of the three features and a different review structure (political and media rather than board and shareholder). The mechanism is individual-psychological and might transfer intact; it might also require the payoff structure it was measured in. Neither has been tested. Cuts directly against treating that source as evidence about "large institutions" generally. |
| Q32 | Is there an aggregation from individual career concerns to institutional behaviour, and does it amplify or cancel? | open | Outcome 2, 4 | Hood, Kahneman & Lovallo, Holmström and Manso are all individual-level; each is explicit that it is. The step from many timid agents to a timid institution is taken by no source and tested by none. It is not obviously amplifying: Kahneman & Lovallo's own pooling arithmetic implies an institution holding a portfolio of many independent decisions should be *closer* to risk-neutral than any of its agents, which would make aggregate timidity evidence that the portfolio is not being pooled — a claim about internal review design (D46), not about size. Their hierarchy-depth compounding hypothesis is the opposite conjecture from the same model. The two predictions are separable and neither is tested. |
| Q33 | Can observed institutional timidity be told apart from outside — risk preference, moral hazard under a mis-specified contract, or structural constraint? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | This matters for the playbook, because the three imply different levers. Manso's result holds for risk-*neutral* agents ("the critical elements... are the likelihood ratios between the different action plans, not the agent's preferences"), and Holmström's risk-neutral manager acts risk-averse via reputational signalling. So two of the three produce the observed behaviour with no risk preference anywhere. Fixing risk attitudes would not fix either. A diagnostic that separates them on observable data — who declines what, under which contract and which review structure — does not exist in this batch and would be worth more than another mechanism. |
| Q34 | Does any structural variable (rule density, external-control layers, veto-point count, blame exposure) actually predict cautious *behaviour*, measured on the same subjects? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | **The batch's structural half and its behavioural half never touch.** Bozeman theorises rule density and never observes a choice — the word "risk" does not appear in his paper. Hammond derives the discretion set and never observes a choice, and the word "risk" does not appear in his either. Nicholson-Crotty observes choices under randomized manipulation and measures no structural variable at all. The settling design is stated on [[presumed-vs-measured-public-sector-risk-aversion]]: collect D42 (external-control density), D43 (feedback quality), D44 (veto points × preference divergence) and D45 (monitoring design) on a set of organisations, then run their managers through a behavioural risk task. Nobody has done it. |

## Raised by ingest (incentives-and-institutional-form, 2026-08-19)

*This batch is predominantly formal theory. Of eight sources, six contain no original data at all; the real tested empirics cluster in two places — Prendergast's survey (the selection-vs-incentive decomposition) and the Courty & Marschke JTPA administrative data reported by both Prendergast and Dixit. Public-bureau coverage rests on Dixit alone, corroborated only narrowly by Hart's prison case. Four of the eight are explicitly silent on selection effects, which is a structural gap in the literature rather than an oversight in the capture. The questions below are what that leaves.*

| # | Question | Status | Bears on | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q35 | Does the multitask account of bureaucratic rule-boundedness survive contact with a real bureau — and if it does, does it beat the rent-seeking account of the same rules? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | **Two untested formal mechanisms currently produce the same observable, and the wiki cannot tell them apart.** Holmstrom & Milgrom derive rule-boundedness from *unmeasurability* ("the rigid rules and limits that characterize bureaucracy... constitute an optimal response to difficulties in measuring and rewarding performance"); Prendergast derives it independently from *influence cost* — where the subjective evaluator can be lobbied, seniority rules and fixed pay bands trade allocative efficiency for reduced lobbying. All seven of Holmstrom & Milgrom's propositions are untested by their own account, and Prendergast calls his own bureaucracy model "illustrative," supporting it with one descriptive statistic and one anecdote. **They make different predictions when measurement improves**: the unmeasurability account says rules should recede, the influence-cost account says they persist as long as evaluation is discretionary. An institution that adopted a good outcome metric and either did or did not shed procedure would discriminate. See [[multitask-incentive-theory]]. |
| Q36 | Which of the three firm-boundary mechanisms — asset specificity, residual control rights, measurement cost — is actually doing the causal work? | open | Outcome 2, 5 | The subject of [[rival-firm-boundary-theories]]. All three predict the same correlation, because specificity, hold-up exposure and measurement difficulty co-occur in most real relationships, which is why the empirical literature has not separated them. **The discriminating case is a relationship with high measurement difficulty and low asset specificity, or the reverse**; no source supplies one. Sharpest single claim to test: Holmstrom & Milgrom reproduce Williamson's central stylised fact (high-powered incentives in markets, low-powered inside firms) "without relying on any assumptions about specific investments" — if that derivation holds, specificity is not necessary for the phenomenon it was introduced to explain. |
| Q37 | Does motivation crowding have a **selection** channel, distinct from the two mechanisms on offer? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | Both crowding sources are fixed-agent stories: Frey's is a change in the same person's perceived autonomy and self-worth, Bénabou & Tirole's a Bayesian update by the same agent. Neither models whether pricing an activity changes *who volunteers for it*. This is the obvious third mechanism — people who would do the work for non-monetary reasons stop applying once it is a job — and it is in none of the wiki's sources. It also matters for the field evidence: Frey's paid-volunteering result is exactly the case where the selection reading and the crowding reading are observationally equivalent, and he does not address it. A discriminating design would measure the composition of the applicant pool, not only the behaviour of incumbents. See [[motivation-crowding]]. |
| Q38 | Is **principal count** (with task substitutability), rather than public ownership, the variable that actually explains the observed public/private divergence in incentive design? | open | Outcome 2, 5 | The testable form of Dixit's "degree, not kind" claim, and the natural successor to Q25 (which asks the same question of *funding mechanism*). Dixit's model says the divergence is mechanical in `n` and `k`, not in ownership: `m = 1/(1+nrcv[1+(n−1)k])`. **The discriminating cases are the mismatches** — a private firm with many effective principals (a regulated utility, a hospital answering to insurers, regulators, physicians and a board) should show public-sector-like incentive design; a public body with few (Dixit's own example is the Social Security Administration, whose eligibility and benefit rules are settled politically in Congress and leave the agency to apply criteria and send cheques) should show firm-like design. Both classes exist and both are observable. Note the sibling test Dixit supplies and dismisses: **compartmentalisation** removes the weakening entirely in the model, so a domain where principals genuinely cannot see each other's dimensions should show strong incentives regardless of sector. If this survives, Q3's framing is wrong in the way Q25 already suspects, and D51 should be promoted over the public/private dummy. |

## Raised by ingest (institutional-evolution, 2026-08-19)

*This is the most theory-heavy batch the wiki has ingested. Of seven sources, **only Arthur is a formal model with proofs — and it is about technology adoption, not institutions.** The only genuine figures anywhere in the batch are Skocpol's association counts (secondhand via Pierson) and Wallis & North's 45%-of-1970-US-GNP transaction sector, which is not about path dependence. Three sources independently carry no falsification condition, one of them (Pierson) conceding it. The batch was commissioned as the place age evidence would appear; Q7 records that it largely did not, and Q39 states the study that would.*

| # | Question | Status | Bears on | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q39 | **Does the success rate of attempts to change a rule fall with elapsed time since that rule was laid down?** | open | Outcome 4, 6 | **The study that would settle the wiki's age question, stated as a design rather than a wish.** Every position in [[rival-models-of-institutional-change]] makes a different prediction about it and none measures it. Lock-in-deepens (North, Pierson, Krasner's stability half) predicts a declining success rate. Gradual/endogenous (Thelen) predicts approximately flat, with the *form* of successful change shifting from formal amendment toward drift and conversion as veto structures harden. Punctuated (Krasner) predicts a step function conditioned on crisis, not on age. **The data class exists and is public**: legislative amendment records, regulatory dockets and rulemaking petitions, corporate charter and bylaw amendments, and collective-agreement renegotiations all carry a dated founding, dated change attempts, and a coded outcome. What has to be got right is the denominator — the *attempt*, not the success — because measuring only successful changes selects on the outcome, which is the mistake the whole path-dependence literature makes. Second requirement: control for stake size, since attempts on consequential rules are both rarer and better resourced. Until something of this shape exists, "institutions rigidify with age" is a hypothesis this wiki has looked for in three literatures and not found. |
| Q40 | Is there any **ex ante** criterion distinguishing a shock that produces institutional discontinuity from one that gets absorbed? | open | Outcome 3, 4 | Neither rival model supplies one, and this is the axis on which they could actually be compared. Krasner offers Skowronek's qualitative crisis definition — "a sporadic, disruptive event that suddenly challenges a state's capacity to maintain control and alters the boundaries defining the legitimate use of coercion" — with no threshold of magnitude, duration or intensity, and whether the boundaries moved is typically only assessable by observing whether institutions changed, which is the outcome being explained. His own examples are heterogeneous in kind (military defeat, diffuse industrialisation, elite coup), so source-of-shock cannot be the marker either. Thelen predicts which *form* gradual change takes but says nothing about whether a given shock will be absorbed — her own strongest case is German VET absorbing two world wars, offered retrospectively. Pierson's critical junctures are "usually attributed, often ex post" by his own admission. **Three frameworks, three post-hoc classification schemes.** A criterion applied prospectively to a set of shocks, with punctuation predicted in advance, is what would turn any of them into a theory. See [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]] and [[gradual-institutional-change]]. |
| Q41 | Do any of DiMaggio & Powell's twelve hypotheses actually hold? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | **Worth asking because testability is rare in this material.** The twelve are directional, stated in terms of measurable organisation and field characteristics (resource dependence and centralisation, technological uncertainty, goal ambiguity, credential reliance, professional-association participation, number of visible alternative models, extent of state transaction, field structuration), and intended by the authors for cross-sectional or longitudinal testing against standard-deviation-based homogeneity measures. The authors tested none — "an empirical test of such predictions is beyond the scope of this paper" — and did not address expected non-linearities or ceiling effects. Two are of particular interest to this wiki. **B-2** (the more a field transacts with agencies of the state, the more isomorphic the whole field) is a public/private claim in disguise and bears on Q3. **B-4** predicts, counter-intuitively, that abrupt increases in uncertainty produce *rapid* homogenisation after only brief experimentation — which contradicts the intuition that shocks open institutional variety, and bears on Q40. The inverse case the authors never state is the design brief for a homogenisation-resistant institution: decentralised resources, few state transactions, well-understood technology, unambiguous goals, low credentialism, many visible alternative models, weakly structured field. Tabulated at [[institutional-isomorphism]]. |
| Q42 | What is observed institutional structure actually tracking — output and knowledge cost, budget-maximising rent, or legitimacy? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | The three-mechanism form of [[functional-vs-rent-seeking-growth]], which this batch extended from two positions to three. Each predicts structural growth under a different, independently observable condition: rising output or shifting information costs (Garicano & Rossi-Hansberg, Simon); a lump-sum-funding sponsor available for extraction (Niskanen); elaboration of a rationalised myth in the institutional environment, output held constant (Meyer & Rowan's Proposition 2, which makes exactly that ceteris paribus claim). **The cheapest discriminating test the wiki has identified in any conflict**: sample an organisation's formal structural elements and report the fraction actually invoked in real decisions ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D56). A low fraction is direct evidence for the legitimacy account and against both others, which each assume the structure does something. Nobody has run it on a bureau. Note the sharpest asymmetry: the first two positions assume an audit is possible and disagree about what it would show, while the third predicts the audit **will not happen**, because evaluation threatens the legitimacy the structure exists to produce. |
| Q43 | Under uncertainty, does an organisation homogenise *substantively* or merely ceremonially while its internal practice stays variable? | open | Outcome 2, 3 | The subject of [[homogenisation-vs-loose-coupling]], and a rare case of two founding papers of the *same* tradition making opposite predictions from the same antecedent — DiMaggio & Powell say so themselves at Hypothesis A-3 ("Here our prediction diverges somewhat from Meyer and Rowan"). Meyer & Rowan: ceremony insulates the work, so loosely coupled organisations vary internally. DiMaggio & Powell: ceremony pulls the work with it via imitation and credentialed hiring, so homogeneity is substantive and internal variation falls. **The discriminating measurement is the cross-organisation variance of a *practice* measure, not of an org chart** — both predict converging formal structure. A candidate scope condition, stated by neither: Meyer & Rowan's cases sit at zero output pressure (schools, hospitals, defence contractors, regulated utilities) while DiMaggio & Powell's include fields with some competitive discipline, so ceremony may convert into substance only where some output test operates. Consequence for the wiki: "sociological institutionalism says X" is not a usable citation about internal behaviour; a page must name which limb it is invoking. |
| Q44 | Why do century-old US voluntary associations persist largely unchanged while old, large US firms restructure substantially — sector, or different dependent variables? | open | Outcome 3, 4 | **Flagged, not adjudicated.** Skocpol via Pierson: 10 of 40 US voluntary associations founded before 1900 were still above a 1%-of-population membership threshold a century later, which Pierson reads as self-reinforcing persistence. Rajan & Wulf: 300+ old (~85 years mean), large (~47,500 employees), size-stable US public firms changed their senior structure substantially over thirteen years — CEO span of control up 50%, hierarchy depth down more than 25%, the COO role eliminated in about a fifth of firms that had one. Two candidate reconciliations, neither stated by any source. **Sector**: membership associations with no takeover market and no product-market test, against public corporations in the post-takeover-wave era. **Different dependent variables**: Skocpol measures whether the organisation still exists at threshold size; Rajan & Wulf measure whether its internal structure changed. An organisation can score "persisted" on the first and "transformed" on the second at once — which is precisely Thelen's form/function decoupling ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D55), and if that is the resolution then the persistence literature has been measuring the wrong thing for the age question all along. |
| Q45 | Is there an institutional analogue of Arthur's adoption count, and does it predict reversal difficulty better than calendar time? | open | Outcome 2, 4 | **The readmission condition for the `rejected` [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D58, and a real hypothesis rather than a bookkeeping note.** Arthur's proved result is that reversal cost rises without bound in *n*, the count of adoption events — not in years — and he warns explicitly against the calendar reading. The wiki's lock-in claims have inherited the conclusion while silently swapping the clock. If some institutional counter does the work Arthur's *n* does — budget rounds completed, election cycles survived, contract renewal rounds, or cohorts of staff socialised into the rule — then reversal difficulty should track that counter and *not* elapsed time, and two institutions of the same age with different cycle counts should differ. That is directly testable on the same data class as Q39 and would give the lock-in account a mechanism it currently lacks. If no such counter predicts better than years do, the analogy should be dropped rather than repeated. Note the structural obstacle Arthur's own model implies: his agents choose once and are frozen, whereas institutional rules are continuously re-enacted by overlapping cohorts with renegotiable authority, so any institutional *n* is a construct rather than a count. |
