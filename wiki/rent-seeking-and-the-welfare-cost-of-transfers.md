# Rent-Seeking and the Welfare Cost of Transfers

Tullock's 1967 claim is narrow, formal, and much sharper than the word "rent-seeking" as it is normally used — including elsewhere in this wiki. It is a **measurement claim about a specific accounting error**: the conventional Harberger deadweight-loss triangle, applied to a tariff or a monopoly, counts only the allocative loss from the distorted quantity and **excludes the resources that rational actors spend competing to obtain, or to prevent, the transfer itself**. Since the capitalised value of a transfer can be large, and since entrants invest up to the point where marginal cost equals the discounted marginal expected return, aggregate investment in pursuit of a transfer can approach or exceed the transfer's own size — and none of it produces anything. Tullock demonstrates the mechanism's independence from legality and from public/private origin by running the same argument through **theft**: the social cost of theft is not the stolen property (a pure transfer) but the thief's effort plus the locks, guards and police bought to stop him. **The paper contains no data.** Tullock says so himself: "I can suggest no way of measuring these expenditures." The term "rent-seeking" is not in it — Anne Krueger coined that in 1974, after this paper; Tullock calls it "the cost of transfers."

**Evidence tier note.** **(b) formal model, comparative-statics diagrams, zero measurement.** Built by extension on Harberger's (1959) already-accepted triangle method, plus a dynamics argument about lobbying investment and a theft/protection equilibrium diagram. Strongest content: the internal derivation, which is rigorous on its own terms and self-limiting about what it can show. Weakest: the single step from "the mechanism exists and could in principle be large" to "the welfare cost… must be quite sizable" — an assertion the paper explicitly cannot back, and the one place its conclusion outruns its argument. The only quasi-empirical touches are illustrative and unquantified: an aside that tariff collection requires customs inspectors and coast guards, and a historical comparison between the sparse lobbying of Gladstone's England and the "large and well financed lobbies" of the modern United States, offered as plausibility.

## The mechanism

**[model]** Wherever a transfer is potentially obtainable — a tariff, a monopoly position, stolen property — the **size of the prize** determines how much rational actors will invest competing for it. The investment, not the transfer, is the welfare loss.

1. A transfer of value *T* becomes available (a tariff is enactable, a market is monopolisable, property is stealable).
2. Profit-maximising entrants invest in lobbying, monopolising or thieving until marginal cost equals the discounted marginal expected return.
3. Because the properly risk-discounted capitalised value of a recurring transfer can exceed its flow value, aggregate investment in pursuit of it can approach or exceed *T*.
4. **Symmetric investment occurs on the defensive side**: customers lobbying against the tariff, incumbents defending the monopoly, householders buying locks. Defence is a cost too.
5. None of this investment has a positive product. Capital and — Tullock's one named scarce input — "that very scarce resource, skilled management" are diverted into pure distributional conflict.

The quantity that drives every prediction here is **the magnitude of the contestable transfer**. Not the size of any organisation, not its age, not its sector, not its funding form.

## The detour through theft, and why it matters

**[model]** The theft case is the paper's proof of invariance and is usually dropped when the paper is cited. Theft is a *pure* transfer — the thief gains exactly what the victim loses, so a naive welfare accounting records zero net cost. Tullock's point is that the real cost is the entire industry that grows up on both sides: the thief's time and skill, the locks, safes, guards, insurance and police, and the resources societies spend deciding how much of the protective function to provide collectively.

Two consequences the source states explicitly:

- **The mechanism does not depend on legality.** A legally granted tariff, a privately assembled monopoly and an outright crime are the same phenomenon under this accounting.
- **Public vs. private provision of the protective function is treated as a technology question, not a categorical one.** "Whether the establishment of a police force is wise or not, depends upon an essentially technological question" — is collective enforcement cheaper than the private protection plus theft it displaces? Tullock notes that societies do *not* push public provision to the point of eliminating private locks, implying an efficient mix rather than a corner solution. Registered as a framing this wiki should keep: see [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]].

## The narrowness of the claim, stated plainly

**[wiki synthesis]** This page exists partly to hold a boundary the literature routinely blurs, and this wiki has blurred it too. Tullock's proposition is:

> The conventional deadweight-loss measure **undercounts** the social cost of a transfer, by the amount of real resources spent competing to capture or defend it.

That is a claim about *what to add to a cost estimate*. It is **not** any of the following, all of which travel under the same word:

- **"Institutions capture rents"** — a claim about who benefits from an arrangement. That is [[regulatory-capture]]'s subject, and Stigler's acquired-regulation model is a *supply-and-demand account of how regulation is allocated*, not a dissipation argument. Tullock's lobbying-investment logic is a plausible micro-foundation underneath it — invest until marginal cost equals discounted marginal return — but **neither source cites the other**, and the wiki should record them as parallel rather than as one theory. Where that page's catalogue lists "implicit bribes and rent-seeking (Laffont-Tirole; Grossman-Helpman)" as one capture mechanism among several, the phrase is doing looser work than Tullock's model licenses.
- **"Coalitions accumulate and slow growth"** — Olson's thesis on [[institutional-sclerosis]], which needs elapsed national political stability and a coalition-formation process that Tullock has no analogue for. In Tullock's vocabulary a distributional coalition is a *standing investment sized to the value of a captured transfer*, which is a compatible reading and not the same claim; that page's own headline finding is that only "a handful" of 53 studies test accumulation directly, and Tullock tests nothing at all.
- **"Institutional growth is rent extraction rather than function"** — the framing of the [[functional-vs-rent-seeking-growth]] conflict, which is about *why bureaus get bigger*. Tullock has no bureau, no budget and no headcount anywhere in the paper.

**Use "rent dissipation" for Tullock's claim and keep "rent-seeking" for the loose family**, or the sharpest thing in the literature gets absorbed into the vaguest.

## Levers named

**[model — all three asserted from the model, none tested]**

1. **Free trade** — shrinks the available transfer, so it shrinks the resources invested in tariff lobbying.
2. **Active antitrust** — shrinks the expected payoff to monopoly formation, so it shrinks the investment in forming one. Tullock notes the enforcement side is itself a cost: the antitrust division and the "large legal staffs" opposing it are both on the ledger.
3. **Efficient centralised property-rights enforcement** — police and courts as a cheaper substitute for the sum of private protection and theft, up to the technological point noted above.

All three run through the same lever: **reduce the size of contestable transfers**. That is the paper's whole policy content, and it is a claim about the *prize*, not about the design of any institution.

## What this source does not contain

**[wiki synthesis — gaps, stated because this paper is cited far outside its scope]**

- **No institution.** The unit is a generic market situation involving a transfer. No organisation is profiled, no internal dynamics appear. This is pre-institutional in the wiki's sense — upstream theory that later work applies to bureaus.
- **No size, no age, no sector, no funding form.** The Gladstone-vs-modern-US contrast is about the growth of organised lobbying and the scale of available prizes, **not** an institutional-age claim, and must not be read as one. See [[open-questions]] Q7.
- **No personnel mechanism.** The one adjacent line treats skilled management as a cost input to rent-seeking, not as a personnel-system variable.
- **No measurement, by the author's own statement**, and no magnitude estimate that the model supports.
- **Scope**: every illustration is Anglo-American and market-economy, and the model presumes functioning markets, a legal system that distinguishes legal from illegal transfer-seeking, and organised lobbying as an available channel. None of that is argued to be scope-general.
