# RTO Governance, Exclusion, and Club Theory

Kiesling's *Journal of Institutional Economics* paper integrates Elinor Ostrom's common-pool-resource (CPR) design principles with James Buchanan's theory of clubs to analyse how US Regional Transmission Organizations (RTOs) govern electricity-grid reliability. The core move: grid reliability cannot be made physically excludable (AC power flow obeys Kirchhoff's and Ohm's Laws — electrons don't respect contracts), so RTOs govern it by making rule-defined *market and governance access* excludable instead, transforming an ungovernable commons into a club good. The paper's original contribution is a structural departure from Buchanan's canonical model: transmission infrastructure cannot be replicated the way a dissatisfied golf-club member can found a rival course, so the competitive discipline Buchanan's model assumes is structurally absent, and governance rules must substitute for it.

## Reliability as commons, market access as club

Electricity flow on an interconnected AC grid is **non-excludable in physics** — power injected anywhere reaches everywhere, subject to line impedance, not to contract — and **rivalrous under congestion**, since a constrained line's capacity is a fixed, shared resource multiple parties compete to use. That combination (non-excludable, rivalrous-when-scarce) is Ostrom's common-pool-resource cell in the four-way goods typology carried on [[polycentric-governance]]. Ordinary CPR governance — user boundaries, monitoring, graduated sanctions — presupposes the resource itself can be bounded. Grid reliability cannot be bounded that way at the level of the physical flow.

RTOs solve this by shifting the excludability problem up one level: instead of excluding electrons, they exclude *participants*. Registration, creditworthiness screening, metering and telemetry requirements, performance obligations, and market-conduct rules jointly define who may inject power, withdraw power, or participate in RTO governance at all. That bundle of access rules is a **club good** in Buchanan's sense — non-rivalrous in principle among admitted members, but excludable by rule rather than by physics. The paper's first contribution is naming this transformation explicitly: RTOs achieve governability of an ungovernable commons by constructing a club around it.

## Buchanan's theory of clubs, briefly

This is the wiki's first page holding Buchanan's theory of clubs as a standalone framework, so it is worth stating plainly. Buchanan (1965) analysed goods that sit between Samuelson's pure private and pure public poles: goods that are excludable (unlike public goods) but only partially rivalrous, because sharing among members lowers each member's cost while congestion above some membership size degrades the good for everyone (a golf course, a swimming pool, a toll road). A **club** is the voluntary association formed to provide and finance such a good jointly. Buchanan derives an optimal club size and toll/fee structure balancing two effects that move in opposite directions as membership grows: **sharing economies** (fixed costs spread across more members, lowering each member's charge) against **crowding costs** (each additional member degrades the good for existing members once congestion sets in). The efficient outcome sets marginal sharing benefit equal to marginal crowding cost.

Critically, Buchanan's model carries an implicit competitive-discipline assumption: if an existing club's managers set bad terms — excessive dues, admitting too many members, degrading quality — a dissatisfied member can exit and, in principle, found or join a **rival club offering the same good on better terms**. This replicability of the institutional form is what keeps club managers honest without needing external regulation; it is the club-theory analogue of market entry disciplining a firm.

## The non-replicability departure

The paper's central theoretical contribution is showing that this replicability assumption fails for transmission infrastructure, and drawing out the consequence. Building a rival transmission grid is not a matter of raising capital and opening for business: high fixed costs, network effects (the value of an interconnection depends on the rest of the interconnected system, not on the new entrant alone), and siting/permitting constraints make a competing grid infeasible as a practical matter. A dissatisfied RTO member cannot found a rival RTO the way a dissatisfied club member can join a different golf course.

This is a **structural, not incidental, departure from Buchanan's canonical model** — not derived from a reworked formal optimization, and not tested against a comparative case where replication *is* feasible (the paper offers none). The consequence the paper draws: with exit-driven competitive discipline unavailable, **governance rules and polycentric oversight must do the work that market entry would otherwise do**. Rule-revision processes, stakeholder collective-choice arrangements, and external ratification (FERC) become substitutes for the missing rival club, not merely complements to it. This reframes RTO governance rules as bearing a disciplinary burden they would not need to carry in an ordinary club setting — and it is the mechanism behind the paper's headline "promise and perils" framing: the same rules that make the commons governable, absent competitive exit, have no external check forcing them to stay efficient or open to new entrants.

## Exclusion as layered institutional technology

The paper decomposes RTO "boundary rules" — the mechanism realizing exclusion-as-club-good — into four components, each mapping onto Ostrom's design principles (carried in full on [[polycentric-governance]]):

1. **Participation criteria** (registration, creditworthiness, metering/telemetry thresholds) — the operational form of Ostrom's **user boundaries** (design principle 1A): who counts as a legitimate participant.
2. **Performance obligations** (dispatch compliance, reserve requirements, resource-adequacy commitments) — an operational analogue of **congruence with local conditions** (2A): obligations scaled to a resource's technical role in the system.
3. **Monitoring and sanctions** — a dual-track architecture combining real-time operational monitoring (flow, voltage, contingency) with market-conduct monitoring via Independent Market Monitors, plus a separate reliability-standards monitor (NERC) — three distinct bodies with different objects and different formal independence from regulated parties. Sanctions are explicitly graduated (warnings → financial penalties → credit tightening → suspension), the direct operational form of **monitoring** (4A/4B) and **graduated sanctions** (5).
4. **Cost responsibility** (network-upgrade cost allocation, capacity-accreditation methods converting physical capability into payment/eligibility obligations) — the operational form of **proportionality between benefit and cost-bearing** (2B), the principle Cox et al.'s meta-analysis (see [[polycentric-governance]]) finds "very strongly supported" among surviving commons regimes.

Collective-choice arrangements (principle 3) take a distinctive two-stage form here: sector-based, weighted voting among restricted membership categories (generation owners, transmission owners, distribution utilities, end-use customers), with supermajority thresholds for rule changes, followed by mandatory external ratification by FERC — a collective-choice-then-external-approval structure with no direct commons analogue. Nesting (principle 8) runs both vertically (local/state/RTO/FERC/NERC, with internal committee/subcommittee structure) and horizontally (inter-RTO seam coordination among formally autonomous, non-hierarchical neighboring regions) — polycentricity in both dimensions, argued to enable parallel experimentation while also multiplying veto points that raise the transaction cost of rule revision (see [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]] and [[veto-players-and-policy-stability]] for the general mechanism).

## The exclusion-innovation tradeoff

Participation rules for the seven US RTOs were substantially set in the 1990s–2000s restructuring era around centralized, dispatchable, fossil generation. The paper argues these rules are now strained by distributed energy resources, storage, and variable renewable generation that don't fit the categories the rules assume — a rule-vintage mismatch rather than an internal age-decay mechanism (this is an *external pacing* problem: institutions changing slower than the technological environment, not an institution's own behavior systematically decaying with age; see [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]] for the related North-style lock-in framing). Concretely:

- **Interconnection-queue backlog**: 2,600 GW pending as of 2024 — more than double the current US generation fleet — with 14–43 month, roughly five-year mean, processing times.
- **Cost-allocation distortion**: network-upgrade costs are assigned disproportionately to later-arriving projects in the queue, compounding delay for new entrants specifically.
- **Measurement/verification burden as indirect exclusion**: capacity-accreditation methods relying on historical performance metrics and conservative adjustment factors undercount the reliability contribution of new resource types (fast-ramping storage, aggregated demand response) — a failure mode running in the opposite direction from ordinary measurement-gaming (undercounting new entrants, not insiders gaming a metric in their own favor).
- **Rent creation**: the paper names a direct mechanism — rules that were reliability-justified when written hardened into entry barriers ("gated communities" is the paper's own phrase for RTO membership vis-à-vis DER aggregators and storage), creating rents and incentives for incumbents to shape rules to their own advantage. Relevant to [[rent-seeking-and-the-welfare-cost-of-transfers]] and, on the participation-cost-asymmetry angle, [[regulatory-capture]] — though the paper only asserts an incumbent resource/complexity advantage in stakeholder process, it does not measure rent magnitude or identify a Stigler-style acquired-regulation mechanism.

This is the sharp form of the "promise and perils" trade-off: rules that make an unbounded physical commons governable by constructing a club also raise the minimum efficient scale of participation and entrench whoever met the rules when they were written.

**Levers — explicitly prescriptive, not established findings.** The paper proposes tiered participation (graduated access scaling with a resource's system impact, in place of a binary include/exclude decision, framed as a substitute for the missing competitive/replication discipline), periodic rule-revision protocols as a "constitutional substitute for exit," and nested sub-arrangements (specialized categories for DER aggregators, microgrids, storage, tailoring telemetry/performance requirements rather than forcing new resource types into legacy categories — a self-aware instance of what [[gradual-institutional-change]] calls layering, though the paper doesn't use Thelen's vocabulary). These are illustrated, not evaluated: CAISO's DER Provider model, NYISO's storage-as-dispatchable-resource market design, and a 2024 FERC workshop are cited as examples, with no before/after outcome data, and FERC Order 2222 (the federal DER wholesale-market-access mandate) is noted by the paper's own admission to have "implementation remains slow and uneven." Treat every lever in this section as untested and illustrative.

## Evidence tier

**`[model]`** — tier (b) throughout. This is a conceptual synthesis applying two established theoretical frameworks (Ostrom's design principles, Buchanan's club theory) to a real governance case, not a hypothesis test. The paper supplies descriptive statistics (interconnection-queue size and processing times, wind/solar/storage capacity growth) as **illustration**, not as an identification strategy — there is no regression, no formal model with derived comparative statics extending Buchanan's own 1965 model, and no natural experiment or counterfactual case. The non-replicability argument, the four-part exclusion decomposition, and the exclusion-innovation trade-off are all structural/institutional arguments, asserted with more confidence in the paper's prescriptive section than the illustrative-only support for the proposed levers warrants. The author (Lynne Kiesling) writes from a market-liberalization, Bloomington-school-adjacent institutional-economics tradition sympathetic to RTO restructuring and to Ostrom/Buchanan-style voluntary design over command regulation — a steelman rather than a critical-political-economy read of RTO governance (contrast Welton 2021's public-accountability critique, cited but not adopted as the paper's own frame).
