# Reorganisation Base Rate

The UK National Audit Office audited **93 central-government reorganisations** between May 2005 and June 2009 — a near-census of one Parliament — surveyed **51** of them in detail, priced them at **£780m gross** (an under-estimate on seven grounds NAO itself lists), and concluded that value for money **"cannot be demonstrated."** The reason is not that the reorganisations were shown to fail. It is that the evidence needed to judge them was never created: no business-case requirement, no budgets set in advance, no post-hoc disclosure obligation, and **0% of departments set any metric to track the benefits they claimed**. This page also carries the batch's headline finding about reform base rates generally, because this is the source that forces the question.

**Evidence tier: (a) empirical, and the strongest design in the batch for its question — a structured audit survey at near-census scope.** Instrument: a comparable survey across the 51 largest reorganisations, a cost model imputing missing data by comparison within ten "type of change" categories, five illustrative case studies, and interviews with Cabinet Office and Treasury staff. **Weaknesses, stated by NAO or visible in it:** the survey data are self-reported by the bodies being reorganised, with no independent verification cited; the 42 smaller reorganisations are excluded from both the survey and the cost estimate; **no counterfactual is constructed** — nothing here says what would have happened without reorganising; and the Cabinet Office claimed to conduct "the equivalent of investment appraisals" for departmental reorganisations but "did not show us any evidence," an unverified self-certification NAO records but cannot test.

## NAO's actual criterion — state it precisely or the finding gets misread

**[empirical]** "Value for money cannot be demonstrated" is a claim about **evidence**, not a measured failure rate. NAO's criterion has three parts, and a reorganisation clears it only by satisfying all three:

1. **A business case** stating the objective *and why the chosen option is the best means to it* — not merely that a change is desirable. NAO found objectives stated "in very broad terms", and found that they generally never explain why this reorganisation rather than another course.
2. **An ex-ante cost and benefit projection**, with a budget set before the change, so the cost is knowable at the time the decision is made.
3. **Post-hoc tracking** of realised costs and benefits against those projections, disclosed to the body's principal.

**None of the three is required anywhere in UK central government.** No part of government is "formally responsible for monitoring or reporting on the implementation of reorganisations." So the audit's logic chain runs: no business-case requirement → no budgets set in advance → true costs hidden even from the reorganised bodies → no disclosure requirement after the fact → Parliament cannot scrutinise, though it has expressed interest → no benefit metrics → value for money cannot be demonstrated. The failure is an **accountability-infrastructure gap**, not misconduct, and NAO is careful about that.

## The numbers

**[empirical]**

- **93** reorganisations identified, May 2005 – June 2009; **51** surveyed in detail (the largest and most significant); 42 smaller ones excluded from the survey and the cost figure.
- **£780m** gross cost for the 51. NAO labels it an under-estimate on seven separate grounds: it excludes the 42 smaller reorganisations; costs falling on other bodies such as parent departments; pre-implementation and legislative-passage costs; lost expertise and institutional memory; and third-party impact.
- Cost composition: **staff 41%** (17% redundancy, 12% pay-scale harmonisation), IT and property 11%, consultancy 7%. Complex multi-body mergers averaged over **£25m**; simple accountability transfers, far less.
- Funded entirely from existing budgets under a Treasury **cost-neutrality rule** — no new money — and because costs need not be identified separately in accounting systems, the diversion happens without being tracked or disclosed. An invisible reallocation away from whatever those budgets would otherwise have funded.
- **Departments vs. arm's length bodies is the sharpest split in the report.** Benefit metrics set: **0% of departments** vs. **73% of arm's length bodies**. Board-level reporting on them: **17% vs. 61%**. Planning time: arm's length bodies averaged **16 months**; new departments can come into being **on the day the change is announced**, with no notice. NAO's own verdict on the better half: even the arm's length bodies "cannot necessarily demonstrate value for money."
- **Organisational learning is near-absent.** Two-thirds of bodies conducted a formal post-implementation review; fewer than a quarter shared it with any other government organisation; **none were published**.
- **Benefit realisation takes two or more years**, and "some reorganisations covered by our report had already been overtaken by further change" — the reorganisation cycle can outrun the benefit-realisation cycle, which is self-undermining for any institution that reorganises often.

## Churn and its legal cause

**[empirical]** Since 1980 the UK has created **25** departments, of which **13 no longer exist**. Over the same period the US created **two** (Veterans Affairs 1989, Homeland Security 2002), and both still exist. UK churn is described as similar to Australia and Canada but much higher than the US, and NAO attributes the difference directly to legal form: **US departments are created by statute; UK departments have no legal existence at all**, being administrative creatures of the Prime Minister under the Ministerial Code.

**[c — wiki synthesis]** This is a clean formalisation-versus-churn datapoint: higher legal friction to restructure produces a lower rate of restructuring and more durable institutional boundaries. It is the empirical companion to Pierson's engineered amendment thresholds ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D54), transposed from rule-change to organisational existence — and it is one country pair, not a tested relationship.

## Findings vs. recommendations — do not merge these

**[empirical, findings]** Everything above is what the audit measured.

**[b/c — recommendations, and NAO's own before/after data do not evidence them]** NAO makes six: (a) a single central oversight team with advance warning of, and power to impose conditions on, all reorganisations, including a mandatory cool-off period and a two-year review before change becomes permanent; (b) a mandatory parliamentary statement quantifying expected costs and benefits before any significant reorganisation is announced; (c) benefits stated in specific, measurable terms; (d) planned and actual costs tracked separately in financial accounting systems; (e) mandatory reporting of costs and benefits to Parliament in the body's annual report; (f) mandatory lessons-learned sharing with the Cabinet Office within two years.

**No natural experiment or comparison group is presented for any of them.** The single evidentiary thread is anecdotal and attaches to (c): the Department of Health's rationalisation of its arm's length bodies had a clear quantified target — a **1:4 cost-benefit ratio**, £61m spent tracking toward £255m of savings — and was "on course to meet" it. One positive case, not a tested mechanism.

**Note the auditor's own position.** NAO is a statutorily independent parliamentary audit body whose mandate is value-for-money auditing; its job is to find exactly this shape of gap, and its central recommendation is a new central team with expanded oversight and blocking authority — a technocratic-proceduralist prescription consistent with an audit body's institutional interest in more auditable process. This does not make the findings wrong. It does mean the recommendations are the part to discount. (The report's masthead claim of £9 returned per £1 spent is an assertion, not something this report tests.)

Also: the recommendations argue for *more* formal rules — mandatory business cases, budgets, appraisals, metrics — as the fix to a demonstrated harm. That is a candidate counter-instance to a straight reading of [[red-tape]], though NAO does not prove that the added formalisation would pay for itself.

## The private-sector comparator is a citation, not a finding

**[empirical, but not NAO's evidence]** The report cites secondary literature (Deloitte 2007, KPMG 1999, Porter 1987) claiming that fewer than half of corporate mergers and acquisitions benefit shareholders in the long run. **This is scene-setting, not NAO's own analysis, and the wiki should not treat it as a measured base rate.** What NAO does establish from its own work is a structural *contrast*: private M&A operates inside a formal disclosure and accountability regime — the Panel on Takeovers and Mergers, mandatory board and shareholder reporting on delivery of claimed savings — that government reorganisation entirely lacks. So even if the underlying non-benefit rates were similar, the accountability infrastructure wrapped around them is not. This is a candidate nuance on Dixit's "degree, not kind": possibly degree-not-kind on outcome, a real kind-difference on the machinery that would let anyone find out. See [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]].

## The base-rate finding

**[c — wiki synthesis; this is the headline of the `enabling-institutional-change` batch and the wiki's standing position]**

Of the eight sources in this batch, **only two give anything resembling a reform base rate, and both point the same way: measured success substantially overstates genuine change.**

- **NAO (this page).** 93 reorganisations, 51 audited, £780m, value for money *cannot be demonstrated*, 0% of departments setting benefit metrics. Measured success here is not even available — the measurement apparatus does not exist.
- **[[political-economy-of-public-sector-reform]] (Bunse & Fritz).** ~75% of World Bank operations rated successful against **47%** of countries showing genuine transformation, with the gap self-attributed to selecting politically easy, non-rent-threatening components.

Two populations, two mechanisms, one direction. That is the entire evidential basis, and it is worth being explicit about what the other six sources are **not**:

- **Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás is not reform evidence at all.** It measures whether the *presence* of a design principle correlates with the **survival and good management of an existing commons institution**. No case in it is an attempt to install a principle where one was absent; no outcome is measured before and after such an attempt. **Using Cox as evidence about reform is a category error** — it would substitute "institutions with feature X survive" for "adding feature X to an institution works." See [[polycentric-governance]].
- **Punctuated equilibrium gives a distribution shape, not a success rate.** Leptokurtic budget-change distributions, replicated across roughly nine countries, tell you that change when it comes is episodic and large. They say nothing about how often an attempted reform achieves its aim, and the authors disclaim point prediction explicitly. See [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-policy-change]].
- **Sabel & Zeitlin, O'Reilly & Tushman, and Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock supply mechanism or diagnosis with zero systematic counts.** Sabel & Zeitlin examine no failed case; O'Reilly & Tushman have roughly nine success narratives against one thin failure; Andrews et al. hand-pick failures and analyse essentially no successes. None of the three reports a denominator.
- **Finan, Olken & Pande supply the batch's best-identified evidence and it is not a reform base rate either.** Their ~40 RCTs and quasi-experiments measure narrow, well-identified *mechanism* effects — what a wage increase does to the applicant pool, what an audit does to unaccounted expenditure — not the probability that a programme of institutional change achieves its objective. See [[personnel-economics-of-the-state]].

**The wiki therefore has no basis to generalise a number beyond those two populations.** UK central-government reorganisations 2005–09, and World Bank public-sector lending FY2000–10. Not "reform fails X% of the time." Not a rate for firms, for regulators, for reforms outside donor lending, or for any period other than those. Anyone who wants a general reform base rate should read this as the wiki reporting that the number does not exist yet, and Q71 as the question that would produce one.

**What both populations do license** is a narrower and more useful claim: **wherever a reform's success is measured by the reforming body against criteria it also chose, the measured rate is higher than the rate of real change, and the gap has a stated mechanism in both cases** — component selection in Bunse & Fritz, absent measurement in NAO. That claim is supported twice, from two directions, in two institutional settings.
