# Ambidexterity as a Dynamic Capability

**Private-sector evidence only — and it is the only private-sector renewal source in this wiki.** Every case in O'Reilly & Tushman's paper is a for-profit firm, the vocabulary is competitive advantage and sales growth throughout, and no public-sector analogue is offered or implied. Nothing on this page should be carried across the public/private boundary without independent evidence, and the wiki has none.

The argument: incumbent firms survive technological and environmental discontinuities not through structure alone but through a senior-leadership-driven **dynamic capability** — **organizational ambidexterity**, simultaneously exploiting mature businesses and exploring new ones through **separately aligned subunits** held together by shared vision, common-fate incentives, targeted integration and senior-team conflict management. The mechanism is explicitly *not* an org-chart move: "the crucial task here is not the simple organizational structural decision... but the processes by which these units are integrated." Three things must travel with any citation of it. The evidence is roughly **five recurring success-firm narratives** restated across five propositions. There is **no base rate for ambidexterity *attempts*** anywhere in the source — the mortality statistics it quotes are base rates for the *problem*, not success rates for the *solution*. And **"dynamic capability" is partly circular by the authors' own definition**, which is why the wiki registers it as `rejected` at [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D77.

**Evidence tier — mixed, and mostly weak.**
- **(a), but cited rather than conducted by these authors:** Jansen (2006), **211 banks**, shared vision predicts ambidexterity; Lubatkin et al. (2006), **139 small-to-medium firms**, top-management-team behavioural integration predicts ambidexterity and performance; He & Wong (2004) and Venkatraman et al. (2006), ambidexterity associated with higher sales growth (sample sizes not given in the text); Tushman et al. (2007, then forthcoming), **22 innovation attempts across 13 companies**, the only cited study directly comparing ambidextrous against functional, cross-functional and spin-out designs, finding ambidextrous designs "more effective" except for product substitutions — a directional finding with **no success/failure count reported**.
- **(b), and load-bearing for the narrative:** four or five named single-firm case narratives — USA Today/Gannett, Ciba Vision, IBM's Emerging Business Organizations, Analog Devices, NUMMI — **re-used across all five propositions**. They are not a sample; they are the same stories told again for each claim.
- **Document status:** a 2007 Harvard/Stanford **working-paper draft**, not a peer-reviewed final version, containing visible unresolved authorial marginalia questioning its own examples. The paper is a literature review plus inductive proposition-generation from "more than 40 studies", offered by the authors as a basis for *future* empirical testing.

## The five propositions

**[b — inductively derived, untested as a bundle]**

1. A compelling **strategic intent** justifying both exploitation and exploration. Illustrated by USA Today and IBM's EBOs, against Intel's Andy Grove dismissing a later-valuable non-core business as a "distraction".
2. Common **vision and values** giving a shared identity across differentiated units (Ciba Vision's "healthy eyes for life"; the J&J Credo). Narrative only, though Jansen's 211 banks support the vision fragment.
3. **Senior-team consensus**, relentless communication, **common-fate rather than line-of-business incentives**, and willingness to remove dissenters. Partially supported by larger-N citations (Beckman 2006 on team diversity; Jansen; Lubatkin et al.) — but the removal component rests on three narratives.
4. **Separately aligned subunit architectures** — competencies, incentives, systems, culture and structure each aligned to that subunit's task — plus **targeted** strategic and tactical integration (USA Today's joint editorial meetings and shared sales force; IBM's EBO asset-sharing).
5. Senior leadership **tolerance of contradiction** and active conflict resolution — "strategic debate" (Burgelman 2002); Analog Devices' literal soundproofed argument room.

**No study in the source tests the five-proposition bundle against a systematic sample of ambidexterity attempts, successful or failed.**

## What the senior-team mechanism actually is

**[b — the source's own cases, read plainly]** "Senior-team consensus" is achieved in the paper's examples largely by **unilateral executive removal**, not by deliberation: Tom Curley replaced 5 of 7 senior managers at USA Today; Ciba Vision's president replaced 60% of the senior team; Lou Gerstner replaced nearly his entire senior team at IBM and is quoted approvingly on the value of "public hangings" to enforce focus. The paper frames this as a success factor and does not interrogate it as a power question — there is **no discussion of who holds the CEO accountable**, of board oversight, or of minority-shareholder protection in this process. Worth flagging on a wiki that tracks decision rights: the mechanism named "consensus" is, in the evidence offered, concentrated removal power.

## Failure modes, including the sharp one

**[b]** Competency trap (Leonard-Barton 1992: success entrenches exploitation and crowds out exploration); failure trap (March 2003: inexperience drives constant strategy-switching); success syndrome / paradox of success (the structures, culture and people that produced success become the source of inertia); predictable surprises (Bazerman & Watkins 2004: senior teams overweight current threats and discount long-term ones).

The genuinely useful addition is **resource rigidity vs. routine rigidity** (Gilbert 2005). A firm can hold all the assets needed to adapt — not resource-rigid — and still fail because management cannot reconfigure how those assets are used. The authors' reading of their own failure cases (Christensen's disk-drive incumbents, Polaroid, Firestone, NCR) is that **resource rigidity was usually not the problem; routine rigidity was.** That is sharper and more falsifiable than "inertia" as a blanket term, and it is registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D75.

**A cited finding that cuts against the thesis, and the source reports it.** Ebben & Johnson (2005), **300 small companies**: firms pursuing both efficiency and flexibility underperformed single-strategy firms — "stuck in the middle". The authors use this to argue that ambidexterity requires *structural* separation rather than blended strategy. It is equally evidence that unmanaged attempts at ambidexterity underperform focus, and **no comparable failure-rate study is offered for firms that attempted the specific design the paper recommends.**

## The base-rate problem, stated precisely

**[empirical — but of the problem, not the solution]** The mortality statistics the paper quotes are real and are somebody else's: of the largest 266 US manufacturing firms in 1917, only **28** remained on the list by 1997, and 49% of firms in that three-period sample appear once and vanish (Louca & Mendonca 2002); S&P 500 firm life expectancy fell from **90 years (1935) to 30 (1975) to 15 (2005)** (Foster & Kaplan 2001); of 266 firms tracked 1984–2004, only a small number were financially successful throughout (Devan et al. 2005); of 6,772 firms across 40 industries over 25 years, only a small minority showed sustained superior performance (Wiggins & Ruefli 2002). Set against those, Figure 1 lists long-lived firms averaging **105 years** that each compete today in a different industry than the one they started in (GKN, 245 years; Harris Corp, 100; B.F. Goodrich, founded 1870; Tandy, 1898).

**None of this is a success rate for ambidexterity.** No statistic of the form "of firms that attempted structural ambidexterity, X% achieved genuine renewal" appears anywhere in the source. The high mortality establishes that the problem is real; it says nothing about whether the proposed solution works.

**Selection on the dependent variable: confirmed and severe.** Roughly nine success narratives (USA Today, Ciba Vision, IBM, Analog Devices, NUMMI, Mettler-Toledo, Oticon, Bang & Olufsen, NCR) against **one thinly described failure** (Durisin & Todorova 2003, a leader unable to build an aligned new culture). Firms that attempted structural separation and failed are not covered. See [[open-questions]] Q72.

## Why "dynamic capability" is registered as rejected

**[c — wiki synthesis, on the authors' own definition]** The authors require that a dynamic capability be **consciously and explicitly managed by senior leaders** and **repeatable**, and use that requirement to **exclude** successful adaptations that were not deliberately designed — HP's culture-driven practice of spinning off a division once it passed **1,200 employees** (later lost when HP centralised financial controls), and B.F. Goodrich's WWII-forced pivot into chemicals. The adaptation outcome in those cases is identical to the cases that do count; only the visible deliberate process differs. So the construct runs: success plus visible deliberate process = dynamic capability; success without visible deliberate process = luck, excluded by definition. **No firm anywhere in the source is identified as having the capability and then failing.**

The authors half-acknowledge the problem, citing Eisenhardt & Martin's (2000) critique that without further specification "dynamic capabilities" is vague and "add[s] little other than terminology", and offering their five propositions as that specification — but they propose **no ex-ante measurable instrument** for the construct independent of the outcome it explains. Registered `rejected` as D77. **Readmission condition: an instrument for "consciously managed and repeatable" that can be scored on a firm before the renewal outcome is known.**

## What is not established

- **Nothing about public institutions.** Not tested on public, nonprofit or government bodies at all. This is the wiki's only private-sector renewal source and it must be labelled as such wherever it is used.
- **Nothing about small firms in the affirmative.** The one small-firm-focused finding cited (Ebben & Johnson) runs against the thesis.
- **Simultaneous vs. sequential** pursuit of exploration and exploitation is contested in the source's own words: "empirical evidence... is contradictory".
- **Scope:** overwhelmingly US multinationals with a few European cases (Ciba Vision, Mettler-Toledo, Oticon, Bang & Olufsen); large, established manufacturing, tech, media and consumer-goods incumbents; case narratives from the 1970s–2000s.
- **Prior:** squarely the management-strategy adaptationist camp, positioned against organisational ecology's inertia school and against Christensen's spin-out prescription, with a normative premise stated in a footnote — "it is senior management's job to keep organizations alive and thriving... not to assist in their death." Heavy self-citation: the framework and many of the cases are the authors' own prior work, and this paper is a direct precursor to their trade book *Lead and Disrupt*.
