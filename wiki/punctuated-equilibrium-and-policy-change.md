# Punctuated Equilibrium and Policy Change

True, Jones & Baumgartner's chapter states the policy-process version of punctuated equilibrium: policymaking systems produce long stasis broken by rare large changes, because **bounded rationality (serial attention) interacts with disaggregated political institutions**. The empirical signature is distributional — annual budget and policy changes are **leptokurtic**, a tall narrow central peak with weak shoulders and fat tails, diverging from normal even after the top 5% of outliers are dropped — and it has been replicated across roughly **nine countries and multiple independent datasets**, not one study. The load-bearing qualification for anyone reasoning about reform: **a crisis or attention shift opens a window; it does not guarantee change.** The authors state it directly — "Agenda access does not guarantee major change... reform is often blunted in the decisionmaking stage. But this access is a precondition for major policy punctuations."

**This is a theory of policy output dynamics inside a system of institutions, not a theory of how one institution reforms itself.** The unit is the policy subsystem or budget subfunction, not the agency as an organisation.

**Evidence tier — the split is sharp and the authors are candid about it.**
- **(a) strongest, and the chapter's own contribution:** US federal budget authority by OMB subfunction, FY1947–2003, 60 programmatic subfunctions, annual percentage change in inflation-adjusted authority, analysed as a distributional claim and replicated by the authors at subfunction, function and agency levels. Independently cross-validated on US local expenditure (Jordan 2003), Texas school districts (Robinson 2004), US state budgets (Breunig & Koske 2005), US national outlays since 1800 (Jones & Baumgartner 2005), the UK (John & Margetts 2003; Soroka, Wlezien & McLean 2006), Denmark, Germany (Breunig 2006), France (Baumgartner, Francois & Foucault 2006) and Belgium (Walgrave 2005).
- **(b) weaker:** the institutional mechanism itself — policy images, monopolies and venue-shopping — rests on qualitative case narratives (the 1968 US crime-policy surge, the collapse of the nuclear-power policy monopoly, the Belgian Dutroux scandal, EU agenda-setting).
- **(b) weakest:** the "general punctuation hypothesis" extending the pattern to any information-processing system, argued by analogy to sandpiles, earthquakes and Barabási's heavy-tailed human task prioritisation. Asserted, not demonstrated at comparable rigour.

## The dual mechanism

**[b — theory, empirically motivated]**

1. **Institutional.** Separated powers, overlapping jurisdictions and multiple venues (federalism, courts, agencies, Congress, states) mean issues normally live inside insulated **policy subsystems** run by specialists — "policy monopolies" sustained by a dominant supportive **policy image**, with negative feedback damping change. When the image is successfully contested and the issue is pushed into **macropolitics**, previously excluded actors pile in through the available venues and a positive-feedback cascade — the authors' words are "bandwagon" and "feeding frenzy" — overwhelms the old subsystem and can lock in new institutional arrangements.
2. **Cognitive.** Simon's bounded rationality, extended by Jones: individuals and organisations process information **serially**, attending to one or a few things at a time rather than integrating everything in parallel. The result is **disproportionate information processing** — long underreaction to a signal, then overreaction once attention shifts.

Institutions in this account **add friction** that delays response until pressure accumulates enough to force a lurch. The authors reject "gridlock" as the description of the US system: "friction... rather than institutional gridlock characterizes American national political institutions." The corroborating measurement is that agenda *inputs* (hearings, press coverage, bill introductions) are **less** leptokurtic than policy *outputs* (laws, budgets) — the institutions are what add the punctuation.

## What the distribution does and does not give you

**[empirical, with an honest gap]** The chapter reports the *shape* — clearly non-normal, replicated across countries and levels of aggregation — and **does not quote a kurtosis statistic or any extractable rate**. There is no "X% of subfunction-years are punctuations" here; the numeric values are in the underlying papers (Jones et al. 1996, 1998), not this chapter. A systems-level regularity is claimed — "some policy punctuation is under way almost all of the time" — and is explicitly *not* a claim that any given policy area is likely to be reformed in a given year: "a complete model will not be locally predictable."

**[c — wiki synthesis]** So this source contributes a **distribution shape, not a success rate**. It cannot tell you how often an attempted reform achieves its aim, and it does not try to. See [[reorganisation-base-rate]] for the wiki's position on which sources in this batch do supply a reform rate — two of eight — and why this is not one of them.

## Scale: punctuation is level-dependent and transmits asymmetrically

**[empirical]** The most reusable structural finding here, and directly relevant to the wiki's scale work. Punctuations are **more pronounced at lower levels of aggregation** — subfunctions more leptokurtic than functions, functions more than higher aggregates. And transmission is **asymmetric**: top-down shocks (fiscal stress originating at the macro level) propagate down the hierarchy more readily than bottom-up punctuations propagate up, because parallel processing inside insulated subsystems damps spillover between them.

**No age claim.** Nothing in this chapter ties punctuation dynamics to years since founding. The variable is hierarchical level, not elapsed time. Do not read it as age evidence — [[open-questions]] Q7 stays where it is.

## Crisis opens a window; it does not deliver reform

**[empirical/b]** The distinction is load-bearing for anything prescriptive and the source supports the weaker of the two readings:

- Agenda access is a **precondition** for major punctuation, not a sufficient condition; Kingdon's "window of opportunity" language is used approvingly for the 1968 crime-policy case.
- **Subsystems can successfully resist** conflict expansion into macropolitics — Worsham (1998) shows subsystem types differ in their ability to block it, and Wood (2006) documents federal land-management "conflict management strategies" doing exactly that.
- **Venue-shopping can backfire on the shopper**: "losing in a narrow venue does not mean winning in a broader one; it could invite even bigger losses" (Guiraudon, on immigration).
- Change is stochastic at the system level and non-deterministic at the case level. The theory disclaims point prediction.

**Selection on the dependent variable — a split verdict, and the split is the point.** The chapter's own budget-distribution evidence is **not** selected on crisis or reform: it is a full unconditional time series of all subfunction-years, capturing punctuation and non-punctuation alike, which is the design's real strength. The *mechanism* narrative is a different matter — every qualitative case is one where mobilisation did produce macropolitical change. No matched case is presented of a crisis that reached the macropolitical agenda and produced nothing, nor of major reform occurring without any attention spike. The caveats above are cited, not counted. See [[open-questions]] Q72.

## Named axes, and the levers other people supply

**[model]** Axes named here: number and independence of policy **venues** (registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D74, **merged into D14 on 2026-08-20** — D14 is the live row); parallel vs. serial issue-processing capacity of a decision structure; institutional friction / supermajority requirement; executive strength; bureaucratic information-processing capacity; regime openness.

**[b — all four are secondary citations, none is the authors' own tested finding]**

1. **Multiple independent venues** enable mobilisation to break monopolies — qualitative case record, and shown to backfire sometimes.
2. **Regular incremental adjustment substitutes for future disruptive reorganisation**: "incremental adjustments in state administrative reorganizations can act to obviate the need for sweeping reorganizations in the future" (Berkman & Reenock 2004). One citation, not replicated here. **This is the batch's only lever that speaks directly to [[reorganisation-base-rate]]'s subject matter, and it is a single unreplicated study.**
3. **Stronger central executives attenuate punctuation magnitude** (Breunig & Koske 2005).
4. **Bureaucratic capacity smooths adjustment**: "more bureaucratic school systems adjust their expenditures to fiscal reality [better] than do less bureaucratic ones" (Robinson 2004) — framed as better information processing, notably *not* as red tape.

## Relation to the wiki's existing change models

**[c — wiki synthesis]** The wiki already carries [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]] (Krasner 1984), which is the same metaphor arriving through a different literature, and [[gradual-institutional-change]] (Thelen), which denies the phase structure. Three things separate the present source from Krasner's version and they all favour this one as the citable form of the claim:

- **Krasner supplies no primary evidence and no ex ante criterion** for when a shock punctuates rather than being absorbed; his own strongest cited case data (Skowronek's periodisation) sit awkwardly with the "short burst" claim. True, Jones & Baumgartner supply a measured distributional signature over a full unconditional time series, replicated internationally.
- **The microfoundation differs.** Krasner's stability mechanism is sunk cost; this chapter's is serial attention plus institutional friction, with the input/output leptokurtosis gap as its measurable trace.
- **This version is weaker about crises.** Krasner states the flat position that all change is crisis-driven, "episodic and dramatic rather than continuous and incremental". True, Jones & Baumgartner state the probabilistic version — necessary, not sufficient, and not locally predictable. **Where the wiki needs a punctuated-equilibrium claim, prefer this formulation and cite Krasner for the state-theory lineage.** Neither is the wiki's adopted position; see [[rival-models-of-institutional-change]].

The complement to [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]] is exact and worth stating: that page explains why institutional matrices lock in; this one supplies the mechanism by which locked-in systems occasionally release, and adds quantitative evidence the lock-in literature lacks.

## What is not established

- **No private-sector claim.** The theory is scoped to public policymaking and government budgeting; cross-*national* generality is stressed repeatedly, cross-*sector* generality never. Do not extend to firms.
- **No mechanism for how subsystem specialists get their positions.** The "who selects, who can remove" question is not analysed.
- **"Policy monopoly" is not framed by the authors as capture or rent protection** — the mapping to the wiki's capture vocabulary is the wiki's, not theirs.
- **Applicability to centralised or non-democratic systems is raised as an open question and not resolved.** The theory is described as "particularly apt" for open multi-venue democracies, though France and Belgium show the same distributional pattern.
