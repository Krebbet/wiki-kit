# Veto Points and Bureaucratic Autonomy

Hammond derives the size of a bureaucracy's zone of unilateral discretion — the **core**, the set of policies no decisive coalition of elected officials can overturn — from the geometry of veto points and the preferences of the officials occupying them. The headline result is negative and it is the reason this page exists: **there is no logically necessary relationship between veto-point count and bureaucratic autonomy.** Comparing six stylised systems pairwise, Hammond shows all 720 possible rank-orderings of core size are logically achievable once preference profiles are allowed to vary. More veto points create more independent blocking coalitions, which *can* enlarge the equilibrium set — but need not, and the direction depends on the interaction with preference divergence, not on the count. "Anything can happen." The methodological consequence he draws is that empirical work regressing outcomes on veto-point counts while omitting the preference profile will have its results "ascribed, erroneously, to just the impact of the institutional variables." The page also now carries two **contested cases** — Fukuyama's vetocracy and Schleicher & Bagley's procedural tax — which argue the count-only position Hammond's result forecloses, and which each partly undercut themselves.

**Evidence tier note.** (b) **Pure formal deduction.** Six spatial models with worked numerical examples over nine hypothetical legislators; unidimensional policy space, complete information, single-peaked preferences. No empirical data on real bureaucracies or real veto-point counts appears anywhere. Others' empirical work (Tsebelis 1999, Bawn 1999, Weaver & Rockman 1993) is cited in passing and nothing is tested. Strongest content: the proof structure — 15 pairwise comparisons across 6 systems, airtight *given the assumptions*. The paper's own "Possible Criticisms" section flags those assumptions as unrealistic (no committees, budgets, courts or removal procedures) and admits the net effect of adding realism is unknown.

## What the model contains

**[model]** The unit is a national democratic *political system* — unicameral parliament, bicameral parliament, presidential separation of powers — and the agency embedded in it. The veto points are **external elected principals** (House, Senate, President, governing party or coalition), not internal layers of the agency's own hierarchy. A policy survives as an equilibrium iff no decisive coalition prefers an alternative to it; bureaucrats hold residual decision rights only within the resulting core, so bureaucratic power is defined *negatively*, as whatever the elected principals' collective veto structure fails to reach.

The named axes, with their status:

- Number of institutional veto points (1 / 2 / 3).
- Number of parties per veto point (1 / 2 / 3+).
- **Preference-profile divergence among veto players** — treated as a variable independent of and *interacting with* the count. Hammond's central methodological point is that this axis is routinely omitted from empirical work.
- Agenda-setting monopoly vs. contestable agenda control (can the opposition propose motions?).
- Party or coalition discipline (perfect vs. imperfect — can members defect?).
- Opposition voting behaviour (automatic bloc opposition vs. preference-based).

Named but explicitly *not* modelled: committee gatekeeping, veto-override provisions beyond one footnote, nomination/confirmation and removal procedures for agency heads, the appropriations process, judicial review, and the informational asymmetry between bureaucrats and elected officials — the Weberian expertise channel, which Hammond distinguishes explicitly from the "divide and conquer" structural channel he models.

**[model — the one monotonic result]** A veto-override mechanism "will never increase the size of the core but can decrease it" (n.7). This is the only lever in the paper with an unambiguous direction. Every other structural variable's net effect on autonomy is stated to be ambiguous — and that ambiguity *is* the paper's contribution.

## What this page must not be used to support

**[wiki synthesis — and the reason this caveat is prominent]** The intuitive story runs: more veto points → more gridlock → more caution → institutional risk aversion. **Hammond's result undercuts that story rather than supporting it.** Three separate things go wrong with citing him for it.

1. **The source never uses the word "risk".** It is about the *size of a discretion set*, not about how bureaucrats behave, cautiously or boldly, within it. Autonomy and risk-taking are different variables here, and converting one into the other silently is exactly the move the page exists to block.
2. **Even the autonomy claim is non-monotonic in the count.** Veto-point count and core size are proved not to be monotonically related. Any "more veto players ⇒ more X" law is unavailable from this model as a general result. If a bridge to caution is wanted, note that it cuts *both* ways: a *narrow* core — few effective veto points, or veto players in close agreement, as under unified party government — means almost any bureaucratic deviation from the status quo can be overturned by a unified coalition, which would itself incentivise defensive status-quo bias. Fewer veto points can imply more caution.
3. **These are the wrong veto points for the usual claim.** Hammond's veto points sit *outside and above* the bureaucracy, in the constitution and the legislature. What "large institutions are risk averse" usually means is a proliferation of sign-offs and review gates *inside* the organisation. The two are different objects, and this source speaks only to the first.

The register records this as a single composite axis rather than two, precisely so that a count-only row cannot be read off it. See [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D44.

## Structure without individuals

**[wiki synthesis]** Hammond's model is purely structural in a narrow and useful sense: it derives autonomy from the geometry of preferences and voting rules among external elected principals and contains **no individual-level mechanism at all** — no career concerns, no blame or credit asymmetry, no reputational payoff for any bureaucrat. It is therefore not evidence for the individual-payoff channel that [[blame-avoidance-and-negativity-bias]], [[career-concerns-and-managerial-risk-taking]] and [[exploration-exploitation-incentive-contracts]] all run on. In the umbrella page's two-by-two, this is the cleanest instance of a purely structural mechanism — and it is also the one that declines to predict.

## What the model is silent on

**[wiki synthesis — gaps]**

- **Size and age: nothing.** No agency headcount, budget, internal hierarchy depth, or years since founding. Entirely about the external political system, orthogonal to the bureaucracy's own scale. A null for the wiki's priority theme; absence, not evidence of no relationship.
- **Sector: public only, and no private analogue is offered.** Elected officials as principals, bureaucrats as agents, veto points as constitutional and legislative institutions. A board, an activist shareholder or a regulator is not treated as a veto player anywhere in the text. Any such extension is the wiki's inference, not Hammond's.
- **Economic production: not addressed**, beyond a throwaway mention of appropriations as an unmodelled extension.
- **No institutional failure modes named.** The paper's only "failure mode" is methodological — the veto-point/preference-profile confound in cross-system empirical research.
- **No prescription.** The paper is positive by design and offers no reform recommendations.

**Scope.** Democratic political systems, national-level executive and legislative institutions; Westminster-style unicameral, generic bicameral parliamentary, and US-style presidential separation of powers. Situated in the 1990s–2000s positive-political-theory literature (Tsebelis, Hammond & Knott, Hammond & Butler).

**Balance note.** The paper is unusually self-limiting: its central result destroys the parsimonious, intuitively appealing story a less careful theorist would have pushed, and it spends a section arguing its own model is descriptively incomplete.

## Contested cases: veto-point count as itself capacity-degrading

**Status: contested, not adjudicated.** Two sources from the `institutional-stagnation` batch (2026-08-19) treat veto-point or procedural **count and density as itself capacity-degrading** — which is precisely the position Hammond's model says is unavailable as a general result. Neither engages Hammond; Hammond, writing earlier, does not engage them. Nothing here resolves the disagreement, and the page does not pick a side.

**[wiki synthesis]** Fukuyama's version. See [[political-decay-and-vetocracy]]. The US has "far more of these checks and balances, or what political scientists call 'veto points,' than other contemporary democracies, raising the costs of collective action and in some cases mak[ing] it impossible altogether." "Vetocracy" is named as a decay mechanism in its own right — not as a permissive condition for one, but as a cause.

**[wiki synthesis]** Schleicher & Bagley's version. See [[state-capacity-and-level-of-government]]. Procedural and participatory density functions as a tax on government action: "It's useful to think of that as imposing a tax on government action... As with any tax, however, taxing more means getting less." Their case that state administrative law degrades capacity *more* than federal law is built almost entirely on demonstrating greater procedural **density** — more little-NEPAs, more legislative-veto states, looser standing, weaker judicial deference — which is a pure count-and-intensity argument. **They never address whether preference divergence among the relevant veto players differs between the state and federal levels**, which is exactly the term Hammond's mechanism requires before a count can mean anything.

**Both sources partly undercut their own count-only reading, and this is the part worth keeping.**

- **Fukuyama gates the effect on polarisation.** The Madisonian veto structure is roughly 230 years old and unchanged in count, and by his own account it worked adequately for long stretches: the system "served to moderate the will of the majority" in earlier periods when one party or another was dominant, becoming "a formula for gridlock" only in the evenly balanced, highly competitive party system that arose **since the 1980s**. Polarisation is a proxy for preference divergence. So his own historical argument is structurally **closer to Hammond's interaction claim than to a count-only story** — the count did not change, the divergence did. His headline rhetoric reads count-only; his history reads interactionist. Both readings are recorded here because the source supports both.
- **Schleicher & Bagley's own best longitudinal evidence cuts the other way.** Caughey & Warshaw find state policy reaching roughly **70% majority-alignment within three decades** — slow, but not the comprehensive unresponsiveness a density-tax account implies. The authors surface this themselves and decline to resolve it.

**[wiki synthesis] What would settle it.** A measurement of **preference divergence among the veto players, taken on the same jurisdictions where procedural density is counted**. Every party to this dispute counts one side of Hammond's product and asserts the other. Fukuyama supplies a divergence story (polarisation) with no count comparison across time; Schleicher & Bagley supply a count comparison across tiers with no divergence measure; Hammond supplies the proof that neither alone licenses a prediction. The discriminating case is a high-density jurisdiction whose veto players' revealed positions are close together — Hammond predicts a small blocked set there, the density-tax accounts predict degradation regardless.

One caveat already on this page applies unchanged to both new sources: these are **external** veto points — constitutional, legislative, judicial, and procedural-access — not internal sign-off gates within an agency's own hierarchy. The two remain different objects, and neither Fukuyama nor Schleicher & Bagley closes that gap.

**A third, much weaker instance (2026-08-25).** A Niskanen Center policy essay on US electricity-grid interconnection permitting treats sequential federal/state/local review layers as straightforwardly and substantially suppressing supply — "massively clogged," costing "tens of gigawatts per year" — with no discussion of what the reviewing parties' preferences are or when added layers would *not* bind. This is the same count-alone reading Fukuyama and Schleicher & Bagley make, applied to a live 2026 sector, but at a markedly lower evidence tier: a short advocacy essay with no dataset, case study or formal model behind its headline claim, versus Fukuyama's historical narrative and Schleicher & Bagley's cross-tier procedural-density comparison. Filed here rather than as a standalone conflict page because it adds no new argument to the dispute already stated above — only a fresh illustration of the same unmeasured gap (no preference-divergence data offered for the veto players in the interconnection pipeline). See [[reform-levers]], where the source's four prescriptive levers are filed in tier (iii).
