# Measuring Trust (Glaeser, Laibson, Scheinkman & Soutter)

The paper's headline finding directly resolves — in the wrong direction for the existing literature, and hence in the right direction for this wiki's caution — a measurement question [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D32 has flagged since it was registered: **standard attitudinal trust surveys (the GSS "most people can be trusted" item) predict trustworthy behavior much better than they predict trusting behavior.** Ten of twelve attitudinal trust measures tested showed no significant correlation with actual trusting behavior in an incentivised trust game; the same GSS-based index correlated 34% with how much subjects actually *returned* when trusted.

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]** — an incentivised lab design (258 Harvard undergraduates surveyed, 96 senders in a real-money trust game modelled on Berg, Dickhaut & McCabe 1995, plus an envelope-drop valuation task), not anonymous between paired subjects (unlike the original design), which lets the paper additionally measure social-connection effects.

## Trust and trustworthiness are different constructs, with different predictors

**[empirical]** **Trust** (operationalised as amount sent in the trust game, or mean reservation value for a hypothetical lost envelope) is predicted by **self-reported specific past behaviors** (lending money or possessions, leaving doors unlocked) — a behavioral index correlating 22.4% with amount sent — not by attitudinal survey items. **Trustworthiness** (the return ratio: amount returned / amount available to return) *is* predicted by the standard GSS attitudinal trust index. The authors' own stated takeaway: "to determine whether someone is trusting, ask him about specific instances of past trusting behaviors. To determine whether someone is trustworthy, ask him if he trusts others." Two narrowly-worded items specifically about trusting *strangers* (not the general GSS item) did predict trusting behavior in both experiments — attributed to matching the specificity of the experimental task.

## What this does to the existing literature, in the authors' own words

**[empirical]** "These findings suggest that the standard trust questions may be picking up trustworthiness rather than trust... This means that most work using these survey questions needs to be somewhat reinterpreted." Social connection (time known, common friends) predicts both trust and trustworthiness; cross-race/nationality pairing has little effect on amount sent but a large, significant negative effect on the return ratio — 92% of zero-return cases were cross-race pairs, against 59% of pairs overall.

## Directly extends D32's operationalization

**[wiki synthesis]** D32 was registered as `candidate` with the explicit note "no settled theory of the individual underlies this, so the measure currently outruns the theory." This paper does not supply that theory, but it supplies something more immediately useful: a **validated behavioral benchmark** (incentivised trust-game/envelope-drop elicitation, or at minimum specific-behavior self-report) against which any candidate survey item for D32 can now be checked, and a stated, evidenced reason to distrust the field's dominant instrument for the specific construct D32 names. [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D32's operationalization is extended accordingly. Note the important caveat this wiki should carry forward with the finding: the sample is small (N≈90–190 per regression) and non-representative (Harvard undergraduates), and several results (e.g. the only-child effect) are explicitly flagged by the authors themselves as low-confidence.
