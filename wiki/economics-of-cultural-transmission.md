# The Economics of Cultural Transmission

Bisin & Verdier's survey formalises the mechanism this job otherwise only illustrates: **why does cultural diversity persist rather than converge to one dominant trait, and when does top-down transmission (public schooling) succeed at homogenising a population versus fail?** Parents choose costly effort $d^i$ to transmit their own trait directly (vertical transmission); failing that, children acquire a trait obliquely, from a random society-wide role model, in proportion to that trait's population share. The paper's central result, **cultural substitution**: when vertical and oblique transmission are substitutes — a minority parent invests *more* in direct transmission precisely because oblique/social transmission is unlikely to reinforce a minority trait — the population converges to a **stable, interior, heterogeneous equilibrium**, not homogeneity.

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** for the core apparatus (a survey chapter synthesising Bisin & Verdier's own 1998–2005 papers), but genuinely mixed: Section 3 surveys a substantial empirical literature testing the theory directly, including the authors' own structural estimation on GSS religious-affiliation data (1972–96, four US groups) and Cohen-Zada's (2006) county-level Catholic-school-enrolment data, which traces an inverted-U against Catholic population share — direct evidence of minority groups investing disproportionately in separate schooling as a substitution mechanism.

## Substitution versus complementarity — the condition that decides the outcome

**[model]** Under **complementarity** (the "it takes a village" case: a child adopts a trait only if parent and a random role model agree), the population instead converges to **homogeneity**, with a tipping threshold. Whether real transmission technologies are substitutes or complements is empirical and case-specific, not fixed — the paper reviews evidence on both sides (status-seeking and market-price effects generate substitution; corruption norms and group-identity consumption goods generate complementarity) and offers no general law either way.

## What this means for public schooling as a homogenising force

**[model]** Applied directly to institutions: Gradstein & Justman's model of centralised schooling under representative democracy can produce **either** "excessive polarisation" **or** "overly rapid homogenisation," depending on the mode of political implementation. Bisin & Verdier's own redistributive-voting model finds a sharp threshold: **minorities below 50% population share have zero socialisation incentive**, so when one trait is strongly majoritarian, the politics of redistribution (including school funding) homogenises toward it — but when the initial distribution is sufficiently balanced, the long-run outcome is genuinely open, following multiple self-fulfilling equilibrium paths.

## Reconciling Cantoni's result with the general model

**[wiki synthesis]** This framework predicts [[curriculum-and-ideology]]'s Chinese curriculum reform was more likely to succeed precisely because (a) the pre-reform political-attitude distribution was likely already unbalanced toward the state-favoured view (per the majority-homogenisation result), and/or (b) the targeted attitudes are not the kind identity-invested minorities have strong reason to counter-transmit at home, unlike the well-documented case of US religious minorities' school-choice behaviour under substitution. **The model does not generically predict schooling fails to homogenise culture** — it predicts homogenisation is conditional on population balance and the transmission technology's substitution/complementarity structure, giving this wiki a parameterised account of *when* each outcome should be expected rather than a single verdict.
