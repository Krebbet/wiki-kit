# Critiques of Governance Indicators (Arndt & Oman)

Arndt & Oman's OECD Development Centre book is the field's ur-text on how country governance indicators are actually built and how they are actually used. It walks through the construction of the five most-used instruments — ICRG, Freedom House, Transparency International's CPI, the World Bank's CPIAs, and the World Bank Institute's KKZ indicators (the WGI's earlier name) — and then catalogues **eight distinct ways investors, aid donors and academics misuse them**. It also does something no other source in this batch does: it produces **original numbers**. Re-running Kaufmann & Kraay's own two-equation simultaneous-equations model with more controls and several alternative instruments, it finds a **significant positive** effect of per-capita income on Rule of Law — the opposite sign to Kaufmann & Kraay's "Growth without Governance" headline, which had income *degrading* governance. That is an internal reversal inside the affirmative camp's own toolkit, and it is logged at [[does-institutions-growth-survive-identification]]. On this wiki's central question the source is confirmatory and negative: the critics of the country-indicator literature have themselves never attempted to score an individual institution, and the authors say in their own voice that internal stakeholders need "a degree of precision well beyond that of most governance indicators currently in use" and that nothing supplies it.

**Evidence tier note.** Mixed, and the source separates the kinds cleanly. **(a) empirical**: their own IV/OLS re-estimation (153-country cross-section, Maddison GDP data, multiple instrument sets, over-identification tests reported in an appendix); a documentary audit re-deriving what KKZ's own published weights, interval construction and error-variance assumptions imply; and citation of two independent perception-vs-reality studies — DIAL's "mirror surveys" and Olken's Indonesian road-project audit. **(b) model**: none of their own; they reproduce and critique KKZ's unobserved-components model in full. **Strongest**: the re-estimated growth/governance IV model, the one place they generate original numbers. **Weakest**: the "10 major banks/companies" interview base, small-n and anecdotal, used only to characterise investor behaviour qualitatively. Funding disclosure is theirs: OECD Development Centre, funded partly by Swiss aid agencies and the Center for International Private Enterprise, a pro-market business advocacy body — disclosed in their own acknowledgements, and the conclusions consistently cut *against* investor and donor interests rather than with them. **(c) wiki synthesis** is marked inline.

## The merge decision: kept separate from Munck & Verkuilen, and why

**[wiki synthesis]** The ingest flagged this source as a merge candidate with [[measurement-validity-framework]], since both critique country indices and their taxonomies overlap. **Decision: keep them separate.** Three reasons, in order of weight.

1. **They audit different objects.** Munck & Verkuilen grade an index's **construction** — did you specify the concept, pick valid indicators, justify the aggregation rule. Arndt & Oman grade an index's **consumption** — what investors, donors, ratings agencies and academics do with a number once it exists. Six of their eight misuse modes (ordinal-as-cardinal, cross-vintage non-comparability, aggregation opacity, bright-line eligibility, circularity, sample bias) are properties of a *use*, not of a construction step, and have no home in the three-stage framework.
2. **One transfers and the other does not.** The three-stage framework is level-agnostic and runs unchanged on an institution's own scorecard; that is why it anchors the batch. Arndt & Oman's content is welded to country indices and to the specific external-user ecosystem around them — merging would blur the batch's one portable artifact with a catalogue that stops at the nation-state.
3. **The original econometrics would be buried.** The re-estimation reversing Kaufmann & Kraay is the source's hardest contribution and belongs on a page that can carry it, not folded into a methods page as an aside.

**Where the two taxonomies do overlap**, stated so the redundancy is visible rather than duplicated: Arndt & Oman's *ordinal-as-cardinal* misuse is Munck & Verkuilen's failure mode 8 (unjustified measurement-level choice) seen from the user's end; their *aggregation-into-a-single-number opacity* is failure modes 11 and 12; their *lack of transparency* is failure mode 9 (non-replicability). Their *correlated-measurement-errors* finding has no counterpart in Munck & Verkuilen at all, and is the sharper of the two treatments.

## The eight misuse modes

**[empirical]** Preserved in the source's own structure:

1. **Ordinal-as-cardinal / ranking misuse** — using a relative rank as an absolute performance measure. Transparency International itself states the CPI "cannot be used as a measure of national performance in the fight against corruption"; donors and media do so routinely.
2. **Cross-vintage non-comparability** — composite scores move year to year because source composition and methodology changed, not because reality did. Documented for CPI, Freedom House, CPIAs and KKZ alike. On KKZ's own rule of thumb (non-overlapping intervals), only ~5–8% of countries per indicator showed genuine change over 1996–2004.
3. **Aggregation-into-a-single-number opacity** — collapsing six KKZ sub-indicators into one "governance" score, which KKZ's own authors reject and which Arndt & Oman show masks divergent sub-scores (their worked pairs: China against India, Israel against Oman).
4. **Bright-line eligibility misuse** — the US Millennium Challenge Account using the Control of Corruption indicator as an in/out median cutoff, criticised by Kaufmann et al. themselves for ignoring a "non-trivial probability" of misclassification, and worse for low-income countries with fewer underlying sources.
5. **Circularity / tautology** — some sub-indicators (Regulatory Quality especially) are partly built from business respondents' perceptions of whether regulation "hurts my business" or competitiveness, so correlating regulatory quality with growth or investment is partly circular by construction. Their quoted verdict, from a World Bank country director: "close to a tautology. What is required for growth? Good governance. And what counts as good governance? That which promotes growth."
6. **Correlated measurement errors treated as independent** — KKZ's weighting assumes source errors are statistically independent. Arndt & Oman give four evidenced mechanisms by which they are not (shared upstream sources, shared third-party inputs, shared exposure to crises and trends, shared cultural context of respondents) and show that **relaxing the assumption to 0.5 correlation doubles the average standard error for Rule of Law, 0.33 → 0.66**. The published intervals are, on their analysis, too narrow.
7. **Sample bias toward business and expert perception, structurally amplified by the aggregation procedure.** The specific numbers: in KKZ's Voice & Accountability, Freedom House's "Nations in Transition" carries weight **0.39** and EIU's Country Risk Service **0.18**, while population surveys — Afrobarometer, Latinobarómetro, Gallup "Voice of the People" — each carry about **0.01**. Nobody chose this as policy; it falls out of inverse-variance weighting, which rewards sources whose errors look small. **[wiki synthesis]** That is the mechanism worth carrying: precision weighting silences the noisiest instrument, and the noisiest instrument here is the general population.
8. **Lack of transparency as a compounding problem** — inaccessible source data (one commercial source priced at $12,700), undisclosed scoring criteria — named as what *enables* all the above, because users cannot audit what they rely on.

**[empirical] Why the misuse is not academic.** Aid allocation shifted in the 1990s to reward governance scores: via Burnside & Dollar and Berthélemy & Tichit, a one-standard-deviation improvement in rule-of-law/democracy indices corresponded to **28% more aid and 50% more IDA finance**. An indicator with a stated margin of error that most of its own comparisons cannot clear is being used to move money.

## Perception against measured experience

**[empirical]** Two independent studies the source imports, and they are the concrete evidence behind the abstract critique:

- **DIAL's "mirror surveys"** compare expert predictions of household attitudes and experience against what households actually report. Experts predicted **32%** would find bribery acceptable against **5%** actual; predicted **54%** would report a corruption experience against **13%** actual.
- **Olken's Indonesian road-project audit** compares perceived corruption against engineering-measured missing expenditure. Across ethnically heterogeneous villages the two move in **opposite directions**.

**[wiki synthesis]** This is a distinct critique from Glaeser et al.'s, and merging them would lose information. Glaeser's distinction is **constraint the ruler cannot escape against constraint the ruler chooses** — a question about what the score is *of*. Arndt & Oman's is **the population's measured experience against expert or business perception of that experience** — a question about whose eyes the score is seen through. Both land on [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D90 and they are independently derived; neither implies the other.

## The re-estimation that reverses Kaufmann & Kraay

**[empirical]** Kaufmann & Kraay (2002a) estimate a two-equation simultaneous system in which the Rule of Law score causes per-capita GDP (positive, IV-estimated, using AJR's settler-mortality instrument) while GDP growth has a **negative** feedback effect on Rule of Law — the "Growth without Governance" result.

Arndt & Oman re-run the identical structure with more controls (population growth, investment rate, regional dummies, log GDP 1970) and multiple alternative instruments for GDP (infant mortality, life expectancy, 1913 per-capita GDP, cars per 1,000). They obtain a **significant positive coefficient of per-capita GDP on Rule of Law** — living standards improve governance — stated as robust to instrument choice.

**What they do and do not claim.** They do **not** claim to have settled the direction. They explicitly flag heterogeneity and non-linearity as unexplored alternative explanations for the OLS/IV divergence in both their model and Kaufmann & Kraay's, and their own stated thesis is that results in this literature are very sensitive to the econometric model used. Their instruments are, in their own words, imperfect.

**[wiki synthesis]** For [[does-institutions-growth-survive-identification]] this is a third kind of datapoint. Albouy audits the affirmative camp's *data*; Glaeser et al. attack its *measure*; this is a **sign reversal on a published headline finding produced from inside the same toolkit**, by re-running the affirmative camp's own model with a fuller control set. It does not adjudicate the main institutions→growth arrow — Kaufmann & Kraay's feedback equation is a side result, not the central AJR claim — but it is a clean instance of the conflict page's own recorded pattern, that the affirmative camp is internally split and that the estimates are not robust to specification. Logged there.

## What the critique got, and what it did not

**[wiki synthesis]** Recorded separately because this is the field's clearest instance of a measurement critique actually landing.

**Addressed.** WGI/WBI **began disclosing per-source scores in 2006**, which the source itself credits as a response to exactly this critique, and the current WGI methodology paper **reports a formal margin of error prominently** and treats doing so as the instrument's defining virtue ([[governance-indicators-and-their-construction]]). Both are on Arndt & Oman's own minimum-transparency list — full disclosure of scoring methodology, and disclosure of underlying disaggregated scores. This is the clearest self-correction by an instrument-builder anywhere in this wiki.

**Not addressed.**
- **Circularity in perception-built sub-indicators.** Regulatory Quality is still defined in the 2024 methodology as perceived ability to "permit and promote private sector development" and is still built from business perception; nothing has changed to break the loop when it is regressed on growth.
- **Bright-line eligibility misuse downstream.** WGI now reports margins of error and simultaneously discloses adoption by MCC, IMF, Moody's, Fitch and MSCI ESG. Publishing an interval does not stop a threshold rule from using the point estimate, and no producer controls that use.
- **Reweighting toward population perception.** Named by Arndt & Oman as a "should" and, on the evidence in this batch, implemented by nobody. WGI's 2024 source roster still runs 24 expert assessments to 11 household or firm surveys, and the inverse-variance mechanism that produces the ~0.01 weights is unchanged.
- **Correlated measurement error.** WGI now *tests* for it — the four-provider comparison in the 2024 update — and concedes the test is "suggestive, not conclusive". The aggregation still assumes independence.

**Levers proposed, and their level.** The authors' remedies are: full disclosure of methodology and disaggregated scores; experience-based survey questions ("what percentage of your time did you spend negotiating with officials") in place of generic perception scales; triangulated mirror surveys; reweighting toward population surveys; and non-ranking "core plus satellite" indicator designs. **None of these moves the unit of analysis below country, region or respondent group.** Even the most granular — experience-based, triangulated household surveys — still measures population perception of a *country's* governance. **[wiki synthesis] The fixed version of this literature, by its own critics' design, remains inadmissible for scoring one institution.** That is a stronger statement than "nobody has done it yet".

## The gaming warning, now with a mechanism

**[wiki synthesis]** Two halves of this batch fit together into a usable diagnostic. WGI's authors state that they exclude objective and de jure data partly *because* such measures are "susceptible to 'gaming' where policy makers target reforms to narrowly change specific measures simply because they happen to be included in an aggregate indicator". Arndt & Oman supply the concrete mechanism by which the incentive to game becomes real: the Millennium Challenge Account's median cutoff on Control of Corruption, and the 28%-more-aid gradient. **A published index plus a bright-line threshold plus money on the other side of it is a Goodhart machine**, and both halves of this batch name it independently.

That is what makes it operationalisable on a single institution rather than only on a country, and it is registered as **D121**: find whether an institution's funding, eligibility or classification is gated by a threshold on an external published index, then test its own reported metrics for a discontinuity at that threshold. The regulatory-capture and goal-displacement literature this wiki already holds ([[goal-displacement-and-bureaucratic-ritualism]], [[regulatory-accretion]]) supplies the behavioural expectation; this supplies the observable.
