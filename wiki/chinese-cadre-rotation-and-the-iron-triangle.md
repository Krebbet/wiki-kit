# Chinese Cadre Rotation and the Iron Triangle

Brødsgaard & Beck trace 359 participants of the China Executive Leadership Program (2005–2019, run jointly with Cambridge/Copenhagen Business School) and find that Chinese elite governance runs on an institutionalised **"iron triangle"** of rotation between Party, government, and state-owned enterprise (SOE) leadership — a single appointing authority, the Central Organisation Department, spanning all three sectors via the **nomenklatura system**, collapsing the public/private boundary most Anglo-American institutional frameworks treat as a starting axiom. 84 of 261 SOE-leader participants (32%) were promoted after the programme; 20 of those 84 (24%) moved directly from SOE leadership into Party or government posts.

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]**, but the CELP sample is explicitly elite and hand-picked (the Central Organisation Department selects attendees) — treat as describing the top layer of SOE-to-politics movers, not the general cadre population.

## One appointing authority, three sectors

**[empirical]** The 46 centrally-managed "nomenklatura companies" have their top leadership (chairman/president/GM) directly managed by the Central Organisation Department rather than the state-asset regulator SASAC — the same body that approves appointments, transfers and dismissals across the Party and government hierarchies. **[wiki synthesis]** This is a stronger claim than "revolving door": it is a single formal appointing authority holding approval power across sectors most Western frameworks model as institutionally separate (an independent civil service, an arm's-length corporate board). A wiki axis premised on public/private personnel systems as distinct needs an explicit exception flag for this case, not a footnote.

## Rank and status, not pay, as the reward

**[empirical]** SOE executive-to-worker pay ratios reach 100x+ in some surveys; the salary tables for equivalent Party/government rank (e.g. ~38,700–52,000 RMB/year at vice-ministerial level, 2006) are far lower. Political promotion is therefore typically a **pay cut** compensated by rank, prestige, and career horizon — and a 2014 Politburo pay cap on SOE executives (600,000 RMB/year) was accepted without visible protest, consistent with non-monetary rewards being what is actually at stake. **[wiki synthesis]** This is direct friction against any wiki axis (e.g. incentive-intensity framings drawn from Western agency theory) that treats monetary pay-for-performance as the default high-powered incentive — here the highest-status move in the system is a monetary downgrade.

## A credentialing/vetting channel, not a scored tournament

**[wiki synthesis]** The paper's own contribution — that management skill and foreign/business-training exposure (demonstrated via CELP attendance and subsequent performance) function as an underweighted promotion channel alongside the literature's usual two factors (economic performance per Li & Zhou 2005; political connections per Shih, Adolph & Liu 2012) — describes a mechanism closer to a **credentialing pipeline** than [[regionally-decentralized-authoritarianism]]'s GDP-tournament. The two sources describe different rungs of the same apparatus: Xu's tournament operates on provincial leaders scored against a legible metric; this source's elite rotation operates on enterprise leaders vetted through training and track record, with no single scored metric named.

## Term-limited rotation as a formally codified anti-entrenchment device

**[model]** Leading cadres rotate after two terms (~10 years), transferred upward or sideways without leadership responsibility, under age-gated informal norms (e.g. "68 down, 67 up" for Politburo re-election). **[wiki synthesis]** This is more rule-bound than typical Western executive tenure norms (usually informal or contractual) but serves a function comparable to term limits — worth reading alongside D129 ([[selectorate-theory-and-the-winning-coalition]]) as a distinct institutional route to the same anti-entrenchment goal.
