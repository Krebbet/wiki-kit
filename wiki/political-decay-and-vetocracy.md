# Political Decay and Vetocracy

Fukuyama's account of American institutional dysfunction runs on two general mechanisms — institutions retain mental models past the point of empirical validity, and institutions create favoured constituencies that mobilise to block reforms threatening them — and three US-specific ones: judicialisation of administration, legalised interest-group influence via "reciprocal altruism", and a high veto-point count interacting with post-1980s polarisation to produce **vetocracy**, government structurally unable to act. The whole runs as a self-reinforcing loop: gridlock produces incoherent and expensive law, which reduces trust, which reduces willingness to delegate or fund, which worsens performance. The essay's central worked case is the US Forest Service, 1905–2014, where missions were layered on and never discarded, each acquiring its own defending interest group. It is an essay, not a study — narrative, secondary citation and illustrative statistics, with no model, test or controlled comparison. Its most important property for this wiki is that **Fukuyama's own evidence cuts against a smooth age-decay reading**: he pins the dysfunction of a 230-year-old veto structure on a contingent shift dated to the 1980s, and records earlier cycles of the same structure failing and being reset by external shock.

**Evidence tier note.** (b) **Essay/book-chapter argument.** No formal model, no regression, no controlled comparison. Three kinds of material:

1. **Historical narrative** — the US Forest Service (1905–2014); *Brown v. Board* and the Title VII litigation history; Oakland Harbor dredging delay set against the Port of Rotterdam; Dodd-Frank contrasted with a hypothetical Glass-Steagall-style bank-size cap.
2. **Secondary citation treated as evidentiary support** — Huntington, Olson, Schattschneider, Fiorina, Melnick, Farhang, Kagan, Lessig, Putnam. Fukuyama synthesises these; he independently tests none of them.
3. **Scattered statistics, used illustratively rather than analytically** — lobbying firms: 175 in 1971 → 2,500 in 1981 → 13,700 by 2009, at $3.5bn in spending. Title VII private-enforcement suits: under 100/yr in the late 1960s → 10,000/yr in the 1980s → 22,000/yr in the late 1990s. National-forest timber harvest: ~11bn → ~3bn board feet/yr by the 1990s. 45% of new federal hires are veterans.

**Strongest:** the documented Forest Service institutional history and the litigation- and lobbying-volume figures — checkable facts, and the litigation series is a genuine measured trend. **Weakest:** the overarching causal claim that vetocracy, judicialisation and interest-group capture *together* explain political decay. It is argued by narrative accumulation and never tested against alternative explanations — simple divided government, or growth in the scope and complexity of what government is asked to do, independent of institutional design. Nothing in the essay quantifies the relative contribution of the four candidate causes it names.

## The general theory

**[model]** "Political decay" is borrowed from Huntington and claimed to apply to *any* political institution, authoritarian or democratic. Two mechanisms:

- **Cognitive.** Institutions are built on a model of their environment; the environment moves and the model does not. The Forest Service's fire-suppression doctrine is the worked example — a doctrine that outlived the fire ecology it rested on, and which the agency continued to apply after the science had turned against it.
- **Group-interest.** Institutions create favoured classes and constituencies, which then mobilise to block reforms that threaten their position. Elite insiders hold superior access to power and information, so they defend gains more effectively than diffuse outsiders can attack them.

**[wiki synthesis]** Note what the first mechanism is *about*: the gap between an institution's model and its environment. Elapsed time widens that gap only if the environment moves; it is drift, not duration, that does the causal work. This distinction is what the section below turns on.

## The American chain

**[model]** Three US-specific mechanisms, stated as a chain of assertions connected by narrative logic rather than as a tested causal model:

**Judicialisation of administration** — "adversarial legalism". Congress creates agencies but withholds clean rule-making authority from them, so enforcement migrates to the courts: private litigants with broad standing become the instrument of policy implementation. The Title VII series is the illustration. Parliamentary systems, on Fukuyama's contrast, delegate rule-making and enforcement cleanly to agencies and do not generate the same litigation channel.

**Legalised interest-group influence** — "reciprocal altruism". The mechanism is explicitly *not* transactional bribery, which is illegal and rare. It is gift-exchange: campaign donations create diffuse obligation rather than purchased votes, which is both legal and, Fukuyama argues, more pervasive and harder to attack. The lobbying-firm counts are the illustration. He extends this to **"repatrimonialization"** — the capture of the state by patronage-adjacent reciprocity, likened to Later Han China, the Mamluks and the French ancien régime; a term the wiki does not otherwise carry, whose nearest neighbour here is interest-group capture.

**Vetocracy** — a veto-point count higher than in other contemporary democracies, "raising the costs of collective action and in some cases mak[ing] it impossible altogether", interacting with post-1980s partisan polarisation.

**The loop.** These produce gridlock, incoherent and expensive law, and declining trust — and declining trust itself reduces willingness to delegate authority or fund government, which degrades performance further. Fukuyama states the self-reinforcement explicitly; it is the essay's structural claim, and it is asserted rather than measured.

## The Forest Service case

**[empirical — institutional history, as narrated]** The essay's one sustained case, and reusable independently of the general theory.

- **Mission layers, added and never discarded.** Timber production → fire suppression → conservation. Each layer arrived with its own external constituency, and each constituency then defended its layer against removal. The agency ends with three mandates it cannot jointly optimise and no mechanism for retiring any of them.
- **The trajectory is driven by discrete shocks, not by smooth ageing.** The Great Idaho Fire of 1910 shifted the mission toward firefighting. The 1988 Yellowstone fires triggered the "let burn" policy reversal. Postwar Western population growth created a new home-owner interest group lobbying for continued suppression. The rise of 1960s–70s environmental consciousness added the conservation mission and its constituency. Every turning point is an event, not an anniversary.
- **Autonomy given and taken back.** Pinchot-era Forest Service autonomy was later reversed as Congress "reinserted itself" into micromanagement via legislative mandates — principals reclaiming control over a delegated agent, not an agent drifting free of one.
- **Funding distortion.** Timber sold below operating cost, with the agency having "an incentive to increase its costs rather than contain them". **Flag this as what it is:** a bare, uncited assertion of the Niskanen budget-maximisation mechanism, offered in passing with no citation and no test. It adds an illustrative case to the open conflict in [[bureaucratic-growth-and-parkinsons-law]] and no evidence to it.
- **Selection by statute.** 45% of new federal hires are veterans — selection criteria changed by legislative fiat over time, a channel by which external actors reshape an agency's personnel without touching its mandate.

## Why his own evidence undercuts a smooth age-decay reading

**[wiki synthesis]** This is the most important content on the page for the wiki's purposes, and it should not be read as a debunking. Fukuyama's decay thesis is serious, the mechanisms are coherent, and the Forest Service case is real. What his own account does *not* support is the specific proposition that an institution's dysfunction is a function of its age.

1. **The stated mechanism is drift, not duration.** "Stability is the source of decay: institutions are built for circumstances that then change." The causal variable is environmental change outpacing institutional adaptation. Age is a *permissive* condition — more elapsed time means more opportunity for drift and for constituency accretion — but it is not the mechanism, and the essay never claims it is.

2. **He decouples institutional age from onset of dysfunction explicitly.** The Madisonian veto structure is roughly 230 years old and unchanged in count. Fukuyama says it worked adequately for long stretches: *"In earlier periods of U.S. history, when one party or another was dominant, this system served to moderate the will of the majority... But in the more evenly balanced, highly competitive party system that has arisen **since the 1980s**, it has become a formula for gridlock."* The dysfunction is attributed to a recent, contingent, exogenous political-sociological shift — polarisation, primary-driven candidate selection, gerrymandering — interacting with a structural feature far older than the dysfunction it now produces. A 230-year-old structure that failed starting in the 1980s is not an age effect.

3. **He records earlier cycles of the same structure failing.** The mid-19th-century slavery-extension crisis and the late-19th-century agrarian/industrial crisis are named as prior instances of the *same* constitutional structure producing dysfunction — resolved by external shock (the Civil War) rather than by continuous decay or continuous renewal. That is a **punctuated, cyclical pattern, not a monotonic age-decay curve**, and it is Fukuyama's own historical framing rather than an outside objection to it. See [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]].

4. **The one genuine duration mechanism in the essay is second-hand and untested.** It is Olson's, imported wholesale and not independently verified: in times of peace and stability democracies accumulate ever more interest groups, whose rent-seeking crowds out productive activity, halted only by a large shock such as war or revolution. That is a claim about the **duration of political stability at the level of a polity**, not about the age of any institution. [[institutional-sclerosis]] carries the review evidence on exactly this claim, including how little of the literature tests the accumulation step rather than the stability-to-growth correlation.

**[wiki synthesis]** The net position: this source supports "accretion of un-discarded mandates and their attendant interest groups, absent a reset shock, tends to increase with elapsed time" as a citable narrative claim about one agency and one polity. It does not support "institutions decay as a function of their age", and its own examples — multi-decade functional stretches, recurrent crises, discrete triggering events — argue against a smooth age-decay function.

## Power

**[model]** Formal decision rights sit nominally with Congress (legislation, budget), the executive and its agencies (implementation), and the courts (adjudication, with expanded standing). Actual influence, on Fukuyama's account, sits disproportionately with organised interest groups able to exploit legal gift-exchange and litigation access — a nominal-versus-actual accountability gap stated in terms. The mechanism by which economic winners entrench is worth quoting for the wiki's power/production axis: they convert wealth into political influence not merely by transactional payment but **by changing the institutional rules to favour themselves — "for example, by closing off competition in markets they already dominate"**. Rents are entrenched, not just extracted once.

**[empirical — descriptive]** One observation cuts against the intuitive reading of accountability: formal democratic exposure and public trust run *inversely* in his examples. The House of Representatives — the most democratically accountable federal institution — has the lowest public approval; the military and NASA — the least democratically exposed — have the highest. He offers this as evidence that formal accountability structure and actual legitimacy can diverge sharply, not as a case against democratic accountability.

## Veto points: the contested reading

**[wiki synthesis]** Fukuyama's veto-point claim is filed as a contested case on [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]], and the tension is genuinely two-sided rather than a straightforward contradiction. His **headline rhetoric reads count-only** — the US has far more veto points than comparable democracies, therefore higher costs of collective action — which is precisely the inference Hammond's formal model says is unavailable, since count alone predicts nothing once preference profiles are allowed to vary. But his **own historical argument gates the effect on polarisation**, which is structurally much closer to Hammond's interaction term: veto-point count × preference divergence, with polarisation as a proxy for divergence. Both readings are in the text. Record both; do not adjudicate.

## Levers — all asserted, none evidenced

**[model]** No lever below is tested, and Fukuyama does not claim otherwise.

- **Cleaner delegation of rule-making and enforcement authority to agencies**, Westminster-style, reducing reliance on courts. Offered via comparative illustration (UK, Germany, Netherlands, Scandinavia), not causally evidenced.
- **Consolidating veto points.** Implied by the US/Westminster contrast; he diagnoses the difference without prescribing that the US adopt a parliamentary system.
- **A cross-partisan reform coalition** of groups without a stake in the current system, on the model of the 1880s Pendleton Act coalition. He states that the collective-action problem facing such a coalition is severe and that no current leadership or agenda exists.
- **Rolling back "democratising" reforms** — open primaries, expanded standing and citizen access to courts, transparency and media exposure — on the ground that they empowered organised activists rather than the median citizen. Stated explicitly as the logical but politically unspeakable implication of his own diagnosis: "no one dares suggest..."
- **External shock as the historical unlock.** Implicit rather than designed: WWII debt levels exceeded today's and were resolved; the 1990s Scandinavian welfare-state crises were resolved by policy adjustment. The essay closes by predicting decay "will probably continue until some external shock comes along."

## Ideological priors

**[wiki synthesis]** Fukuyama writes as a state-capacity-oriented, classical-liberal institutionalist, and positions himself explicitly against both camps he expects to read him: he rejects the conservative/libertarian prescription (deregulate, abolish agencies) and the progressive one (more litigation, more judicial policy-making, more participation), arguing instead for a **stronger, more autonomous, more Weberian executive bureaucracy** on European parliamentary lines. His causal case leans heavily on citation of others' work rather than original analysis, and the conclusion — systemic, self-reinforcing decay requiring an external shock to reverse — plausibly outruns what the cited evidence individually establishes, since nothing here quantifies the relative contribution of vetocracy, judicialisation, interest-group capture and polarisation to observed dysfunction.

**Scope.** Single country (United States), federal level, heavily weighted to executive agencies, Congress and the courts, primarily late 20th century through 2014; the Forest Service case runs 1905–2014. Comparative references to the UK, Germany, the Netherlands, Scandinavia, the EU and Latin America are illustrative contrasts with no comparable depth of case evidence for any comparator. Treat any generalisation beyond US federal political institutions as unsupported by this source. On private institutions the essay is **silent**, not negative: its only gesture is a passing claim that kin selection and reciprocal altruism are natural modes of human sociability against which modern *states* build countervailing rules. Do not read it as a public/private-invariant mechanism.
