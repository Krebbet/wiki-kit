# The Embedded State and Unpaid Public Service (England, 1835)

Heldring, Kedrosky, Robinson and Weigand (NBER WP 35130, April 2026) use the 1835 Municipal Corporations Report on 258 English boroughs to argue that unpaid local-government positions outperformed paid ones, that the advantage came mainly from positive selection of prestige-seeking local elites rather than from on-the-job incentives, and that this "Embedded State" bought its low fiscal cost with corruption, patronage and an inability to staff onerous tasks. It is the wiki's first source on a non-salaried workforce as a governance form, and a historical test of whether a "Weberian" salaried bureaucracy is the only route to public-goods provision.

**Evidence tier note.** Status: **un-refereed NBER working paper**; treat as provisional. Tier (a) for the unpaid/paid performance gap as measured, with serious measurement caveats listed below; the selection decomposition is (a) but rests on fixed-effect and subsample exercises, not an exogenous shock to who serves; the state-level "Embedded vs. Weberian" typology is closer to (b), a conceptual scheme applied to a few illustrative boroughs. Capture-frontmatter title ("Selection Index (1) (2) (3)") is a PDF-extraction artefact from a table header. The summarising pass read the main text and appendix headings, not the appendix tables in full.

## Terminology: not Wade/Evans "embedded autonomy"

**[wiki synthesis]** Two unrelated constructs share the word. In [[developmental-state-and-embedded-autonomy]], embedded autonomy (Evans, Wade) means career bureaucrats who are insulated by Weberian rules yet tied to industrial elites by networks. Here "Embedded State" (a Polanyi-derived usage) means a state that extends into social structure so that local elites staff it unpaid, out of status concerns. The first is a property of a salaried, insulated bureaucracy; the second is the absence of one. Do not cite either as support for the other.

## The design and the headline result

**[empirical]** Unit: the position-by-task within a borough, with borough, task and (in some columns) position-title fixed effects; a team-production model at the borough-task team level; borough-level aggregates. Performance is the royal commissioners' own contemporaneous judgement, binarised at the median into seven indicators of very different kinds (courts held, accounts kept, documents supplied, paving and lighting, ordinal prison and police grades). Unpaid status raises performance by 0.09 (s.e. 0.02) with borough and task fixed effects and 0.12 (s.e. 0.02) with title fixed effects, against a mean of 0.61 (about a fifth of the mean). At team level, switching a median-size team (median size 2) from paid to unpaid is worth 34 percentage points, with diminishing returns to team size.

**[empirical]** Scale of the form: at least 148,000 local posts in 1835, nearly 95% unpaid, against about 21,000 paid central officials. England governed extensively at roughly 10% of income in tax; the local tier was about seven times the centre in headcount at almost no fiscal cost.

**[empirical]** A "predetermined affordability" design uses borough rental income from inherited assets (balanced on observables) to say which positions a borough could afford to pay; an "out of budget" indicator gives about a 15% performance rise, similar to baseline. A charter-mandated-position subset is also used.

## Selection, not incentives

**[empirical]** The authors' own decomposition: unpaid posts are prestigious, stepping-stone, flexible and less supervised, and attract the local elite (wealthier middle class, gentry, clergy). AKM-style fixed effects show unpaid officers are higher-ability. Removing the selected individuals makes paid and unpaid positions "perform equally well" (Appendix D); adding them back reproduces the headline gap. **[wiki synthesis]** The headline "unpaid is more productive" is therefore a claim about who sits in the post, with the absence of a salary acting as the sorting device. The paper does not separate prestige from career-advancement as causes of the sorting.

## What the form costs

**[empirical]** Unpaid officers are associated with more patronage and nepotism (family appointments), financial corruption and electoral corruption (bribery, partisan control, "cooping"); effect sizes are 50% to about 100% of the sample mean. The qualification that matters: corruption is not the precondition for the performance advantage. The effect is positive and significant in both corrupt and non-corrupt subsamples, but larger in the corrupt one, so corruption is partial compensation for unpaid work, not its engine.

**[empirical]** Task-feasibility gradient: governing and judicial work (prestigious) is unpaid; policing, gaol-keeping and clerical work (low prestige, tightly constrained) is more often paid. Fourteen gentlemen in succession refused nomination as Common Councillors in Bristol in 1810. **[wiki synthesis]** The form works for tasks that can be made prestigious and fails on onerous ones, which fits [[multitask-incentive-theory]]'s logic of low-powered incentives where tasks are hard to measure and cash is reserved for tasks that cannot be made status goods.

**[empirical]** Governance structure: a self-recruiting corporation appoints its own members, writes the position list and appoints the officers, with members also sitting as judges (no separation of powers). Accountability is internal and oligarchic: peer status, patrons, and the county and parliamentary ladder, with the centre holding no records until the 1835 inquiry. This is the [[iron-law-of-oligarchy]] and [[cooptation]] case, producing performance and corruption together.

## State-capacity measurement tension with [[endogenous-state-capacity]]

**[wiki synthesis]** Scope note, not a ruling. [[endogenous-state-capacity]] (Besley and Persson) operationalises capacity as fiscal and legal capacity that a ruling group invests in. This paper takes that identification of capacity with tax revenue as its target and argues that capacity in the sense of public-goods provision can be high without fiscal capacity. The scopes differ (a pre-modern local tier in 1835, versus national fiscal and legal capacity in a formal model), so the two need not contradict; the open question is whether the measurement premise holds outside the fiscally constrained local setting. No conflict file is opened; the tension is recorded here and on that page.

## Tension notes

- **[[weberian-structure-and-growth]].** The Weberian page reports salaried, meritocratic organisation correlating with growth (Evans and Rauch) at national, modern level. This paper finds an unpaid, elite-staffed form outperforming the paid form within a low-revenue, pre-modern local sample. **[wiki synthesis]** Likely a scope difference (the paper's own Section 1 hints the Embedded form gives way to Weberian structures as revenue rises, but tests no transition). Liverpool (203 officers, 86% paid) and Plymouth (15% paid) both succeed, so the paper does not claim the Embedded form wins at every scale.
- **[[motivation-crowding]].** Unpaid posts outperform, but the paper locates the effect in selection, not in crowding-in of intrinsic motivation: holding incentive structure fixed and removing the selected individuals eliminates the gap. Weak tension, since crowding-out is not tested. It supports the wiki's separation of selection from incentive channels, as in [[personnel-economics-of-the-state]].

## Caveats

**[empirical]** (i) Performance is the commissioners' own judgement and several indicators partly measure record-keeping or cooperation with the inquiry; (ii) the commissioners were Benthamite-leaning reformers, so the yardstick may embed a reform view of good government; (iii) incentive and selection variables are coded from the same report (prestige by list order), with officer identity linked indirectly via a 1790 gazetteer, MP biographies and a wealth compilation; (iv) the affordability instrument is a construct (market wage proxied by the mean paid wage for the same task profile), not an external shock; (v) corruption is measured by mention in a report whose authors were hostile to the corporations; (vi) a single 1835 cross-section, so no dynamics and no claim about institutional age; (vii) the unpaid-versus-paid gap is larger in corrupt officers' subsample. Scope: England and Wales, municipal boroughs only (London omitted); 258 boroughs the commissioners could survey; pre-reform franchise; mostly time-intensive rather than capital-intensive tasks. The authors expect the status sociology to be England-specific (Dutch regents were motivated by downstream financial benefit; Rwanda is community-driven) and flag their Rwanda and South Korea remarks as speculation. The title outruns a cross-section on a contemporaneous inspection score.

**[wiki synthesis]** Public sector only. The structure (prestige and career-ladder pay in place of cash, positive self-selection, less oversight) resembles unpaid board service or professional-society office, but the paper makes no such extension. Not a recommendation to run unpaid public service.

**Ideological priors.** Mainstream political-economy economists (Robinson of Acemoglu and Robinson; Heldring); institutional-historical frame, sympathetic to non-Weberian state capacity and critical of identifying capacity with tax revenue. Conclusions mostly stay within the evidence.
