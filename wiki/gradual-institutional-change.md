# Gradual Institutional Change

**One of a paired, rival-models set.** Read with [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]], which states the opposite position, and with [[rival-models-of-institutional-change]], the open conflict between them. The two pages are written to be read against each other; neither is the wiki's adopted position.

Thelen's claim is that political-economic institutions in advanced economies change mainly through **gradual, endogenous** mechanisms — layering, drift, conversion, with displacement as the rare limiting case — rather than through long stasis broken by exogenous shock. The mechanism is that institutions are sets of rules which must always be *interpreted, enforced and enacted* by actors with divergent interests, so there is a permanent gap between institutional design and on-the-ground effect, and that gap is continuously exploited. Which gradual mechanism you get is predicted by two variables: **veto possibilities** available to defenders of the status quo, and **discretion** afforded in the interpretation and enforcement of the targeted rule. The claim that matters most for this wiki is a scoping one: contestation over a rule "often begin[s] as soon as a rule is laid down", so on this account there is no ossification curve to find, because the process that would produce one is running at full strength from year zero.

**Evidence tier.** (b) framework, illustrated by qualitative case narrative. A century-long single-case institutional history (German vocational education and training, 1897 onward, sketched here from Thelen's own 2004 book), three parallel qualitative case narratives inside one country (German collective bargaining, VET, labour-market and welfare policy, 1990s–2000s) built from secondary literature and court-case tracing, and one cross-national quantitative claim (18 OECD countries on a two-dimensional coordination/solidarism space, Swank, Martin & Thelen 2008) that is **referenced but not shown** — the reader cannot inspect it, and it is the weakest item. Nothing here is (a) empirical in this wiki's sense. Note the attribution: the captured source is Thelen's 2009 BJIR annual lecture, presenting a typology developed with Streeck (2005) and Mahoney (2010).

## Why gaps between design and effect are permanent

**[framework — the mechanism, and it is a real one]** Institutions are "sets of rules that are enforced or complied with by actors" who have divergent and conflicting interests, so the rule must always be interpreted, enforced and enacted by somebody with a stake in what it means. Four named sources of the resulting gaps:

1. **Cognitive limits of designers** — institutions get used for purposes their designers never anticipated.
2. **Institution-building as political compromise** — rules are ambiguous *by design from the outset*, because they encode a coalition's conflicting interests. Ambiguity is not decay; it is present at founding.
3. **Institutions are non-neutral and therefore contested** — "because they instantiate power, they are contested". The losers of the original design fight do not disappear and can later "occupy and redeploy" institutions not built for them.
4. **Time** — designers eventually die or lose power while the rule persists, and the context around a fixed rule changes, opening interpretive space that was unavailable at founding.

The fourth is the only one that runs on elapsed time, and note its direction: **time makes rules more available for capture and reinterpretation, not less.** That is the opposite sign from the lock-in account on [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]].

Thelen credits the gap concept to Pierson (2004) and then redeploys it against Pierson's own lock-in reading.

## Two typologies, kept separate

**[model]** They operate at different levels and must not be flattened into one scheme.

### Table 1 — process × result (Streeck & Thelen 2005)

| | Continuity | Discontinuity |
|---|---|---|
| **Incremental** | **Reproduction by adaptation** — institutions absorb new pressure without changing their basic logic (the Varieties-of-Capitalism school's preferred quadrant) | **Gradual transformation** — cumulative small changes with a transformative net result. *The neglected quadrant this research programme exists to fill.* |
| **Abrupt** | **Survival and return** — the institution survives a shock largely unchanged (German VET through two world wars and multiple regime changes) | **Breakdown and replacement** — the punctuated-equilibrium literature's default and, she argues, near-exclusive focus (collapse of Soviet and Eastern European political institutions) |

**[wiki synthesis]** The "survival and return" cell is the direct empirical challenge to [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]]: shocks large enough to satisfy anyone's definition of crisis, absorbed without discontinuity. Krasner's framework has no cell for it.

### Table 2 — which gradual mechanism (Mahoney & Thelen 2010)

Sits entirely inside Table 1's *incremental* row.

| | Low discretion in interpretation/enforcement | High discretion |
|---|---|---|
| **Stronger veto possibilities** | **Layering** (pensions, social insurance) | **Drift** (collective bargaining, vocational training) |
| **Weaker veto possibilities** | **Displacement** (relatively rare) | **Conversion** (the *Günstigkeitsprinzip* litigation) |

## The four mechanisms

**Layering** — "the original institutions are left in place, but new elements are added alongside... that have transformative potential in the longer run" (credited to Schickler). Strong veto players block outright repeal, so change is accomplished by **addition rather than revision**. German case: traditional employment protections for core workers left untouched while a new, encapsulated layer of low-wage, mini-job and atypical-employment rules was added alongside in the Hartz reforms; Hartz IV created a parallel, separately financed means-tested social-assistance system running on a completely different logic (poverty alleviation) beside the traditional contribution-based social insurance (status and income maintenance).

**Drift** — formal rules unchanged, real-world coverage and impact erode, because surrounding conditions change and actors exploit the enforcement discretion already built into the design (the term and citation are Hacker's "policy drift" on US welfare retrenchment). Veto blocking of *formal* change is irrelevant, because nothing needs to change formally. German case: collective bargaining's multi-industry structure fully intact and in some respects strengthened by union mergers, while the **share of workers actually covered** shrank steadily as individual — especially small — firms opted out and atypical employment expanded in services.

**Conversion** — existing rules redeployed or reinterpreted toward new ends without formal amendment, typically by **shifting the arena of contestation to a venue with fewer veto players**. German case: the *Günstigkeitsprinzip* (favourability principle) — employers, having lost in the legislature, sought a court reinterpretation of an existing bargaining-law clause to permit local wage concessions traded against job security, which would have converted a centralisation-reinforcing rule into a decentralisation vehicle with no statute rewritten. At the time of writing the unions had prevailed in the courts, so this is an *attempted*, not completed, conversion.

**Displacement** — outright replacement of old rules by new, arrived at through accumulating defection and delegitimation rather than a single shock. Stated to be "relatively rare" in advanced capitalist political economies, and — importantly — **given no worked German example.** Three of the four cells get concrete illustrations; this one gets none. That is an evidentiary gap in the typology's own core matrix, admitted by the source.

## Levers, for an actor who cannot win a frontal fight

**[framework — descriptive, not tested]** The three illustrated mechanisms read directly as strategies:

1. Exploit existing administrative or enforcement discretion to erode an institution's real coverage without touching its formal rules (**drift**).
2. Add a new rule or programme alongside the old one rather than repealing it, when veto players protect the core (**layering**).
3. Shift the venue of contestation to a forum with fewer veto players — courts instead of the legislature — and seek reinterpretation of an ambiguous existing rule (**conversion**).

All three are *observed* in the German case, not evaluated for effectiveness against alternatives. Illustrative case narrative, not causal-inference-grade.

**[wiki synthesis]** This is the most directly usable material in the batch for the wiki's playbook outcome, and also the most dangerous, because it is entirely uncontrolled. What it does establish is that the frontal-repeal frame is wrong: an actor with weak formal position has three routes that never require winning a repeal vote. It says nothing about how often they succeed.

## What the original design does not determine

**[framework — the single most important claim on this page]** The German VET system was legislated in **1897** by an authoritarian government together with reactionary artisan guilds, *specifically to weaken organised labour*. By the late twentieth century it was the institutional core of union–management "social partnership". Formal continuity across a century; complete reversal of whose interests it serves.

Thelen's general statement: feedback effects "are not associated with the faithful reproduction of the system as originally constituted"; institutions "created for one set of purposes and resting on the shoulders of one set of actors could be carried forward on the shoulders of another coalition altogether". Who effectively controls an institution is the **shifting coalition that currently carries it forward**, which is distinct from who designed it and from who nominally administers it.

**[wiki synthesis]** Two consequences the wiki should carry forward:

- **Survival is not evidence of continuity of function.** Any dataset that measures whether institutions persist — Skocpol's voluntary associations, on [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]] — measures the wrong thing for the age hypothesis, on this account. Formal persistence and functional persistence are separately measurable and routinely decouple. Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D55.
- **The age-gating premise is denied directly, not merely unevidenced.** "Conflicts over these issues in fact often begin as soon as a rule is laid down." If contestation is continuous from founding, an age-gated ossification model has no phase structure to describe. This is the strongest theoretical argument in the wiki against the premise the wiki's own framing assumed. See [[open-questions]] Q7 and Q39.

## Failure modes named

**Drift** (above) — rules formally unchanged, real impact erodes. **Dualisation / segmentalism** — the narrowing of an institution's coalitional base into a protected core and an excluded periphery; Thelen's empirical finding for Germany is dualisation rather than liberalisation. **Self-exhaustion** (Streeck 2009; Streeck & Hassel 2004) is mentioned once as the convergence camp's term for coordinated-market institutions decaying under their own weight, and is deliberately recorded here as **not** part of the Mahoney–Thelen 2×2 — it risks being conflated with drift and is a different claim.

None of these is framed as a pathology of scale or of age. They are framed as normal, continuous political processes.

## Falsifiability

**[wiki synthesis, plus the source's own admissions]** Assessed on the same terms as [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]], because the comparison is the point.

- **Norm or exception?** Stated as the *dominant* mode, not merely a valid alternative: "it is not at all clear that this model [punctuated equilibrium] captures the most common way institutions change over time." Abrupt exogenous change is not denied — the Soviet and Eastern European collapses are cited as real instances, and New Zealand's 1980s reform is footnoted as an exception — but framed as the less common pattern for this domain.
- **Ex ante distinguishing condition: weak or absent.** The typology predicts *which gradual mechanism* occurs, given veto possibilities and discretion. It supplies **no** condition predicting in advance whether a given shock will produce discontinuity or be absorbed as German VET absorbed two world wars. "If punctuated equilibrium works anywhere it should work in Germany, and it doesn't" is a retrospective case observation, not a derived rule.
- **The 2×2 is exhaustive by construction.** It is a full cross-tabulation of two roughly binary dimensions, so almost any observed instance of gradual change can be redescribed post hoc as one of the four cells once the two axes are somehow scored. There is a specific circularity risk: **discretion is sometimes inferred from the fact that drift occurred**, and the source shows no independent ex ante measurement procedure for either axis.
- **One cell is untested by the source's own evidence** — displacement, with zero worked example.

**Net: better than Krasner on one dimension and no better on the other.** Thelen supplies a stated, in-principle-measurable predictor for the *form* of change (veto possibilities × discretion) where Krasner supplies no predictor at all. Neither supplies an ex ante criterion for when a shock produces discontinuity. Both frameworks classify well after the fact.

## Scope

Advanced, developed, democratic political economies, overwhelmingly **Germany**, with comparative gestures to Sweden, Japan, Denmark, France, Britain and the US, and one explicit counter-case (New Zealand's 1980s radical reform, where abrupt change did dominate). Mostly 1990s–2000s, plus the VET case reaching back to 1897. Coordinated-market-economy labour and welfare institutions; the author does not claim extension to authoritarian regimes, developing economies, or domains outside political economy.

**Unit of analysis: national institutional arenas, not single organisations.** The mechanisms are illustrated with micro-level actor behaviour — individual firms opting out of bargaining agreements, works councils cutting local deals, courts reinterpreting a clause — but the explanandum is always the macro arena. Do not read this as evidence about single-firm or single-agency behaviour.

**Public/private: silent, and the silence is honest.** The analysis covers institutions jointly constituted by state, capital and organised labour — neither purely public bureaucracies nor purely private firms. Nothing in the stated mechanism (contested, always-interpreted rules) is intrinsically public-sector-specific, so there is no positive reason to expect it would not generalise — and no test. Treat as an open extension.

**Ideological prior, recorded.** Thelen writes as a historical institutionalist positioned against three camps at once: Varieties-of-Capitalism functionalists for over-predicting stability; the punctuated-equilibrium and path-dependence school for treating change as necessarily exogenous; and both New Institutional Economics rational-choice functionalism and sociological neo-institutionalism (Meyer & Rowan by name) for collapsing the institution into the behaviour under it and leaving no room for endogenous change. A legible centre-left concern for labour solidarity colours which findings get emphasised — declining coverage, insider/outsider divides — but the four-mechanism typology is offered as analytic machinery independent of that frame.
