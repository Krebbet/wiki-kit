# Schooling and Institutional Quality

**The schooling hypothesis: human capital is upstream of institutional quality, not downstream of it.** Glaeser, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer (2004) argue that the causal arrow the growth literature assumes runs backwards — that education accumulates first (often under unconstrained rulers, as a policy choice), that political improvement follows, and that both growth and institutional quality are consequences of human capital rather than institutions being the cause of growth. Their load-bearing evidence is a single fixed-effects panel at five-year intervals in which **initial years of schooling predict subsequent improvement in political institutions on three of four measures, while no political measure predicts subsequent improvement in schooling**; their identification evidence is a re-run of AJR's own settler-mortality instrument showing it loads more strongly on schooling than on executive constraints, with predicted schooling significant in the second stage and predicted executive constraints wrong-signed and insignificant.

**This page was deliberately provisional pending research job 17 (`schooling-norms-and-institutional-formation`), which has now landed [2026-08-21] and is rewritten accordingly, not patched.** The one-paper, one-team status is gone. Two independent camps have now engaged this exact claim directly, by name, with their own designs; a third source reverses the causal arrow entirely; a fourth complicates the mechanism with causal evidence the original paper's own mechanism-selection logic didn't anticipate. **Net effect of job 17: the sequencing claim is weaker than this page previously had to treat it, and the identification evidence behind it is now directly disputed on construct-validity grounds, not just re-estimated.** Read the whole page — the verdict is not a simple reversal, and several open threads remain open on both sides.

**Evidence tier note.** The original source is **(a) empirical**: cross-country OLS and 2SLS on a 1960–2000 global panel, plus one fixed-effects sequencing panel and one illustrative paired case. No formal model of its own; [[why-democracy-needs-education]] later supplies one, from the same research programme. The job-17 rebuttals are also (a) empirical, with their own independent designs — see below.

## The claim, stated precisely

**[empirical]** The claim is a *sequencing* claim, not a levels claim, and the distinction is what makes it testable. Glaeser et al. do not dispute that measured institutional quality correlates with national income — they concede the correlation and explain it in the other direction. Their proposed order is:

1. Human capital accumulates — frequently under dictatorship, as a discretionary policy choice rather than as a product of political constraint.
2. Political stability and measured institutional improvement follow.
3. Growth follows, and is *also* driven directly by human capital.

The negative half is stated as flatly as the positive half: "at least in the OLS regressions, the evidence that institutions cause economic growth, as opposed to growth improving institutions, is non-existent." Note what they are *not* claiming. They explicitly disown "institutions don't matter" — "That proposition is flatly contradicted by a great deal of available empirical evidence, including our own" — and they disown the normative reading, citing Mulligan, Gil & Sala-i-Martin (2004) that democracies are significantly more benign on press freedom, torture, the death penalty and religious regulation, and framing democracy as valuable independently of growth. The claim is about *what causes what, in poor countries, over the post-1960 record*.

The measurement critique that underpins the negative half — that ICRG expropriation risk, KKM government effectiveness and Polity constraints-on-the-executive are outcome and perception measures rather than durable constraints — is set out at [[colonial-origins-and-the-settler-mortality-instrument]] and is not restated here. It matters to this page only as a premise: if the institutional variables are recording recent policy choice, then a levels regression cannot distinguish the two orderings, and only a timing design can.

## The sequencing test, and exactly what it shows

**[empirical]** **Table 12 is the paper's strongest evidence by its own framing, and the only design in its entire literature batch that targets direction of causality rather than correlating levels.** Country fixed effects, five-year intervals, errors clustered at country level, following Barro (1997, 1999) on the timing approach.

*Panel A — dependent variable: five-year change in years of schooling (N = 514, or 420 for the Polity-autocracy specification).* None of the four political measures predicts subsequent schooling growth. Coefficients (standard errors): executive constraints −0.0099 (0.0118); Polity autocracy 0.0373 (0.0391); Alvarez autocracy 0.0065 (0.0080); democracy −0.0094 (0.0074). All insignificant.

*Panel B — dependent variables: five-year changes in each political measure (N = 499, 499, 349, 499).* Initial years of schooling significantly predicts subsequent change on three of four: executive constraints **+0.4975** (0.1191), Polity autocracy **−0.9092** (0.1790), democracy **+0.7004** (0.1804). The fourth — Alvarez autocracy, −0.0958 (0.0707) — is the one that fails, and it is worth naming rather than leaving as an anonymous "3 of 4". Initial log GDP per capita predicts *no* institutional change on any of the four. All four measures show strong mean reversion in their own lagged level (coefficients −0.51 to −0.86).

**[empirical]** **Panel A contains a finding this wiki has so far omitted, and it complicates the headline.** Initial log GDP per capita is a large, significant predictor of subsequent schooling growth: **+0.2839** (0.0790), and +0.3978 (0.1055) in the Polity specification. So the paper's own sequencing table does not show human capital as the unmoved first mover. It shows income → schooling → institutions, with income having no *direct* path to institutional change. That is a three-link chain, not a two-link one, and the first link is the one the paper's own rhetoric skips.

**[wiki synthesis]** The consequence is worth stating plainly because it cuts both ways. The chain is still incompatible with the AJR/Rodrik ordering — nothing in Table 12 lets institutions cause anything. But it is also weaker than "human capital is the deep cause": on this table, human capital is an intermediate that income drives. Anyone using Glaeser et al. to argue that education is the exogenous root of development is using a stronger claim than Table 12 supports, and the paper's conclusion ("it might be less profitable to look for the 'deep' factors... than for policies favoring human and physical capital accumulation") sidesteps rather than resolves this.

**[empirical]** **The supporting evidence, weakest-first, with the authors' own hedges.**

- *Tables 5–6, growth regressions on initial values.* Decade by decade from 1960, and for 1960–2000 as a whole, initial executive constraints have **no predictive power for subsequent growth outside the 1980s**, while initial human capital is a strong predictor. Table 6 extends this on small samples using executive-constraints data back to the mid-19th century (Maddison 2003 growth, Lindert 2001 primary enrolments from 1870): again no evidence that constraints predict growth, some evidence that initial human capital does. They use executive constraints specifically because it is "the only measure that is clearly not a consequence of dictatorial choices."
- *The "averaging" rescue, pre-empted.* The obvious defence of the levels results is that the *average* institutional score over a period proxies durable constraint even if each year's score tracks the last election. Glaeser et al. test it: regressing ten-year growth on the **previous** decade's average executive constraints yields results "very similar to those in Table 5" — no predictive power. The affirmative results survive only when growth is regressed on *contemporaneous* average institutional quality, which is the specification reverse causality most easily explains.
- *Tables 7–9, the poor-country cross-tabs.* Splitting the 1960 sample by schooling tercile (low < 2.68 years, intermediate 2.68–5.01, high > 5.01) against 1960–2000 average Polity democracy score (autocracy < 2, imperfect autocracy 2–7, imperfect democracy 7–10, stable democracy = 10): nearly all high-schooling countries are stable democracies and nearly all dictatorships are poorly educated. The only dictatorships at intermediate human capital are **Paraguay, Singapore and Taiwan**; the only stable democracies at intermediate human capital are **Italy and Costa Rica**. High-1960-human-capital countries grew ~2× faster over 1960–2000, and growth *dispersion* is much higher among autocracies and poorly-educated countries — which the authors read as policy choice, not institutional constraint, driving the variance. Leader-tenure data: the least-educated countries spend **66%** of sample-years under autocrats who eventually last five years or more, against ~13% under stable democratic leaders.
- *North/South Korea (Figure 1).* Explicitly illustrative, n = 1. Near-identical starting conditions, similarly poor 1950–1980 executive-constraint scores (South 2.16, North 1.71 out of 7), the South's dictators choosing capitalism ($1,589 per capita by 1980 against $768), and democratisation arriving only after 1980 — *following* growth.

**[wiki synthesis]** The cross-tabs and the Korea pair are decoration. They are consistent with the sequencing claim and consistent with several rival orderings, and the authors say so. If Table 12 fell, nothing else on this list would hold the claim up.

## The identification, and what it assumes

**[empirical]** **The crux, stated exactly.** Section V re-runs AJR's own instruments and asks what they instrument *for*. Glaeser et al. take log settler mortality and log 1500 population density (used one at a time), plus French legal origin, as instruments for **both** average years of schooling and average executive constraints 1960–2000 simultaneously, with log GDP per capita 2000 as the outcome and temperate-zone population share as a control (their Table 11).

*First stage.* Log settler mortality loads on schooling (**−1.0183**, s.e. 0.2293) more strongly than on executive constraints (−0.8212, 0.2053); log 1500 population density likewise (**−0.6140**, 0.1691 vs. −0.3737, 0.1582). French legal origin behaves oppositely — it loads hard on executive constraints (−1.4124, −1.1988) and not at all on schooling. Outside the horse race, log settler mortality correlates **−.73** with years of schooling in 2000.

*Second stage.* Predicted years of schooling is significant and positive: **+0.7894** (0.2753) with the settler-mortality instrument, **+0.4836** (0.1875) with the 1500-density instrument. Predicted executive constraints is **negative and insignificant in both**: −0.3432 (0.2577) and −0.2965 (0.2410). N = 47 and 55; F-tests for excluded instruments 17.23 and 4.70.

**[empirical]** **What the authors conclude from it, kept narrow.** Not that human capital is identified — that the instruments are *not valid instruments for institutions specifically*, because the exclusion restriction (no channel from settler mortality to income except through institutions) is indefensible when the instrument tracks human capital more strongly, and because the instrument also correlates .67 with modern malaria risk and .66 with malaria ecology. They concede colonisers brought "guns, germs, and steel" too (Diamond 1997; Engerman & Sokoloff 1991), so their own second stage inherits the same objection: **whatever channel invalidates the instrument for institutions invalidates it for schooling.**

**[wiki synthesis]** **Two things about Table 11 that this wiki's other pages do not carry and that matter for how much weight the horse race can bear.**

First, **the two predicted regressors are correlated at 0.8182 and 0.8163.** The paper reports this in the table footer and does not discuss it. A horse race between two instrumented variables whose fitted values are collinear at r ≈ 0.82, on 47 and 55 observations, has very little power to attribute the shared variance to one of them. "Schooling wins" here should be read as *the instruments cannot separate the two, and the sample splits the difference toward schooling*, not as a decisive contest. The F-test of 4.70 on the 1500-density specification is a further reason to hold that column loosely.

Second, **the executive-constraints coefficient is not merely insignificant but negative in both columns.** A wrong-signed point estimate is not evidence that institutions harm income; it is a symptom that the specification is not identifying an institutions effect at all. That reading is more consistent with the authors' own stated conclusion (the instrument is invalid) than with the stronger reading their framing invites.

Third, and structurally: **this is a levels design.** It cannot establish sequencing, and Glaeser et al. do not claim it does. The identification section and the sequencing section support each other rhetorically but are logically independent — the horse race would survive if Table 12 fell, and vice versa. Treat them as two claims, not one.

**[empirical, added 2026-08-21]** **[[institutions-human-capital-development]] directly disputes the instrument itself, not just its estimated coefficients.** Acemoglu, Gallego & Robinson argue French legal origin, conditional on settler mortality and population density, has no explanatory power for the expropriation-risk/executive-constraint construct AJR and this page are both about — it instead affects *contracting* institutions (court efficiency), a different construct. If correct, the French-legal-origin leg of Table 11 was never a valid instrument for either variable, independent of the collinearity problem above.

## The mechanism, and how it was selected — now contested on two fronts

**[model]** The proposed channel is **political, not technological**. Glaeser et al. consider and set aside the human-capital-externality reading (Lucas 1988) on the grounds that Pritchett (2000) finds returns to education in developing countries are not especially high, and adopt Lipset (1960) instead: human capital produces "more benign politics, less violence, and more political stability" — in their phrase, **"courts and legislatures replace guns"** — and it is that stability which then secures property and enables growth. Supporting citation: Alesina et al. (1996) on political stability predicting growth.

**[model]** Nested inside this is Djankov et al. (2003)'s **institutional possibility frontier**: a community's human and social capital determines the *set* of institutional arrangements available to it; actual institutions are points chosen on that set by efficiency, history and politics. On this framing institutions are persistent (history shapes the choice) and improve with wealth (the opportunity set expands), but they have only a **second-order** effect on economic performance — the first-order effect running from human and social capital, which shapes both institutional and productive capacity.

**[model, added 2026-08-21] The mechanism gap this page previously flagged as unaddressed now has a formal answer — from the same research programme.** [[why-democracy-needs-education]] (Glaeser, Ponzetto & Shleifer) derives the channel formally: schooling raises the local/social benefit of civic participation, which disproportionately strengthens the broad, weakly-incentivised coalition (democracy) against the narrow, strongly-incentivised one (dictatorship), because democracy structurally needs many weak supporters where dictatorship needs few strong ones. **This is not independent corroboration** — Glaeser and Shleifer are co-authors of both papers — but it does convert the earlier "adopted by citation, never measured" mechanism into a derived, testable one.

**[empirical, added 2026-08-21] And the mechanism-selection argument that paper uses to get there is directly complicated by causal evidence.** Glaeser, Ponzetto & Shleifer reject a straightforward political-indoctrination mechanism partly because they find education raises social participation broadly, not selectively. [[curriculum-and-ideology]] (Cantoni et al.) is a genuine natural experiment — China's staggered 2001–2010 curriculum reform — showing targeted, content-specific belief change **did** occur: trust rose specifically for government institutions and not for non-governmental ones, in the direction the state's own reform documents named as their goal, with an estimated persuasion rate (~20%) larger than comparable media-persuasion studies. **[wiki synthesis]** This does not prove indoctrination is *the* mechanism behind Glaeser et al.'s original correlation — Cantoni's case is a different country, reform, and outcome set entirely — but it removes one of the two grounds Glaeser, Ponzetto & Shleifer use to rule indoctrination out, and it establishes that schooling's institutional effects are at minimum *content-dependent*, not a fixed, direction-neutral socialisation dividend. [[economics-of-cultural-transmission]] (Bisin & Verdier) supplies the formal scope conditions for when such top-down content pushes succeed (population balance, substitution vs. complementarity in the transmission technology) versus trigger compensatory resistance — a parameterised answer this page did not have before job 17, in place of a single verdict either way.

## The rebuttal camp, now populated

**[empirical, added 2026-08-21] Two sources directly, explicitly, by name engage this exact paper — the single highest-priority item this page asked job 17 to find.**

[[education-democracy-fixed-effects-critique]] (Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson & Yared, 2005) replicates Glaeser et al.'s own regressions on democracy/political-institutions measures and shows the positive result depends entirely on omitting **year** fixed effects — country fixed effects alone are not enough, because education and democracy both trended upward globally over 1965–2000, and a country-FE-only design attributes that shared secular trend to education. Adding year dummies flips every coefficient negative and insignificant. **Scope caveat, stated precisely rather than assumed**: it is not fully established from the capture whether this replication targets the identical Table 12 sequencing specification or a related-but-distinct regression within the same 2004 paper — treat it as a direct, technically rigorous engagement with the same broad claim and the same authors, not a confirmed line-for-line re-run of Table 12's own numbers.

[[institutions-human-capital-development]] (Acemoglu, Gallego & Robinson, 2014) goes further: a **fully independent re-identification**, using different instruments entirely (protestant missionary presence 1920s and 1900 primary enrolment for human capital; the classic, corrected settler-mortality and population-density instruments for institutions) and a different institutions measure (WGI rule of law, not Polity). Once both are properly instrumented, human capital's coefficient collapses to 0.069–0.094 — statistically indistinguishable from zero and consistent with microeconomic Mincerian returns (6–10%) — while institutions' coefficient stays large (1.32–1.46) across every robustness check, including dropping neo-Europes and controlling for malaria and climate. Testing the reverse direction, instrumented schooling has **no significant effect on institutions**, "half the time even has the wrong sign." The same paper independently attacks the mechanism's historical premise: contrary to Glaeser et al.'s assumption that European settlers "brought" human capital to the colonies that became inclusive institutions, the authors' own literacy data show **Spanish conquistadors were more literate than British North American settlers** (76.6% vs. ~41–60% able to sign their names) — "It is plausible, but it turns out not to be correct."

**[wiki synthesis]** Neither rebuttal is a literal replication of Table 12 on Glaeser et al.'s own data and specification — that specific item on this page's prior list remains formally unfulfilled. But together they are a substantially stronger response than "no reply exists": a direct time-effects critique of the closely related democracy regressions, a fully independent cross-country and cross-regional re-identification reaching the opposite conclusion on the growth question, an explicit instrument-validity objection to the French-legal-origin leg of Table 11, and a reversal of the mechanism's own historical premise. Weighed together, they shift this page's confidence in the sequencing claim downward, without eliminating it — Table 12's own three-of-four schooling-predicts-institutional-change result has not itself been re-run and refuted on its own terms by either paper.

## The reverse-causality alternative, from a different design entirely

**[model + empirical, "suggestive," added 2026-08-21]** [[nation-building-and-education]] (Alesina, Giuliano & Reich) supplies exactly what this page's expansion note asked for — an affirmative-camp-adjacent source with institutions causally prior to schooling — but by a mechanism neither this page nor its critics anticipated. In their model, a ruler's schooling policy is driven by the **threat of losing power**: a secure dictator does no nation-building; the more threatened a ruler is by democratisation, the more intensely he deploys schooling to homogenise national culture and lock in preferred outcomes before losing control. Historical cases (France, Italy, England) show intense nationalist schooling reform arriving in the same period as, and often just before, major suffrage extensions — read as elite-driven pre-emption, not popular demand (surveys of 1830s riot causes in 526 English parishes show no mentions of education as a grievance). Their cross-country panel (172 countries, 1925–2004) finds threats to autocratic regimes predict subsequent educational reform, though the authors themselves call this "suggestive correlations," not a causal estimate on par with the sequencing designs above.

**[wiki synthesis]** This does not contradict Table 12's finding that schooling predicts subsequent institutional change — it supplies a *prior* stage the sequencing panel cannot see: an already-existing power configuration and its anticipated trajectory determining whether and how much schooling gets deployed in the first place. The two claims can both be true, describing different links in a longer, partly recursive chain (existing power → schooling deployment → culture → future institutional form) rather than a single two-link arrow in either direction.

## Sub-national evidence, at last — and its own honest limit

**[empirical, added 2026-08-21]** [[culture-institutions-regions-of-europe]] (Tabellini) is the sub-national design this page's own list called for: 69 European regions, 8 countries, using regional literacy circa 1880 and regional political-institution history (1600–1850) as instruments for present-day culture, with country fixed effects removing the confound every cross-country design in this debate carries. Culture instrumented this way predicts regional output strongly — the Lombardy/Southern Italy culture gap implies "almost half of the observed income difference." Applying the identical logic cross-nationally with **AJR's own settler-mortality instrument**, culture (not schooling per se) explains "over two thirds of the difference in output per worker, and almost all of the difference in institutional outcomes" between high- and low-culture countries.

**[wiki synthesis]** Tabellini does not resolve Q94 any more than Glaeser et al. do, and says so in his own words: he names explicitly, as the "special case" that would invalidate his design, the exact reverse-causality threat this whole debate turns on — history affecting output directly, with output then shaping culture, rather than his own postulated history → culture → output chain — and his one attempt to address it (a 13-observation test using Italy's 1946 anti-monarchy referendum) "cannot dispel all doubts," in his words. His conclusion refuses the framing this debate keeps reaching for: **"There is no primacy of formal institutions over culture. On the contrary, both are likely to interact."** Treat Tabellini as a genuine methodological advance on the level-of-analysis problem (see [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D90), not as a tiebreaker on sequencing.

## What it would mean if true

**[wiki synthesis]** **This section is inference, not source, and now carries more caveats than it did before job 17.** If the sequencing claim survives further scrutiny, the consequence for this project's power/institutions/economy triad is structural rather than marginal — but "survives" is now doing real work in that sentence, given the rebuttal camp above.

- **Institutions move substantially downstream, if the claim holds.** The triad's working reading places institutions as the mediating term through which power arrangements produce or obstruct economic output. On the schooling hypothesis, institutional quality is instead largely a *lagging indicator* of a population's accumulated human and social capital — an output of the system rather than its lever. The arrow does not fully reverse (nobody here argues institutions are inert), but the position of the institutions node changes.
- **The prescriptive limb (project outcome 3) is hit hardest, either way.** A playbook that says "fix the rules and the output follows" presupposes institutions are upstream and manipulable. If Djankov et al.'s frontier framing is right, the reachable institutional set is *bounded* by human and social capital, and institutional reform imported above that bound is attempting a point off the frontier — the same structure as [[isomorphic-mimicry-and-capability-traps]], reached from a completely different literature. But [[nation-building-and-education]]'s recursive account suggests the frontier itself is partly a policy choice by whoever currently holds power, which complicates "bounded by capital" into "bounded by capital, which is itself shaped by who is deploying schooling and why."
- **It relocates rather than dissolves the question.** "Human capital" as measured on this page is years of schooling — a national average. [[curriculum-and-ideology]] shows the relevant variable is at least as much *content* as *years* — a claim neither Glaeser et al. nor their critics engage directly, since none of the growth-regression literature on either side of Q94 measures curriculum content.
- **It does not license the paper's policy conclusion, on any reading.** Glaeser et al. conclude that good-for-growth dictators can substitute for constrained government, citing Deng-vs-Mao. That is a claim about the *variance* of autocratic outcomes, and their own Table 8 shows exactly that variance — a reason for caution about the strategy, not confidence in it.

## Why this page was thin, and what changed

**[wiki synthesis]** This page previously rested on one source, one research team, and one paper, with the team a party to the argument rather than an arbiter of it. Two things about that assessment stand unchanged by job 17, and four things about it are now different.

**Unchanged**: three of the four original authors (La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, Shleifer) are the core of the LLSV legal-origins programme; Glaeser works in the same tradition; the paper's conclusion still reads as more politically confident than its own hedged statistical language licenses. And the measurement critique and the schooling result are still not independent of each other — they come from the same paper and the same priors.

**Changed**: (1) **The paper is no longer unreplicated and unengaged.** [[education-democracy-fixed-effects-critique]] and [[institutions-human-capital-development]] both name it directly and dispute it on independent grounds. (2) **The reinforcing mechanism paper is not independent, and this page now says so explicitly rather than treating [[why-democracy-needs-education]] as a second source** — same authors, same programme, widening the same team's footprint rather than corroborating it from outside. (3) **A genuine ideological-balance gate is now met**: [[institutions-human-capital-development]] and [[nation-building-and-education]] both argue institutions/power precede human capital, from the affirmative-camp side, with their own designs. (4) **The mechanism is no longer adopted by citation and unmeasured** — it has a formal derivation and a direct causal complication (Cantoni) neither of which existed on this page before.

## What would still strengthen or kill it

**[wiki synthesis]** Revised in light of job 17. Struck items are answered; the rest stand.

1. ~~Independent replication of the Table 12 sequencing design.~~ **Partially answered.** [[education-democracy-fixed-effects-critique]] directly disputes the closely related democracy regressions with a time-effects critique; [[institutions-human-capital-development]] runs a fully independent parallel design reaching the opposite conclusion on the growth question. Neither is a confirmed line-for-line re-run of Table 12's own specification — that remains formally open.
2. ~~A symmetric sequencing design from the affirmative camp.~~ **Answered, partially and unexpectedly.** [[nation-building-and-education]] supplies institutions-cause-schooling evidence, but via a regime-threat mechanism neither this page nor Q94 anticipated, and self-described as "suggestive."
3. ~~Sub-national or within-country variation.~~ **Answered.** [[culture-institutions-regions-of-europe]] — genuinely advances the level-of-analysis problem, does not itself resolve sequencing, and says so.
4. **Natural experiments in schooling expansion, not chosen by the government whose institutions are the outcome.** [[institutions-human-capital-development]]'s missionary and 1900-enrolment instruments are a step in this direction for the *growth* question, not the *institutions* question specifically — still open for a design that isolates an exogenous schooling shock and traces it forward to institutional (not just income) outcomes.
5. **Direct measurement of the Lipset channel** (violence, dispute-resolution mode, political stability) remains absent from every source in this wiki bearing on this claim.
6. ~~Work reporting the reverse sequencing.~~ **Answered**, per item 2.
7. **Anything that separates the two instrumented regressors in Table 11.** Still open, and now sharpened: [[institutions-human-capital-development]] argues the French-legal-origin leg was never valid for either variable, which if right makes "separating" the wrong frame — the instrument would need replacing, not refining.
8. **New, added by job 17: does curriculum *content*, not years of schooling, mediate the schooling-institutions relationship?** [[curriculum-and-ideology]] and [[economics-of-cultural-transmission]] make this the most concrete open item this page now has — no source on either side of Q94 has re-run any growth or sequencing regression with a content measure rather than an enrolment/years measure.
