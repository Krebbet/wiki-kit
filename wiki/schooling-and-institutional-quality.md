# Schooling and Institutional Quality

**The schooling hypothesis: human capital is upstream of institutional quality, not downstream of it.** Glaeser, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer (2004) argue that the causal arrow the growth literature assumes runs backwards — that education accumulates first (often under unconstrained rulers, as a policy choice), that political improvement follows, and that both growth and institutional quality are consequences of human capital rather than institutions being the cause of growth. Their load-bearing evidence is a single fixed-effects panel at five-year intervals in which **initial years of schooling predict subsequent improvement in political institutions on three of four measures, while no political measure predicts subsequent improvement in schooling**; their identification evidence is a re-run of AJR's own settler-mortality instrument showing it loads more strongly on schooling than on executive constraints, with predicted schooling significant in the second stage and predicted executive constraints wrong-signed and insignificant. **This page rests on one paper by one research team that is a party to the dispute, and is deliberately provisional pending research job 17.**

**Evidence tier note.** The single source is **(a) empirical**: cross-country OLS and 2SLS on a 1960–2000 global panel, plus one fixed-effects sequencing panel and one illustrative paired case. No formal model. The paper's *mechanism* — how education produces institutional improvement — is (b), adopted by citation from Lipset (1960) and Djankov et al. (2003), not measured here. The authors self-caveat repeatedly ("we do not wish to push these results too far"; "at most suggestive") and this page keeps those caveats attached rather than stripping them.

## The claim, stated precisely

**[empirical]** The claim is a *sequencing* claim, not a levels claim, and the distinction is what makes it testable. Glaeser et al. do not dispute that measured institutional quality correlates with national income — they concede the correlation and explain it in the other direction. Their proposed order is:

1. Human capital accumulates — frequently under dictatorship, as a discretionary policy choice rather than as a product of political constraint.
2. Political stability and measured institutional improvement follow.
3. Growth follows, and is *also* driven directly by human capital.

The negative half is stated as flatly as the positive half: "at least in the OLS regressions, the evidence that institutions cause economic growth, as opposed to growth improving institutions, is non-existent." Note what they are *not* claiming. They explicitly disown "institutions don't matter" — "That proposition is flatly contradicted by a great deal of available empirical evidence, including our own" — and they disown the normative reading, citing Mulligan, Gil & Sala-i-Martin (2004) that democracies are significantly more benign on press freedom, torture, the death penalty and religious regulation, and framing democracy as valuable independently of growth. The claim is about *what causes what, in poor countries, over the post-1960 record*.

The measurement critique that underpins the negative half — that ICRG expropriation risk, KKM government effectiveness and Polity constraints-on-the-executive are outcome and perception measures rather than durable constraints — is set out at [[colonial-origins-and-the-settler-mortality-instrument]] and is not restated here. It matters to this page only as a premise: if the institutional variables are recording recent policy choice, then a levels regression cannot distinguish the two orderings, and only a timing design can.

## The sequencing test, and exactly what it shows

**[empirical]** **Table 12 is the paper's strongest evidence by its own framing, and the only design in its entire literature batch that targets direction of causality rather than correlating levels.** Country fixed effects, five-year intervals, errors clustered at country level, following Barro (1997, 1999) on the timing approach.

*Panel A — dependent variable: five-year change in years of schooling (N = 514, or 420 for the Polity-autocracy specification).* None of the four political measures predicts subsequent schooling growth. Coefficients (standard errors): executive constraints −0.0099 (0.0118); Polity autocracy 0.0373 (0.0391); Alvarez autocracy 0.0065 (0.0080); democracy −0.0094 (0.0074). All insignificant.

*Panel B — dependent variables: five-year changes in each political measure (N = 499, 499, 349, 499).* Initial years of schooling significantly predicts subsequent change on three of four: executive constraints **+0.4975** (0.1191), Polity autocracy **−0.9092** (0.1790), democracy **+0.7004** (0.1804). The fourth — Alvarez autocracy, −0.0958 (0.0707) — is the one that fails, and it is worth naming rather than leaving as an anonymous "3 of 4". Initial log GDP per capita predicts *no* institutional change on any of the four. All four measures show strong mean reversion in their own lagged level (coefficients −0.51 to −0.86).

**[empirical]** **Panel A contains a finding this wiki has so far omitted, and it complicates the headline.** Initial log GDP per capita is a large, significant predictor of subsequent schooling growth: **+0.2839** (0.0790), and +0.3978 (0.1055) in the Polity specification. So the paper's own sequencing table does not show human capital as the unmoved first mover. It shows income → schooling → institutions, with income having no *direct* path to institutional change. That is a three-link chain, not a two-link one, and the first link is the one the paper's own rhetoric skips.

**[wiki synthesis]** The consequence is worth stating plainly because it cuts both ways. The chain is still incompatible with the AJR/Rodrik ordering — nothing in Table 12 lets institutions cause anything. But it is also weaker than "human capital is the deep cause": on this table, human capital is an intermediate that income drives. Anyone using Glaeser et al. to argue that education is the exogenous root of development is using a stronger claim than Table 12 supports, and the paper's conclusion ("it might be less profitable to look for the 'deep' factors... than for policies favoring human and physical capital accumulation") sidesteps rather than resolves this.

**[empirical]** **The supporting evidence, weakest-first, with the authors' own hedges.**

- *Tables 5–6, growth regressions on initial values.* Decade by decade from 1960, and for 1960–2000 as a whole, initial executive constraints have **no predictive power for subsequent growth outside the 1980s**, while initial human capital is a strong predictor. Table 6 extends this on small samples using executive-constraints data back to the mid-19th century (Maddison 2003 growth, Lindert 2001 primary enrolments from 1870): again no evidence that constraints predict growth, some evidence that initial human capital does. They use executive constraints specifically because it is "the only measure that is clearly not a consequence of dictatorial choices."
- *The "averaging" rescue, pre-empted.* The obvious defence of the levels results is that the *average* institutional score over a period proxies durable constraint even if each year's score tracks the last election. Glaeser et al. test it: regressing ten-year growth on the **previous** decade's average executive constraints yields results "very similar to those in Table 5" — no predictive power. The affirmative results survive only when growth is regressed on *contemporaneous* average institutional quality, which is the specification reverse causality most easily explains.
- *Tables 7–9, the poor-country cross-tabs.* Splitting the 1960 sample by schooling tercile (low < 2.68 years, intermediate 2.68–5.01, high > 5.01) against 1960–2000 average Polity democracy score (autocracy < 2, imperfect autocracy 2–7, imperfect democracy 7–10, stable democracy = 10): nearly all high-schooling countries are stable democracies and nearly all dictatorships are poorly educated. The only dictatorships at intermediate human capital are **Paraguay, Singapore and Taiwan**; the only stable democracies at intermediate human capital are **Italy and Costa Rica**. High-1960-human-capital countries grew ~2× faster over 1960–2000, and growth *dispersion* is much higher among autocracies and poorly-educated countries — which the authors read as policy choice, not institutional constraint, driving the variance. Leader-tenure data: the least-educated countries spend **66%** of sample-years under autocrats who eventually last five years or more, against ~13% under stable democratic leaders.
- *North/South Korea (Figure 1).* Explicitly illustrative, n = 1. Near-identical starting conditions, similarly poor 1950–1980 executive-constraint scores (South 2.16, North 1.71 out of 7), the South's dictators choosing capitalism ($1,589 per capita by 1980 against $768), and democratisation arriving only after 1980 — *following* growth.

**[wiki synthesis]** The cross-tabs and the Korea pair are decoration. They are consistent with the sequencing claim and consistent with several rival orderings, and the authors say so. If Table 12 fell, nothing else on this list would hold the claim up.

## The identification, and what it assumes

**[empirical]** **The crux, stated exactly.** Section V re-runs AJR's own instruments and asks what they instrument *for*. Glaeser et al. take log settler mortality and log 1500 population density (used one at a time), plus French legal origin, as instruments for **both** average years of schooling and average executive constraints 1960–2000 simultaneously, with log GDP per capita 2000 as the outcome and temperate-zone population share as a control (their Table 11).

*First stage.* Log settler mortality loads on schooling (**−1.0183**, s.e. 0.2293) more strongly than on executive constraints (−0.8212, 0.2053); log 1500 population density likewise (**−0.6140**, 0.1691 vs. −0.3737, 0.1582). French legal origin behaves oppositely — it loads hard on executive constraints (−1.4124, −1.1988) and not at all on schooling. Outside the horse race, log settler mortality correlates **−.73** with years of schooling in 2000.

*Second stage.* Predicted years of schooling is significant and positive: **+0.7894** (0.2753) with the settler-mortality instrument, **+0.4836** (0.1875) with the 1500-density instrument. Predicted executive constraints is **negative and insignificant in both**: −0.3432 (0.2577) and −0.2965 (0.2410). N = 47 and 55; F-tests for excluded instruments 17.23 and 4.70.

**[empirical]** **What the authors conclude from it, kept narrow.** Not that human capital is identified — that the instruments are *not valid instruments for institutions specifically*, because the exclusion restriction (no channel from settler mortality to income except through institutions) is indefensible when the instrument tracks human capital more strongly, and because the instrument also correlates .67 with modern malaria risk and .66 with malaria ecology. They concede colonisers brought "guns, germs, and steel" too (Diamond 1997; Engerman & Sokoloff 1991), so their own second stage inherits the same objection: **whatever channel invalidates the instrument for institutions invalidates it for schooling.**

**[wiki synthesis]** **Two things about Table 11 that this wiki's other pages do not carry and that matter for how much weight the horse race can bear.**

First, **the two predicted regressors are correlated at 0.8182 and 0.8163.** The paper reports this in the table footer and does not discuss it. A horse race between two instrumented variables whose fitted values are collinear at r ≈ 0.82, on 47 and 55 observations, has very little power to attribute the shared variance to one of them. "Schooling wins" here should be read as *the instruments cannot separate the two, and the sample splits the difference toward schooling*, not as a decisive contest. The F-test of 4.70 on the 1500-density specification is a further reason to hold that column loosely.

Second, **the executive-constraints coefficient is not merely insignificant but negative in both columns.** A wrong-signed point estimate is not evidence that institutions harm income; it is a symptom that the specification is not identifying an institutions effect at all. That reading is more consistent with the authors' own stated conclusion (the instrument is invalid) than with the stronger reading their framing invites.

Third, and structurally: **this is a levels design.** It cannot establish sequencing, and Glaeser et al. do not claim it does. The identification section and the sequencing section support each other rhetorically but are logically independent — the horse race would survive if Table 12 fell, and vice versa. Treat them as two claims, not one.

## The mechanism, and how it was selected

**[model]** The proposed channel is **political, not technological**. Glaeser et al. consider and set aside the human-capital-externality reading (Lucas 1988) on the grounds that Pritchett (2000) finds returns to education in developing countries are not especially high, and adopt Lipset (1960) instead: human capital produces "more benign politics, less violence, and more political stability" — in their phrase, **"courts and legislatures replace guns"** — and it is that stability which then secures property and enables growth. Supporting citation: Alesina et al. (1996) on political stability predicting growth.

**[model]** Nested inside this is Djankov et al. (2003)'s **institutional possibility frontier**: a community's human and social capital determines the *set* of institutional arrangements available to it; actual institutions are points chosen on that set by efficiency, history and politics. On this framing institutions are persistent (history shapes the choice) and improve with wealth (the opportunity set expands), but they have only a **second-order** effect on economic performance — the first-order effect running from human and social capital, which shapes both institutional and productive capacity.

**[wiki synthesis]** The mechanism is the weakest link in the paper and is not evidenced anywhere in it. It is adopted by citation, chosen as a residual after the direct-returns interpretation was found wanting, and never measured — no violence variable, no dispute-resolution variable, no stability variable enters any regression on this page. The paper is an argument about *timing* with a mechanism attached afterwards. That is the reverse of this wiki's stated preference for mechanism over correlation, and it is the largest single thing job 17 needs to fix.

## What it would mean if true

**[wiki synthesis]** **This section is inference, not source.** If the sequencing claim survives replication, the consequence for this project's power/institutions/economy triad is structural rather than marginal.

- **Institutions move substantially downstream.** The triad's working reading places institutions as the mediating term through which power arrangements produce or obstruct economic output. On the schooling hypothesis, institutional quality is instead largely a *lagging indicator* of a population's accumulated human and social capital — an output of the system rather than its lever. The arrow does not fully reverse (nobody here argues institutions are inert), but the position of the institutions node changes.
- **The prescriptive limb (project outcome 3) is hit hardest.** A playbook that says "fix the rules and the output follows" presupposes institutions are upstream and manipulable. If Djankov et al.'s frontier framing is right, the reachable institutional set is *bounded* by human and social capital, and institutional reform imported above that bound is attempting a point off the frontier. This is the same structure as the capability-trap argument at [[isomorphic-mimicry-and-capability-traps]], reached from a completely different literature and a completely different ideological direction — which is a reason to take it more seriously, not less.
- **It relocates rather than dissolves the question.** "Human capital" as measured here is years of schooling — a national average. Schooling is itself produced by institutions (education ministries, school systems, universities), and the paper never asks who built them. So a confirmed sequencing result would not remove institutions from the causal chain; it would say that *the institutions that matter are the ones producing human capital*, not the ones constraining executives — which is a claim about **which** institutions, not about whether. Nothing in the source addresses this, and it should be a first-order question for job 17.
- **It does not license the paper's policy conclusion.** Glaeser et al. conclude that good-for-growth dictators can substitute for constrained government, citing Deng-vs-Mao. That is a claim about the *variance* of autocratic outcomes, and their own Table 8 shows exactly that variance — which is a reason for caution about the strategy, not confidence in it. The step from "schooling precedes institutional improvement" to "democratisation need not come first" is a policy inference the statistics do not carry.

## Why this page is thin, stated plainly

**[wiki synthesis]** This page has **one source, one research team, and one paper**, and the team is a party to the argument rather than an arbiter of it.

- Three of the four authors (La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, Shleifer) are the core of the LLSV legal-origins programme, broadly market-liberal and policy-over-politics in orientation; Glaeser works in the same tradition. The paper's conclusion reads as more politically confident than its own hedged statistical language licenses.
- **The measurement critique and the schooling result are not independent.** They come from the same paper, the same authors and the same priors. The measurement critique clears the ground (the institutional indices don't measure institutions) and the schooling result occupies it. A reader who accepts the first is being handed the second, but the first does not entail the second — the indices could be bad measures *and* institutions could still be upstream. Anyone weighing this page should weigh those two claims separately.
- The supporting apparatus leans heavily on the authors' own prior work (La Porta et al. 1997/1998/1999/2004; Djankov et al. 2003), including the framework — the institutional possibility frontier — that gives the result its interpretation. Self-citation of this density is not disqualifying and the regression results are self-contained, but it means the paper's *framing* is not corroborated by anyone outside the group.
- **No replication is recorded in this wiki.** The wiki does not currently know whether anyone independent has re-run Table 12. That is a gap in this page's evidence, not a fact about the literature, and it is stated here so it is not mistaken for one.

## What would strengthen or kill it

**[wiki synthesis]** Concretely, in rough order of how much each would move the claim:

1. **Independent replication of the Table 12 sequencing design** — by anyone outside the LLSV group, on updated data (the panel ends in 2000, over two decades ago), and on the measures the affirmative camp prefers rather than only Polity-family ones. This is the single highest-value item. It is cheap to run and its absence from this wiki is conspicuous.
2. **A symmetric sequencing design from the affirmative camp** — the test named at [[open-questions]] Q94. That literature has produced dozens of cross-sectional IV specifications and, in this wiki's batch, exactly one timing test, run by its opponents. If a symmetric design exists and reports the opposite ordering, that is the most direct kill.
3. **Sub-national or within-country variation.** Everything on this page is one number per country. A design comparing regions, districts or states inside a single national institutional environment would break the country-scalar problem flagged at Q90 and would remove the whole class of cross-country confounds — including the modern-malaria confound Glaeser et al. themselves raise against AJR and inherit.
4. **Natural experiments in schooling expansion** — compulsory-schooling laws, school-construction programmes, mission-station placement, any exogenous shift in education that can be traced forward to subsequent institutional outcomes. This is the design that would supply what Table 12 cannot: a schooling change not chosen by the same government whose institutions are the outcome. Note the confound it must handle — a state capable of expanding schooling is already demonstrating capacity, so the shock must be plausibly independent of the institutional trajectory it is used to predict.
5. **Direct measurement of the Lipset channel.** Violence, dispute-resolution mode, political stability — the intermediate variables the mechanism runs on and which appear in no regression here. If schooling predicts institutional improvement but not through reduced violence, the result survives and the explanation does not.
6. **Work reporting the reverse sequencing** — institutional change preceding schooling expansion. Glaeser et al.'s Panel A finds none, but on four Polity-family measures only, and their own Panel A shows *income* driving schooling, which is a live alternative ordering their framing does not confront.
7. **Anything that separates the two instrumented regressors.** The 0.82 correlation between predicted schooling and predicted executive constraints is the horse race's real limitation. An instrument that moves schooling without moving measured constraint (or vice versa) would turn a near-collinear contest into an actual one.

## Expansion note

**This page is deliberately provisional and should be rewritten, not patched, when research job 17 lands.** Job 17 — `schooling-norms-and-institutional-formation`, queued at [[research-agenda]], requested by the user 2026-08-20 — is scoped to cover human capital as a cause of institutional quality, education and state formation, civic education and norm transmission, cultural transmission of cooperation norms, and the sequencing question directly.

What job 17 must bring back, stated as requirements rather than hopes:

- **At least one source from the affirmative camp** arguing institutions precede human capital, with its design. Ideological balance is a hard gate on this topic and a one-camp expansion would be a research failure, not a finding.
- **Any independent replication or non-replication of the Table 12 sequencing result.**
- **The norms half, which this page does not touch at all.** Glaeser et al. invoke "human and social capital" and measure only years of schooling. Social capital, civic norms, cultural transmission of cooperation — none of it appears in any regression on this page, and the frontier framing that gives the result its meaning depends on it.
- **Sub-national and natural-experiment evidence**, per items 3 and 4 above.
- **Whatever this wiki's assistants know from training and could not source.** There is a substantial named literature adjacent to this claim — modernisation theory, human-capital-and-democracy work, the Lipset tradition's own empirical descendants — that is deliberately absent from this page because no captured source supports it. Its absence here is a rule being followed, not a judgement that it does not exist. Job 17 should go get it.

When that evidence arrives, the correct move is a rewrite: this page's structure is shaped around a single paper's table numbers and will not survive contact with a real literature.
