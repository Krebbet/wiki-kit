# Internal Conflict and Structural Inefficiency

A single-author theory program (van de Poll, two 2026 preprints) argues that organizations lose more performance to a mechanism the literature under-names than to the one it studies most: not misallocated effort, weak incentives, or bounded rationality (**allocative inefficiency**), but the mixed-sign cross-effects an internal mechanism — an incentive scheme, a control, a KPI, an authority line — has across an organization's *own* multiple objectives, which cause effort applied to that mechanism to partly cancel itself out (**structural inefficiency**). The claim is that allocative inefficiency declines as organizations mature and professionalize, while structural inefficiency *rises*, because organizations add mechanisms without removing old ones ("additive layering").

**Evidence tier note.** Both papers are **[model]** in full — formal/definitional frameworks with worked numerical illustrations, no dataset, case study, or regression anywhere in either document. The two read as one contribution split in two: the first (arXiv 2609.20462) supplies the definitions and failure-mode vocabulary; the second (arXiv 2609.23744) builds a measurement/typology layer on top and states, in its own related-work section, a heading that matches the first paper's Section 4.2 verbatim. Treated here as one page rather than two. Author affiliation ("Transparency Lab Research Institute") is a non-university, non-standard provenance for this wiki's usual sourcing (NBER/journals/university-affiliated authors) — flagged for that reason, not dismissed.

## The formal mechanism

**[model]** Each internal mechanism *i* has a signed effect *b_ik* on each of an organization's KPIs *k*. Three quantities are defined from this effect vector:

- **Magnitude** M_i = Σ|b_ik| — total absolute cross-objective influence.
- **Net direction** D_i = Σb_ik — signed net benefit.
- **Internal conflict** C_i = 1 − |D_i|/M_i ∈ [0,1] — the fraction of the mechanism's influence lost to cross-objective cancellation.

Realized performance is R_i = E_i · M_i · (1 − C_i): effort applied, times leverage, times the usable (non-cancelled) fraction. The paper *proves*, given these definitions, that adding a new objective or dimension to an existing mechanism weakly increases conflict unless the new effect is perfectly sign-aligned — so magnitude grows faster than net direction as organizations layer mechanisms without removing old ones, and conflict rises mechanically.

**[wiki synthesis — the honest reading]** This is a derived accounting identity given the model's own primitives, not an estimated causal pathway: C_i is *defined* as the residual after summing signed effects, so whether real organizational mechanisms actually carry persistent, opposing signed effects of this kind is an empirical question the papers explicitly defer to "companion work" — work not presented in either captured document. One paper asserts in the present tense that "forthcoming empirical analyses... show that internal conflict is both prevalent and economically material" while simultaneously calling itself "purely theoretical" two paragraphs away; that gap should be treated as a flag on the framework's credibility, not as evidence it has already cleared.

## A genuinely new failure mode: misdiagnosis

**[model]** Because conflict reduces net outcomes while effort/activity stays visible and high, managers observe *magnitude* (salient) far more readily than *conflict* (diffuse, delayed, politically contested) — an **effort–conflict coupling** that concentrates locally-rational attention on the most-conflicted mechanisms, not the most-productive ones. Left uncorrected, this produces "misdiagnosis": weak performance is attributed to insufficient effort, met with "increased pressure, tighter controls, or additional initiatives" that raise effort without changing conflict, and can *increase* cancellation by intensifying already-conflicted mechanisms — a self-reinforcing escalation spiral. The authors explicitly disclaim incentive-misalignment and information-asymmetry as the explanation: there is no shirking agent here, the mechanism is a visibility asymmetry between two properties of the same primitive. That distinguishes it from goal displacement, capture, rent-seeking, principal-agent drift, and measurement gaming — a genuinely distinct addition to this wiki's failure-mode vocabulary if it survives empirical contact, unverified here.

A second named pattern, "cheese-grater" reorganization — repeated addition of authority/accountability dimensions (product, function, region, risk, ESG) without removing prior ones — and "composite-metric masking" (aggregation hiding rather than resolving cross-objective trade-offs) are offered as instances of the same structural-layering logic.

## Levers, and why they carry no evidence

**[model]** Five governance responses, mapped from a mechanism's (magnitude × direction × conflict) profile: **Scale** (high magnitude, aligned, low conflict → increase deployment), **Guardrail** (aligned, nontrivial conflict → scale with constraints), **Unbundle** (high magnitude, high conflict → split into separate mechanisms), **Redesign or remove** (negative net direction → fix or eliminate), **Ignore** (low magnitude → deprioritize). A companion "conflict pricing" proposal (a shadow price on conflict, formally analogous to Pigouvian internalization of an externality) is offered as the reallocation lever. **Evidence base for all of it: none** — the authors state outright that the work is "intentionally static and theoretical" and defers "measurement instruments, empirical identification strategies, or organizational interventions" to future work, while also asserting the five responses are "structural necessities" rather than discretionary choices. Under this wiki's [[reform-levers]] tiering convention, none of this clears even tier (iii) (pure prescription): the mechanism for each lever is modeled, but no lever has been tried or observed, not even in a single case.

## Scale, age, and scope

**[model]** The maturity claim (allocative inefficiency down, structural inefficiency up, over an organization's life) is asserted directionally, not tested — the "settings" analyzed (incentive-intensive, governance-heavy, KPI-saturated, cheese-grater organizations) are a rough typology, not a panel or cohort. This carries the same epistemic status as Downs's C.11 in [[downs-vs-merton-on-age-dependence]]: a directional theoretical claim about organizational age, with zero data behind it. **Public/private invariance is explicitly asserted** ("neither sector-specific nor limited to poorly managed firms... appears in private corporations, public agencies, and non-profit organizations") but is a bare assertion in the introduction with no comparative evidence anywhere in either paper.

## Not a new engagement with the North/Williamson efficiency debate

**[wiki synthesis]** This framework does not bear directly on [[efficiency-of-institutions-north-vs-williamson]] and should not be filed as a conflict against it. Structural inefficiency here is a third, distinct mechanism — internal, apolitical, purely combinatorial — with no reference to distributional power or path-dependent lock-in (North's mechanism) and no reference to discriminating alignment against feasible governance alternatives (Williamson's remediableness test). If anything, the papers' own account of why "conflict pricing" is rare in practice (measurement difficulty, concentrated-and-attributable benefits versus diffuse costs, institutional inertia) reads closer to a Williamson-style remediableness argument than to North's — but the authors never make that connection themselves, so this is this wiki's own inference, not a stated claim, and does not rise to a precise contradiction with either page.

## A literature gap worth naming

**[wiki synthesis]** Neither paper cites Holmström or Holmström & Milgrom, despite [[multitask-incentive-theory]] being the most directly relevant existing framework for "effort applied to one objective has cross-effects on others sharing a limited budget." Van de Poll's claim that the mechanism is "conceptually distinct" (works "even in the absence of incentive misalignment or informational constraints") is asserted, not argued against Holmström & Milgrom's actual model — a claimed-distinct-but-uncontested-against parallel, not a resolved contrast.
