# The Developmental State and Embedded Autonomy

Wade's account of South Korean, Taiwanese and Singaporean catch-up growth is the standing counter-case to the proposition that growth comes from generic "good institutions" — open markets, secure and general property rights, constrained executives. His claim is that the causal institutions were **selective, discretionary and centralised**: directed credit through state-controlled banks, protection paired with tariff rebates so exporters got duty-free inputs, hard bargaining with inbound multinationals, and above all **performance conditions attached to every form of assistance**, withdrawn if a firm failed to close the price and quality gap with imports. The concept doing the institutional work is **embedded autonomy** (Evans 1995) — bureaucrats close enough to capital for information and buy-in, autonomous enough to discipline it — which Wade treats as necessary but explicitly *not* sufficient. **The finding this wiki should take from him is uncomfortable for the affirmative camp on its own terms: his fastest-growing cases were one-party states and military dictatorships, and would score low on exactly the constraints-on-the-executive axis that Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson's framework treats as the mark of growth-producing institutions.**

**Evidence tier note.** Mixed and sharply uneven, tiered here because the source's strength varies more within itself than between it and its neighbours. **Strongest:** Lane (2017), cited at length — an input-output panel analysis of Korea's 1973–79 Heavy and Chemical Industries drive comparing targeted against non-targeted sectors around a policy-timing break, finding targeted sectors grew faster in output and productivity, the differential *persisting after the policy formally ended in 1979*, with spillovers running downstream through input-output linkages but not upstream. Quasi-experimental in design, not from an exogenous shock. **Middling:** investment/GDP time series (Taiwan 14%→40% 1954–74; Korea 10%→36–39%) offered as suggestive of state capital mobilisation — correlational, no counterfactual. **Weakest:** comparative institutional history, Wade's own 1979 Korea fieldwork, personal communications, and secondary-literature synthesis (Amsden, Evans, Johnson, Kohli, Deyo, Thurbon). Wade concedes the general point himself: the developmental-state literature "has not produced 'knock-out evidence'" and "it is difficult to find 'exogenous' sources of variation from which to test causality 'rigorously'" (quoting Rodrik 2012). This is an argument piece with a committed prior, written by a long-standing advocate of industrial policy, and should be attributed as such rather than read as a neutral review.

## The mechanism, in six parts

**[empirical, case-narrative]** Wade names the decisive factors:

1. **Directed and subsidised credit** through state-owned or state-controlled banks.
2. **Fiscal investment incentives** — tax rebates tied to reaching the technology frontier.
3. **Trade protection paired with a tariff-rebate system**, so exporters obtained duty-free inputs.
4. **Hard bargaining with inbound multinationals** for local-content and skill-transfer commitments.
5. **Performance conditions attached to all of the above** — assistance withdrawn on failure to close the price and quality gap with imports.
6. **Embedded autonomy** as the institutional capacity making 1–5 executable.

**[wiki synthesis]** Item 5 is the load-bearing one and the only one Wade treats as *the* discriminating variable. His counter-cases are unconditional protection: India's Ambassador car, functionally static for decades under indefinite protection; Philippine landlord-patronage networks. His own formulation of the failure mode is blunt — subsidies and protection given "to friends and relatives with no performance conditions... the route to large-scale corruption and slow economic development." **Same instrument, opposite outcome, and the difference is whether the rent can be withdrawn.** This is the batch's one clear institution-level axis and is registered at [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D91.

## Embedded autonomy, and the counter-case Wade supplies himself

**[model]** Embedded autonomy is two properties at once: enough closeness to capital for information feedback and buy-in, enough autonomy from it to impose discipline. Wade treats it as **necessary but not sufficient** and gives his own falsifying instance: **the Bank of England has had embedded autonomy for decades and used it to champion neoliberal rather than developmental ends.** Without a "developmental mindset" — an elite consensus that catch-up is the national project — embedded autonomy produces nothing developmental.

**[wiki synthesis]** That admission is why embedded autonomy is registered `rejected` at [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D94 rather than promoted. There is no ex-ante instrument: the concept is scored on cases already known to have succeeded, and the one high-scoring case with the opposite outcome is used to add a second, unmeasured condition (mindset) rather than to falsify the first. The readmission condition is recorded on the row — an instrument scoring **information density with the client** and **disciplining instances** separately, applied before the outcome is known. Wade's own Bank of England case is exactly what such an instrument would need to classify in advance.

## The political scaffolding, and why it is a problem for the inclusive-institutions story

**[empirical, case-narrative]** Wade treats the following as jointly necessary alongside the economic levers: centralised bureaucratic power in one or a few dominant ministries; an elite consensus around catch-up; **labour repression and exclusion from the "embedded" relationship**; land reform removing a landed-elite veto; and Cold War geopolitical luck — front-line containment status securing US aid, market access, and tolerance of protectionism, all before the WTO and before what he calls the global political awakening.

**[empirical]** The political facts, in his own framing: "Taiwan was a repressive one-party state until the late 1980s; Korea was a repressive military dictatorship until the late 1980s"; and "the 'embedded' part of embedded autonomy did not include much input from labour unions... Labour repression, political marginalization of the working class, was part of the model." Capture of the resulting surplus is split cleanly: "the state dominated in state–capital relations, and the state helped capital to dominate in capital–labour relations."

**[wiki synthesis]** **This is a direct tension with the political half of AJR's extractive/inclusive scheme, and it is not merely a difference of weighting.** AJR code narrow, unconstrained executive power as extractive and predict it suppresses broad-based investment; Wade's cases had narrow, unconstrained executive power and the fastest sustained non-Western growth on record. Two honest readings are available and this wiki does not have grounds to choose between them:

- **Scope condition.** Constraint on the executive matters for the *long-run* institutional equilibrium AJR measure at 1985–95, while a disciplined developmental bureaucracy can substitute for it over a several-decade catch-up window. AJR's own data would then be measuring the outcome of the democratisations that followed the growth, not its cause — which is precisely Glaeser et al.'s sequencing claim at [[colonial-origins-and-the-settler-mortality-instrument]].
- **Genuine counter-evidence.** The constraint axis does not measure what secures investment, and what does is credible conditional discipline, however it is produced.

**[wiki synthesis]** Note two things that stop this being a clean refutation. Wade never cites AJR; his named target throughout is the neoliberal/Washington Consensus account — the World Bank's *East Asian Miracle*, Krueger, Riedel — that attributes East Asian success to "getting the fundamentals right". The overlap with AJR is substantial but it is overlap, not engagement. And his cases are four countries under scope conditions he states himself as non-repeatable. Filed at [[constraint-vs-capacity-as-the-investment-mechanism]].

## The rare institution-level material

**[empirical, case-narrative]** Wade is the only source in this batch that names individual institutions and gives them causal weight:

- **Japan's MITI and MOF** — the "one or a few dominant ministries" concentrating bureaucratic power both horizontally and vertically.
- **Taiwan's central bank** — governor of senior cabinet rank, with covert capital-control capacity built specifically to survive WTO accession commitments.
- **Taiwan's STAG/BOST** (Science and Technology Advisory Group; Board of Science and Technology) and **ITRI/ERSO** (Industrial Technology Research Institute, ~10,000 staff, with a ~20,000-staff secret military counterpart by the mid-1980s) as R&D-steering bodies.
- **Korea's Saemaul Undong** — a parallel hierarchy for village-level incorporation and patronage.

**[wiki synthesis]** **These are comparative-historical narrative and are never built into an identified estimate.** Lane's quasi-experimental result — the strongest evidence on this page — is at *sector* level, not agency level: it compares targeted against non-targeted industries, not MITI against some other ministry. So even here, where individual institutions are named and credited, nothing measures an individual institution's effect on production. Wade also does not detail how these bureaucracies selected their staff beyond "technocrats", and gives no account of internal removal or accountability. Notably absent: Korea's Economic Planning Board, the canonical case elsewhere in this literature, appears nowhere in this source.

## Institutional evolution: a zig-zag, not a decay curve

**[empirical, second-hand]** The one explicit change-over-time claim: national innovation-policy bureaucracies in Korea, Taiwan and Japan followed a **zig-zag rather than a monotonic trajectory** — centralised top-down developmental control, then decentralised "peripheral islands" of innovation agencies borrowing US and UK approaches, then back to renewed central control (citing Karo, forthcoming 2018). Wade presents this as adaptive evolution driven by changing objectives and government turnover.

**[wiki synthesis]** Worth carrying to [[decay-as-real-vs-decay-as-overstated]] as a case of **cyclical reorganisation**, which neither position in that conflict predicts: not monotonic ossification and not crisis-punctuated rupture, but oscillation between centralised and distributed forms in the same national bureaucracies within a few decades. It is second-hand and undated in detail, so it is a datapoint, not evidence. Note ITRI's ~10,000 headcount is given descriptively; Wade never uses scale as a variable.

## The reflexive case: an institution overriding its own evaluation function

**[empirical, case-narrative]** Wade's second power example is the World Bank itself: a 1991 internal report favourable to industrial policy was suppressed by Bank management and released only after the Japanese Executive Director insisted. He reads this as revealing "the distribution of power in its governance."

**[wiki synthesis]** Wade never uses the word capture, but this is a clean instance of an institution's nominal research and evaluation function being overridden by its actual power structure — the same shape as goal displacement and capture-of-the-evaluator elsewhere in this wiki, arriving from a source with no stake in that vocabulary. One case, reported by an involved and committed observer.

## Levers, and their evidence base

**[empirical, case-illustrative]** Performance-conditioned assistance withdrawn on failure to close price and quality gaps; "buffering not insulating" — time-limited protection; export/import complementarity via duty-drawback schemes; hard bargaining with inbound MNCs for local content and skill transfer (his natural comparison is Dow Chemical's ethylene plants across Korea, Chile and Hong Kong, with Korea's harder bargaining correlated with rising national-employment ratios in successive plants); capital-account management against financialisation; exchange-rate management to offset commodity-export overvaluation; sustained R&D investment beyond imitation.

**[wiki synthesis]** **Every one of these is case-illustrative, drawn from Wade's own earlier fieldwork-based books, and none is regression- or experiment-tested in this source.** They belong in tier (ii) of [[reform-levers]] at best — plausible, case-illustrated only. The scope conditions are severe and Wade states them himself: four countries (Hong Kong explicitly bracketed as a colonial special case), roughly 1950s–2010s, under Cold War conditions he says are not repeatable, and he warns that middle-income countries today face financialisation, global-value-chain entry barriers, IP monopoly and asymmetric adjustment pressure that his cases did not.

## Level of analysis

**[wiki synthesis]** National economy and state apparatus, with the agency-level naming above as narrative colour rather than measurement. Every institution discussed is part of the state or organised capital inside a state-directed relationship; the source is silent on whether any of it generalises to private institutions, and the one public/public comparison (Bank of England against developmental ministries) is used for a different purpose. Record the silence as a silence.
