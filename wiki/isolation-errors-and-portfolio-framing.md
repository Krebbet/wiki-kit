# Isolation Errors and Portfolio Framing

Kahneman & Lovallo's claim is that decision-makers evaluate risky choices **one at a time** — narrow framing, or isolation — and that this, combined with loss aversion, makes them reject gambles they would accept as part of a portfolio. The paper's own numerical model prices the error: three independent gambles worth $300 evaluated separately are worth $433 pooled, a 27% mispricing; a portfolio of one large and ten small gambles priced at $600 in isolation is worth $934 pooled. The timidity is real and formally demonstrated. But the paper's title has two halves, and a page that kept only the first would misrepresent it: the same decision-makers are shown, with comparably good evidence, to make **bold forecasts** — systematically over-optimistic estimates of the odds of success. The two biases push in opposite directions and neither the authors nor anyone since has established which dominates. Hence the authors' own explicit disclaimer, which this wiki treats as binding on any use of the source: **"we do not claim that an objective observer would describe managerial decisions as generally risk averse."**

**Evidence tier note.** Mixed, and the pieces are not equally strong. (b) **Formal model** — the aggregation calculation is the paper's own contribution and the strongest thing in it, internally valid by construction and never measured against a real firm. (a) **Cited experiments** — Tversky & Kahneman (1986) on dominated combinations under isolation; Samuelson (1963) single-versus-multiple-play, replicated by Redelmeier & Tversky (1992); Swalm (1966) on executives with planning horizons from $50K to $24M; and, the one result that speaks directly to organisational accountability, Tetlock & Boettger (1991): decision-makers become more risk averse when they expect their choices to be reviewed by others. (a) **Organisational outcome data**, all of it evidencing the *optimism* half — Rand's pioneer process plants (construction cost routinely >2× initial estimate), PIMS (>80% of start-ups fell short of planned market share), Roll (1986) on acquirers not earning positive returns, Cooper et al. (1988) (>80% of entrepreneurs judged their own odds ≥70% against a ~33% five-year survival rate). **Weakest: the entire transfer from individual lab choice to organisational behaviour is argued, not measured.** The authors say so: "Extending these ideas to business decisions is necessarily speculative."

## The formal result

**[model]** Two premises, one conclusion.

- **Loss aversion.** Losses are weighted roughly 2–2.5× equivalent gains. The authors name this "perhaps the most important cause" of the timidity, and its immediate effect is a bias toward inaction and the status quo.
- **Narrow framing / isolation.** Each choice is evaluated as if it were the last, ignoring both concurrent decisions and the stream of similar future opportunities.

Together these make risk attitudes *locally* sensible and *globally* incoherent. Swalm's finding is the diagnostic: executives applied near the same **proportional** risk aversion to small stakes as to large ones relative to their own resources. That is irrational twice over — small gambles raise no ruin question, and small gambles are exactly the ones pooling would help most. Note carefully what the headline scale finding is: an *absence* of scaling in relative risk attitude across decision sizes. It is the puzzle the paper sets out to explain, not a finding that bigger organisations are more cautious.

A third contributor, the **certainty effect** (sure outcomes overweighted relative to merely high-probability ones), is asserted rather than re-tested here.

## The other half: bold forecasts

**[a mix of (a) organisational data and (b) assertion]** The paper names organisational mechanisms that *suppress* caution:

- **Illusion of control** — managers believe skill overcomes objective risk (March & Shapira 1987; Shapira 1986), and reflexively contest externally-supplied risk estimates.
- **Groupthink** (Janis 1982) — pessimists are shunned, bad news is filtered out, so organisational review does not correct individual optimism and may amplify it by mutual validation.
- **Winner's-curse project selection** — where projects compete for internal funding on their forecasts, the *surviving* projects are by regression to the mean the most over-optimistic ones. This is a structural selection mechanism, not a cognitive one, and it produces excess boldness.
- **Forecasts as targets and commitments** — the official number doubles as a goal, creating an incentive to adopt and circulate optimistic figures, then "satisficing indolence" once the target is met.
- **Hubris in acquisitions** (Roll 1986) — acquirers overpay by overestimating synergies and underestimating incumbent management.

**[wiki synthesis]** Groupthink and winner's-curse selection matter to this wiki beyond this page. They are *organisational process* mechanisms that make behaviour bolder, sitting inside the same settings that the veto-point and red-tape literatures treat as brakes. Any framing that reads organisational process as uniformly a brake on risk-taking is contradicted by evidence from within this batch.

## The organisational extensions, and their status

**[model — the authors' own extrapolations, explicitly labelled as such]**

- **Review cadence.** Where outcomes are assessed at predictable checkpoints against value since the last review, the timing of review changes what gets accepted: the probability of an unfavourable review at the next checkpoint falls from 0.50 to 0.25 if evaluation is deferred across two plays rather than one. This is the paper's one genuinely actionable institutional-design variable, and it is derived, not measured. Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D46.
- **Hierarchy-depth compounding.** Subordinates make smaller, more numerous decisions; supervisors make larger, rarer ones; the fully-pooled portfolio is larger still. If each layer narrow-frames independently, aggregate organisational risk aversion could rise with the number of layers. **This is the closest thing in the batch to a size mechanism — and it is the authors' own untested extrapolation from a toy model, not a result.** Do not upgrade it.
- **Recurrence recognition.** Organisations impose statistical discipline on decision types they have *classified* as recurrent, and fail to on decisions that could be seen as recurrent but present as unique. The classification, not the underlying frequency, is what does the work.
- **Hierarchical position sets frame size.** "Many decisions are both unique and large at the level at which they are initially made" even where, from the top of the firm, they are moderate and routine. Effective frame size is a function of position, independent of organisational size.
- **Domain base rate.** Industries with inherently low per-attempt success rates (oil exploration, pharma R&D) are *hypothesised* to have institutionalised portfolio-level evaluation. Footnoted to Thaler as "a plausible hypothesis"; not demonstrated.

## Levers, and the dilemma the authors refuse to resolve

**[model / assertion — none tested here]** Aggregate concurrent decisions and evaluate outcomes in terms of final wealth rather than decision-by-decision gains and losses. Lengthen the interval between performance evaluations so more decisions pool before review. Have executives deliberately push subordinates to a *higher* risk tolerance than the executive's own comfort level, to offset layer-by-layer compounding — flagged by the authors as counterintuitive and likely to be resisted by executives who treat risk-bearing as a senior prerogative. Use outside-view / reference-class forecasting against the optimism side; Arnold's (1986) lender's "staying power analysis" is cited as an existing instrument.

The warning attached is the important part. Correcting only the timidity — encouraging more risk-taking — without correcting the optimism could make outcomes worse, because the caution was accidentally offsetting bad odds. The authors call this "a genuine dilemma that will not yield to any simple rule" and leave it there.

## What this paper is not evidence for

**[wiki synthesis — gaps]**

- **No institutional size variable, and no age variable.** The paper's "size" is the size of the *decision* relative to the decision-maker's resources, never the size of the institution making it. Nothing here supports a size claim.
- **Private-sector evidence only, and the public case is untouched.** Corporate executives, entrepreneurs, capital investment, M&A, industrial process plants. The single gesture at a public body is a passing citation of Landau & Chisholm (1990) and Starbuck & Milliken (1988) on Challenger, used to illustrate vigilance eroding after repeated success, not developed. The cited evidence base is built on people with equity stakes, market returns and board review; whether the mechanism survives where none of those exist is untested either way. See [[open-questions]] Q3, Q31.
- **The scope conditions are narrow and stated.** Decisions of *intermediate* size — big enough to matter, not potentially fatal; decisions *not* already treated as routinely repeated; conditions "essentially favorable", explicitly excluding escalation-of-commitment and sunk-cost dynamics, which the authors hand off to Staw & Ross (1989). The result is scoped to 1990s US corporate capital-allocation decisions of moderate, non-routine size.
- **It is not an institutions paper.** No mandates, no selection mechanisms, no accountability regimes, no structural mechanism of any kind. Veto points, procedural accretion and red tape are outside its scope entirely and are not mentioned.
