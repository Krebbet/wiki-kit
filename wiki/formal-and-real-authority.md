# Formal and Real Authority

Aghion & Tirole separate **formal authority** — the right to decide, conferred by contract, charter, custom, law, or (following Grossman-Hart) by ownership of an asset — from **real authority**, effective control over what actually gets decided. The gap between them is located entirely in the structure of information. Both parties start uninformed about which of *n* candidate projects is best; each can pay a cost to become informed. If the formal-authority holder is uninformed and her subordinate is, she faces a binary choice — investigate at a cost, or **rubber-stamp** the proposal — and when the subordinate is much better informed and their objectives are not too antinomic, rubber-stamping is optimal. At that moment the subordinate holds real authority while holding no formal authority at all. From that one mechanism the paper derives comparative statics for everything that widens or narrows the gap: span of control, delegation, urgency, reputation, performance measurement, and the number of principals. Its sharpest and least intuitive result is that **it is always optimal for a firm to operate in a state of overload** — because genuine overload is the only *credible* commitment a principal can make not to stifle a subordinate's initiative. The paper is explicitly domain-general: its very first illustration is that "the president of a country really controls only a small number of the decisions made by the executive branch."

**Evidence tier note.** **(b) a formal model**, self-consciously so: an incomplete-contracting game with derived propositions and comparative statics, **zero data, zero regressions, zero experiments**. Section V.F, "Testing the Determinants of Real Authority," is the authors *proposing* an empirical programme, not reporting one — "While we all have personal experience with some determinants of real authority, it is harder to come up with more systematic evidence." Real-world material (Du Pont and GM via Chandler, GE's 1980s dual top management, Wal-Mart, post-CERCLA divestiture) is used to motivate assumptions and interpret predictions and is **not** tested against the model; the authors label these "offers a rationale for" and "suggests." They are unusually disciplined about their own evidence, calling a cited supporting study "some evidence, although weak." **(c) wiki synthesis** is marked inline.

## The mechanism

**[model]** Definitions, the source's own:

- **Formal authority** — "the right to decide." The holder "can always reverse her subordinate's decision."
- **Real authority** — "effective control over decisions": who actually determines the outcome regardless of who holds the contractual right.

The agent exerts costly effort *e* to become informed (probability *e* of full information); the principal exerts *E*. Congruence parameters α and β capture how far each party's preferred project serves the other. An **uninformed principal holding formal authority optimally rubber-stamps** rather than risk picking a worse alternative — "she will refrain from [overruling] if the subordinate is much better informed and if their objectives are not too antinomic." The paper states this as a direct formalisation of Weber's observation that bureaucratic staff who own none of the means of production nonetheless exert substantial control over the bureaucratic machinery.

**"Rubber-stamping" is the paper's own term for nominal-versus-real accountability and should be the wiki's anchor word for it.**

## What moves real authority, with the derived direction

**[model]** Each is a comparative static from the model; none is tested.

| Driver | Effect on the agent's real authority | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Information asymmetry** | ↑ | The root cause. Formal authority is "entirely ineffective" precisely when the principal is uninformed and the agent is |
| **Overload / span of control** | ↑ | A higher marginal cost of gathering information lowers the principal's equilibrium probability of becoming informed (E falls) |
| **Delegating formal authority outright** | ↑ | With formal authority moved to the agent, E^d < E and e^d > e: the agent's initiative strictly rises, the principal's strictly falls, "because the principal cannot overrule the agent" — at the cost of the principal losing *both* formal and real control |
| **Urgency** | ↑ | Short decision horizons force cursory investigation; optimal investigation time S rises in horizon length T and falls in congruence α |
| **Reputation (repeated play)** | ↑ on small matters | A principal can credibly commit to overruling only when the loss is large, without a one-shot temptation to renege |
| **Better performance measurement** | ↑ | Two channels: it raises the agent's incentive to propose the principal's preferred project, **and** it lowers the principal's incentive to monitor at all, since she can rely on the wage contract instead |
| **Splitting *returns* among several principals** | ↑ | Free-riding on monitoring |
| **Splitting *authority itself* among principals** | ambiguous | Depends on the governance mechanism — veto power against majority voting; an agent can sometimes play principals against each other |

Net framing, stated by the authors as their central trade-off: "An increase in an agent's real authority promotes initiative but results in a loss of control for the principal."

**[wiki synthesis]** The performance-measurement row is worth pausing on because it points the opposite way from intuition: improving measurement *reduces* monitoring and thereby *increases* the agent's real authority. Read alongside [[multitask-incentive-theory]], where measurability determines how strong an incentive can be, this gives measurement two distinct organisational consequences from two different models — contract slope there, authority allocation here.

## Optimal overload

**[model]** The paper's sharpest result, Section V.A. With *m* identical subordinates, define a firm to be in **overload** when the marginal profit of one more subordinate, *holding agent behaviour fixed*, is negative. Equation 17 shows it is **always optimal for the firm to be in that state**. The reason is a commitment problem, and footnote 22 states it plainly: **"Overload is a credible commitment not to stifle initiative."** A principal who merely *chooses* not to supervise — "playing golf" — is not credible, because she can always change her mind. A principal who is genuinely overloaded cannot.

**A growth-rate result, distinct from a size result** (the Section V.A remark). Fast growth — hiring subordinates quickly — raises overload costs and causes the principal to lose control. Slow growth lets the principal accumulate **monitoring experience over time** ("learning by doing in monitoring"), which raises her effective oversight and therefore **stifles subordinate initiative more as the organisation ages**. Holding final headcount constant, the growth *path* determines how much real authority migrates. Elsewhere the same logic appears informally: the principal "keeps decision rights for activities she knows very well from experience, namely the 'core competencies'."

**[wiki synthesis]** This is a formally derived instance of "behaviour changes with years elapsed," and it is the first one in this wiki that is neither rule accretion nor goal displacement. It runs on the *principal's accumulated skill*, not on the institution's ossification, and it predicts **centralisation increasing with age** — the opposite sign from the delegation-with-maturity story. It is a model with no data, so it does not move [[open-questions]] Q60; it changes what the age question could be about. Registered against Q112.

## The framing tension with overload-as-bloat

**[wiki synthesis]** Aghion & Tirole's reading of overload is the reverse of the one carried at [[bureaucratic-growth-and-parkinsons-law]], where headcount and scope growth outrunning workload is the *explanandum* — Parkinson's assertion-only bloat, Niskanen's budget maximisation, Downs's climber ambition. Here the same observable is an **optimal design feature**: the marginal employee whose direct contribution is negative is buying a commitment the principal cannot otherwise make.

This is **not filed as a conflict**, and the reason is that no source on either side measures anything. It is a framing tension between a formal model with no data and three growth accounts with, respectively, two restated historical series, invented numbers, and nothing at all. There is no shared measured quantity to disagree about. What it does supply is a **second reading of the same observation** that any future test of bureau overstaffing would have to rule out — a diagnosis that finds a negative marginal product and stops has not distinguished bloat from commitment. Recorded on both pages.

## Measurement: proposed, not built

**[model]** Section V.F is the authors' own list of candidate instruments for measuring real authority, and they flag it as "the key issue" while admitting the existing evidence is "personal experience." In full:

- **Organisational proxies**: span of control, concentration of ownership, number of principals, number of supervising layers.
- **Questionnaires** (design unspecified).
- **Ex post examination of the nature of decisions taken** — whether a decision appeals to the lower-level staff who recommended it rather than to management; who benefits from what was actually decided.
- **Counting how often agents are overruled** — with the authors' own caveat that an agent who knows his preferred project will be rejected may not propose it at all, biasing a naive overrule count *downward* as a measure of principal control.
- **"Find out who gets courted in the organization"** — an informal network/influence proxy.
- **Indirect evidence from legal doctrine**: vicarious-liability rulings read as courts' revealed assessments of real authority, with directors, parent companies and creditors held more liable when spread across fewer activities or subsidiaries (cited to Strasser & Rodosevich 1993, not authored by Aghion & Tirole). Their own normative extension: liability rules loading responsibility onto integrated principals may perversely induce excessive divestiture.

All are flagged by the authors as requiring "much more careful attention than can be given here." **This is why [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D112 is registered `candidate` and explicitly not scoreable**: the axis is named and its drivers derived, but the instrument does not exist. Building one is the work.

## The public/private claim is explicit, not silent

**[model]** Unusually for this literature, generality is asserted directly rather than left implicit. The paper's opening illustration is political — "the president of a country really controls only a small number of the decisions made by the executive branch" — set alongside the shareholders/board/executives case as an instance of the *same* gap. The list of worked hierarchy types is "board of directors/management, chief executive officer/division manager, thesis advisor/student, supervisor/worker, or **supranational authority/country**." The contingent-delegation mechanism is illustrated with a president whose authority is delegated but revocable via impeachment, treated as formally identical to a board losing control after a takeover — the same cost *c* of reclaiming a delegated right.

**[wiki synthesis]** This is a stronger public/private invariance claim than most sources in this wiki make, and it is a claim about the *model's* scope, not evidence of transfer. Nothing here has been run on a bureau. But it means the source cannot be dismissed as firm-specific, and it makes the rubber-stamping mechanism the most direct mechanism-level answer this wiki holds to [[open-questions]] Q11 — who an institution is *actually* accountable to, as distinct from nominally.

## Failure modes named

**[model]** In the source's terms: **loss of control** (the principal's cost of delegating or of being overloaded); **overload**, reframed as optimal; **rubber-stamping**; **free-riding** among multiple principals sharing returns; and, in passing and unmodelled, large 1960s conglomerates becoming "bureaucratized" with headquarters "responsible for too many units, whose strategy they could not understand or influence."

## Levers

**[model]** All derived, none tested:

1. **Allocate formal authority by the participation rule** (eq. 9): delegate decisions that matter little to the principal, on which the agent is trusted, and that matter a lot to the agent.
2. **Raise real authority under formal centralisation** via any of the six drivers in the table — deliberate overload, urgency, a reputation for restrained intervention, better performance measurement, or splitting authority among imperfectly aligned principals.
3. **Contingent delegation** — cheap to grant, costly to reverse — as an intermediate design between full centralisation and full delegation.
4. **Delegate sensitive people-decisions** (layoffs, discipline, restructuring) to an intermediary whose objectives sit between principal's and agent's, limiting the agent's expected cost of losing formal rights and so preserving initiative.
5. **Liability-rule design**: courts should weight organisational characteristics (span, number of subsidiaries) when assigning vicarious liability, since these proxy for real authority. A genuine legal-design lever, and it is sourced to legal-literature citations the authors did not produce.
