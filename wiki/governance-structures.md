# Governance Structures

Williamson's transaction cost economics (TCE) makes the *transaction* the unit of analysis and asks which governance structure — market, hybrid, or hierarchy — will be selected to carry it. Transactions vary along three attributes (asset specificity, uncertainty/disturbance, frequency); governance structures vary along three discrete attributes (incentive intensity, administrative command and control, contract law regime); the **discriminating alignment hypothesis** holds that transactions are matched to governance structures "so as to effect a (mainly) transaction cost economizing outcome." The framework yields two results this wiki needs beyond the make-or-buy question: a specific mechanism for why firms cannot grow without limit (fiat cannot credibly bind itself, and bureaucratic politics worsens with size), and the *remediableness* criterion, an explicit argument that efficiency analysis must be applied symmetrically to public and private organisations rather than treating government as frictionless.

**Evidence tier note.** This page is (b) theoretical framework throughout. Williamson's Nobel lecture contains no data, no regression tables, and no methodological detail. It asserts a supporting empirical literature — "over 800" empirical tests by 2006, characterised as "broadly corroborative" and cited to meta-analyses (Macher & Richman 2008; Geyskens, Steenkamp & Kumar 2006) — but the wiki has not read those meta-analyses and cannot vouch for them. The lecture's own illustrations are two single cases (the Schwinn antitrust case; Boeing's 787 outsourcing and its acquisition of Vought Aircraft). The selective-intervention and scaling-up arguments are established by logical demonstration, not data.

## Attributes of the transaction

**[framework]**

- **Asset specificity** — the central variable. Named sub-types: physical, human, site-specific, dedicated, brand-name capital, and episodic/temporal. Williamson treats it as a *design variable*, not a given: a good or service can be redesigned to be less specific, at a cost in performance (Riordan & Williamson 1985).
- **Uncertainty / disturbance** — specifically "outlier disturbances for which unprogrammed adaptations are needed." Disturbance exposure rises with contract length, so time horizon enters through this channel.
- **Frequency** — named as the third canonical dimension but not elaborated in this lecture.

Of the three, Williamson says asset specificity and outlier disturbances are "most important."

**Bilateral dependency** is derived, not primitive. It emerges when asset specificity converts a large-numbers bidding situation into a small-numbers exchange after the fact — the **fundamental transformation**. Once parties are locked in, the hazard is *maladaptation*: costly failure to adapt cooperatively when a disturbance hits, because each side can hold up the other.

## Attributes of the governance structure

**[framework]** Three binary attributes, which Williamson treats as a *syndrome* — internally consistent combinations, not free-floating dials:

| Attribute | Market | Hierarchy |
|---|---|---|
| Incentive intensity | Strong (autonomous residual claimancy) | Weak (cost-plus) |
| Administrative command and control (fiat) | Weak | Strong (unified ownership, a common "boss") |
| Contract law regime | Strong — court-enforced legal rules | Weak — private ordering and forbearance; "hierarchy is its own court of ultimate appeal" |

The contract-law axis is the genuinely novel one: contract law is plural, not singular, and the choice of legal regime is a design parameter (following Llewellyn's "contract as framework", 1931). Courts decline to hear intrafirm disputes, which is exactly what makes internalisation a *different* institution rather than a relabelled market.

**Alignment rule.** Generic transactions with ascertainable, autonomously implementable adaptation needs go to the market. Transactions with significant transaction-specific investment, contractual incompleteness (bounded rationality), and exposure to disturbances that invite opportunistic defection go to hierarchy, whose fiat and forbearance regime resolve disputes without renegotiation breakdown. **Hybrid** is named as a third mode but is not dimensionalised in the same binary scheme in this lecture.

**Behavioural assumptions.** Bounded rationality (from Simon) generates contractual incompleteness. **Opportunism** — "self-interest seeking with guile" — is the strategic-defection condition, and Williamson distinguishes it from mere "frailty of motive," which is benign, non-strategic slippage.

## Why firms cannot grow without limit

**[framework — load-bearing for institutional lifecycle]** Section 4.1 of the lecture, "selective intervention," addresses Coase's and Knight's puzzle: why can't a large firm do everything a collection of smaller firms can do, and more? The hypothetical mechanism would be *replication* (acquire units and run them exactly as before) plus *selective intervention* (intervene only where it creates net value). Williamson's answer is that this requires four promises that are **not self-enforcing** absent costless three-way arbitration:

1. preserve high-powered incentives in the acquired unit;
2. use the acquired assets with due care;
3. exercise fiat only where there is genuine net benefit;
4. divide the resulting gains truthfully.

Each fails for a specific reason. The owner controls the accounting system and can manipulate transfer prices, depreciation schedules, and claims about which state of the world was realised, shifting net receipts to its own advantage. Failures of due care are hard to prove. Gain-division is contestable with no neutral adjudicator, because the firm is its own court of ultimate appeal. And Williamson adds a fifth point directly: "the political game is now played in a larger firm that is more susceptible to bureaucratic ploys and political positioning than in smaller firms."

**[wiki synthesis]** The structure of this argument is worth isolating because it generalises past firms: *the very property that gives hierarchy its advantage over markets — unreviewable fiat — is what makes hierarchy self-defeating at scale, because fiat cannot credibly bind itself.* That is the same shape as the ruler-shackling problem in [[credible-commitment]]: the party with authority cannot promise not to use it. Williamson makes no explicit claim about age, headcount thresholds, or hierarchy-layer counts — the argument is structural and incentive-based, not empirical or quantitative, and nothing in this source tells you at what size the effect bites.

## Levers

**[framework]** Four design levers the lecture argues for:

1. **Redesign the good or service to reduce asset specificity** — explicit trade-off: less specificity may cost performance.
2. **Credible commitments** — consciously agreed assurance mechanisms that expand the range of transactions sustainable *without* full ownership integration: information disclosure and auditing, specialised private dispute-settling bodies, and "hostages" (Williamson 1983). Illustration: the Nevada Power / Northwest Trading 32-year coal contract.
3. **Choice of contract law regime** — court-enforced legal rules versus private ordering, as a deliberate parameter rather than an environmental given.
4. **Remediableness** — see below.

## Remediableness and the public/private symmetry claim

**[framework]** Section 4.2 is Williamson's most direct contribution to this wiki's public/private invariance question. His charge: standard policy analysis applied zero-transaction-cost reasoning *asymmetrically*. Private-sector shortfalls were analysed as "market failures" arising from positive transaction costs, but "there was no corresponding concept for public sector failures" — an implicit assumption that public-sector transaction costs are zero, which he calls "nonsense."

The **remediableness criterion**: an extant mode of organisation is presumed efficient unless a *feasible and implementable* superior alternative can be shown, with net implementation costs included in the comparison. Williamson states it applies identically "in any sector whatsoever – public, private, nonprofit, etc.", and describes it as dealing "symmetrically with real world institutions, both public and private, warts and all." He names public bureaus as an underused application domain, citing the post-9/11 design of the Department of Homeland Security as a case of political-purpose-driven rather than efficiency-driven bureau design (Cohen, Cuellar & Weingast 2006).

**[wiki synthesis]** Two things to keep separate. The claim that transaction-cost reasoning *should* apply symmetrically across sectors is methodological and hard to argue with. The claim that discriminating alignment *does* predict public-sector organisational form is empirical and, by Williamson's own account, undeveloped — he flags the public-bureau application as aspirational. The symmetry argument also has a sharp edge that cuts against Williamson's own framework: if extant arrangements are presumed efficient until a feasible alternative is demonstrated, the framework is close to unfalsifiable in exactly the way North objects to. See [[efficiency-of-institutions-north-vs-williamson]].

**Self-flagged gap.** Williamson concedes TCE has not addressed "systems complications" among related transactions, using the Boeing 787 outsourcing failure — malcoordination across a set of interdependent outsourced transactions, each individually alignable — as an open problem.
