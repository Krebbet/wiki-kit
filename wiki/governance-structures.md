# Governance Structures

Williamson's transaction cost economics (TCE) makes the *transaction* the unit of analysis and asks which governance structure — market, hybrid, or hierarchy — will be selected to carry it. Transactions vary along three attributes (asset specificity, uncertainty/disturbance, frequency); governance structures vary along three discrete attributes (incentive intensity, administrative command and control, contract law regime); the **discriminating alignment hypothesis** holds that transactions are matched to governance structures "so as to effect a (mainly) transaction cost economizing outcome." The question is Coase's, and this page now carries his original answer alongside Williamson's: firm size is set at the margin where the cost of organising one more transaction internally equals the cost of doing it through the market or through another firm. It also carries the only panel evidence in the wiki on what firm hierarchies actually look like and how they moved (Rajan & Wulf). The framework yields two results this wiki needs beyond the make-or-buy question: a specific mechanism for why firms cannot grow without limit (fiat cannot credibly bind itself, and bureaucratic politics worsens with size), and the *remediableness* criterion, an explicit argument that efficiency analysis must be applied symmetrically to public and private organisations rather than treating government as frictionless.

**Evidence tier note.** The Williamson and Coase material on this page is (b) theoretical framework throughout; the Rajan & Wulf section is (a) empirical — a fixed-effects panel of 300+ US firms — and is the only measured content here. Read the tier marks per section. Williamson's Nobel lecture contains no data, no regression tables, and no methodological detail. It asserts a supporting empirical literature — "over 800" empirical tests by 2006, characterised as "broadly corroborative" and cited to meta-analyses (Macher & Richman 2008; Geyskens, Steenkamp & Kumar 2006) — but the wiki has not read those meta-analyses and cannot vouch for them. The lecture's own illustrations are two single cases (the Schwinn antitrust case; Boeing's 787 outsourcing and its acquisition of Vought Aircraft). The selective-intervention and scaling-up arguments are established by logical demonstration, not data.

## Attributes of the transaction

**[framework]**

- **Asset specificity** — the central variable. Named sub-types: physical, human, site-specific, dedicated, brand-name capital, and episodic/temporal. Williamson treats it as a *design variable*, not a given: a good or service can be redesigned to be less specific, at a cost in performance (Riordan & Williamson 1985).
- **Uncertainty / disturbance** — specifically "outlier disturbances for which unprogrammed adaptations are needed." Disturbance exposure rises with contract length, so time horizon enters through this channel.
- **Frequency** — named as the third canonical dimension but not elaborated in this lecture.

Of the three, Williamson says asset specificity and outlier disturbances are "most important."

**Bilateral dependency** is derived, not primitive. It emerges when asset specificity converts a large-numbers bidding situation into a small-numbers exchange after the fact — the **fundamental transformation**. Once parties are locked in, the hazard is *maladaptation*: costly failure to adapt cooperatively when a disturbance hits, because each side can hold up the other.

## Attributes of the governance structure

**[framework]** Three binary attributes, which Williamson treats as a *syndrome* — internally consistent combinations, not free-floating dials:

| Attribute | Market | Hierarchy |
|---|---|---|
| Incentive intensity | Strong (autonomous residual claimancy) | Weak (cost-plus) |
| Administrative command and control (fiat) | Weak | Strong (unified ownership, a common "boss") |
| Contract law regime | Strong — court-enforced legal rules | Weak — private ordering and forbearance; "hierarchy is its own court of ultimate appeal" |

The contract-law axis is the genuinely novel one: contract law is plural, not singular, and the choice of legal regime is a design parameter (following Llewellyn's "contract as framework", 1931). Courts decline to hear intrafirm disputes, which is exactly what makes internalisation a *different* institution rather than a relabelled market.

**Alignment rule.** Generic transactions with ascertainable, autonomously implementable adaptation needs go to the market. Transactions with significant transaction-specific investment, contractual incompleteness (bounded rationality), and exposure to disturbances that invite opportunistic defection go to hierarchy, whose fiat and forbearance regime resolve disputes without renegotiation breakdown. **Hybrid** is named as a third mode but is not dimensionalised in the same binary scheme in this lecture.

**Behavioural assumptions.** Bounded rationality (from Simon) generates contractual incompleteness. **Opportunism** — "self-interest seeking with guile" — is the strategic-defection condition, and Williamson distinguishes it from mere "frailty of motive," which is benign, non-strategic slippage.

## Why firms cannot grow without limit

**[framework — load-bearing for institutional lifecycle]** Section 4.1 of the lecture, "selective intervention," addresses Coase's and Knight's puzzle: why can't a large firm do everything a collection of smaller firms can do, and more? The hypothetical mechanism would be *replication* (acquire units and run them exactly as before) plus *selective intervention* (intervene only where it creates net value). Williamson's answer is that this requires four promises that are **not self-enforcing** absent costless three-way arbitration:

1. preserve high-powered incentives in the acquired unit;
2. use the acquired assets with due care;
3. exercise fiat only where there is genuine net benefit;
4. divide the resulting gains truthfully.

Each fails for a specific reason. The owner controls the accounting system and can manipulate transfer prices, depreciation schedules, and claims about which state of the world was realised, shifting net receipts to its own advantage. Failures of due care are hard to prove. Gain-division is contestable with no neutral adjudicator, because the firm is its own court of ultimate appeal. And Williamson adds a fifth point directly: "the political game is now played in a larger firm that is more susceptible to bureaucratic ploys and political positioning than in smaller firms."

**[wiki synthesis]** The structure of this argument is worth isolating because it generalises past firms: *the very property that gives hierarchy its advantage over markets — unreviewable fiat — is what makes hierarchy self-defeating at scale, because fiat cannot credibly bind itself.* That is the same shape as the ruler-shackling problem in [[credible-commitment]]: the party with authority cannot promise not to use it. Williamson makes no explicit claim about age, headcount thresholds, or hierarchy-layer counts — the argument is structural and incentive-based, not empirical or quantitative, and nothing in this source tells you at what size the effect bites.

## Coase's original limits-to-firm-size argument

**[framework — Coase 1937]** Williamson's selective-intervention argument answers a puzzle Coase posed and answered differently. Coase defines firm size as **the number and scope of transactions organised by one entrepreneur** — not headcount: "a firm becomes larger as additional transactions... are organized by the entrepreneur and becomes smaller as he abandons the organization of such transactions." The equilibrium condition is a margin, not a threshold: the firm expands until "the costs of organizing an extra transaction within the firm become equal to the costs of carrying out the same transaction by means of an exchange on the open market or the costs of organizing in another firm."

Four named diseconomies stop it there:

1. **Decreasing returns to the entrepreneur function** — organising cost rises as transactions are added; Coase's own gloss is "diminishing returns to management."
2. **Rising misallocation** — "the entrepreneur fails to place the factors of production in the uses where their value is greatest," and the mistake rate rises with the number of transactions organised.
3. **Rising factor supply price at larger firms** — because the non-pecuniary advantages of small-firm employment (independence, heading a small business rather than a department) are worth more to some workers and managers than the premium a larger firm can pay. A size-driven adverse-selection effect on who agrees to work inside large organisations.
4. **Spatial and heterogeneity costs** — "the costs of organizing and the losses through mistakes will increase with an increase in the spatial distribution of the transactions organized, in the dissimilarity of the transactions, and in the probability of changes in the relevant prices." Since added transactions trend toward being more dispersed and more dissimilar, this is a scale-driven efficiency decay distinct from anything in the asset-specificity account.

Two exogenous shifters are named and matter for the wiki. **Technology**: improvements in managerial technique or communications (telephone, telegraph) that cut internal organising cost more than they cut market-transaction cost raise optimal firm size, and the reverse shrinks it — so observed firm size is partly a technology reading, not a governance reading. **Regulation**: sales taxes, quota schemes and price controls that apply to market transactions but not to the same transaction organised inside a firm push firms to be **artificially larger** than organising-cost economics alone would produce. And, quoting Knight, "the possibility of monopoly gain offers a powerful incentive to continuous and unlimited expansion of the firm" — absent the diseconomies above, market-power incentives alone would drive unbounded consolidation.

**[framework — Coase 1991]** The Nobel lecture restates the margin condition — "a firm... could only continue to exist if it performed its co-ordination function at a lower cost than would be incurred if it were achieved by means of market transactions and also at a lower cost than this same function could be performed by another firm" — and gives the limiting case: Lenin's proposal to run the Soviet economy "as one big factory," which Western economists held to be impossible even as large Western firms plainly worked. Coase frames his own 1932 puzzle as reconciling the existence of management with the impossibility of economy-wide planning. **No threshold, no mechanism-with-numbers, and no test** is offered for where coordination-by-hierarchy breaks down. It is asserted as a puzzle resolved by theory.

**Scope condition, load-bearing.** Coase's size logic requires a market alternative the entrepreneur can lose transactions to — "it is always possible to revert to the open market if he fails." A bureau with no market alternative fails that condition. Coase's own note 14 treats state planning as categorically different in origin (imposed rather than voluntary) even while granting the structural analogy, so the firm-size argument should not be assumed to transfer to bureau size. That transfer is exactly what [[bureaucratic-growth-and-parkinsons-law]] and [[functional-vs-rent-seeking-growth]] leave open.

## Empirical evidence: span and depth over time

**[empirical — Rajan & Wulf 2003]** The only panel evidence in this wiki on what corporate hierarchies actually look like. 300+ large US public firms, 1986–1998, from a proprietary Hewitt Associates compensation survey; a balanced sub-panel of 51 firms present all thirteen years; firm and year fixed effects with firm-clustered standard errors. Sample means: ~47,500 employees, ~85 years since founding, 19% return on sales — "a large mature stable firm, not one whose organizational structure is likely to be in flux."

| Measure | 1986 | 1998 |
|---|---|---|
| CEO span of control (unbalanced panel, mean) | 4.46 | 6.79 (+50%) |
| CEO span (balanced 51-firm panel, mean) | 4.39 | 8.16 (+86%) |
| Depth — positions between CEO and division head | 1.58 | 1.18 (−25%) |
| Probability a division manager reports directly to the CEO | 0.05 | 0.31 |
| CFOs reporting directly to the CEO | 67% | 90% |

Span and depth are negatively correlated across firms (−0.27): wider organisations are also shorter. Delayering operates partly by **eliminating specific intermediary positions**, not only by reshuffling reporting lines — COO prevalence fell about 20% over the period, and the presence of a COO reduces CEO span by roughly two managers.

**This is not a size effect, and there is a separate size effect.** The paper tests and rejects the reading that flattening is hardwired by firm growth: in the balanced panel average employment *fell*, 86,000 → 74,000; quartiles sorted by employment growth show no pattern in span; dropping firms with significant acquisitions leaves the result intact (4.4 → 8.2); diversification does not predict span. Flattening is a calendar-time trend across firms of stable size. Separately and orthogonally, in the depth regression with firm and year fixed effects the coefficient on log employees is positive — **holding the year fixed, bigger firms still have more layers**. The era trend and the cross-sectional size→depth relationship are two different findings, and the paper keeps them apart.

**[wiki synthesis]** The cross-sectional half is what [[knowledge-hierarchies-and-the-cost-of-scale]] predicts (layer count rising with output), and the era half is what its comparative statics predict when communication and information technology change at fixed size. The two sources are consistent, and this is filed as reconciled rather than as a conflict.

**[empirical — formal authority is not reporting proximity]** The paper's sharpest structural finding: division managers who move closer to the CEO are also given **officer designation** — formal authority — and higher, more incentive-weighted pay, while their units get *smaller* (divisional unit headcount fell ~6,000 → ~4,700, ruling out consolidation into fewer larger divisions). The reading offered is Aghion & Tirole's formal-versus-real authority: a CEO with a wide span credibly commits to *light monitoring* because he has no time to scrutinise everyone, and compensates the subordinate with formal authority and incentive pay instead of supervision. Proximity in the reporting line and delegated authority are separable, and they moved together here for a stated reason — registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D41.

**[empirical — a tested and failed hypothesis]** Rajan & Wulf test the empire-building explanation of hierarchy — unmonitored managers hiring layers of "bureaucrats reporting to bureaucrats" — by regressing depth on institutional shareholding and the Gompers-Ishii-Metrick governance index. They find "little systematic relationship", and depth is uncorrelated with market-to-book. A named failure-mode hypothesis, tested against data and not confirmed. Cite it as such: it is evidence against governance-pressure-driven delayering in this sample, not evidence for any alternative.

**Limits.** US only; large, publicly-traded, industry-leading firms; senior management only, so nothing below the divisional level; 1986–1998. Firm **age is not a covariate** — the sample is old by construction (~85 years), so this source says nothing about how hierarchy changes as a firm ages, and must not be cited as if it did.

## Levers

**[framework]** Four design levers the lecture argues for:

1. **Redesign the good or service to reduce asset specificity** — explicit trade-off: less specificity may cost performance.
2. **Credible commitments** — consciously agreed assurance mechanisms that expand the range of transactions sustainable *without* full ownership integration: information disclosure and auditing, specialised private dispute-settling bodies, and "hostages" (Williamson 1983). Illustration: the Nevada Power / Northwest Trading 32-year coal contract.
3. **Choice of contract law regime** — court-enforced legal rules versus private ordering, as a deliberate parameter rather than an environmental given.
4. **Remediableness** — see below.

## Remediableness and the public/private symmetry claim

**[framework]** Section 4.2 is Williamson's most direct contribution to this wiki's public/private invariance question. His charge: standard policy analysis applied zero-transaction-cost reasoning *asymmetrically*. Private-sector shortfalls were analysed as "market failures" arising from positive transaction costs, but "there was no corresponding concept for public sector failures" — an implicit assumption that public-sector transaction costs are zero, which he calls "nonsense."

The **remediableness criterion**: an extant mode of organisation is presumed efficient unless a *feasible and implementable* superior alternative can be shown, with net implementation costs included in the comparison. Williamson states it applies identically "in any sector whatsoever – public, private, nonprofit, etc.", and describes it as dealing "symmetrically with real world institutions, both public and private, warts and all." He names public bureaus as an underused application domain, citing the post-9/11 design of the Department of Homeland Security as a case of political-purpose-driven rather than efficiency-driven bureau design (Cohen, Cuellar & Weingast 2006).

**[wiki synthesis]** Two things to keep separate. The claim that transaction-cost reasoning *should* apply symmetrically across sectors is methodological and hard to argue with. The claim that discriminating alignment *does* predict public-sector organisational form is empirical and, by Williamson's own account, undeveloped — he flags the public-bureau application as aspirational. The symmetry argument also has a sharp edge that cuts against Williamson's own framework: if extant arrangements are presumed efficient until a feasible alternative is demonstrated, the framework is close to unfalsifiable in exactly the way North objects to. See [[efficiency-of-institutions-north-vs-williamson]].

**Self-flagged gap.** Williamson concedes TCE has not addressed "systems complications" among related transactions, using the Boeing 787 outsourcing failure — malcoordination across a set of interdependent outsourced transactions, each individually alignable — as an open problem.
