# Separation of Ownership and Control

Fama's argument is that the separation of security ownership from control in the large corporation is **efficient rather than pathological**, and that it survives competition without owner monitoring because three markets do the disciplining instead. Portfolio theory makes diversified risk-bearers rationally indifferent to controlling any single firm. The **managerial labour market**, inside and outside the firm, prices managers off past performance signals, so anticipated wage revision substitutes for direct ex post settling up: a manager who shirks today pays in the revaluation of his own human capital. The **board of directors** is a market-induced internal monitor whose insiders police each other because each wants to be boss of bosses, with outside directors added as "professional referees" specifically to suppress the risk that the insiders instead collude against security holders. The outside **takeover market** is discipline of last resort, more expensive than the other two and invoked only when they fail. The paper's most-quoted line is that "ownership of the firm is an irrelevant concept" — security holders hold a residual financial claim and, by virtue of that claim alone, no decision rights. All of this is asserted from theory; Fama says so in his own conclusion. **This page also carries a correction to this wiki's commissioning brief** — see below.

**Evidence tier note.** **(b) a theoretical argument** in the nexus-of-contracts / property-rights tradition, with one embedded formal illustration: a stochastic model (eqs. 1–7) in which a manager's measured marginal product is a random walk plus white noise, used to derive the wage-revision weights under which anticipated future wage changes constitute *precise* full ex post settling up. That model demonstrates that a scenario exists in which the mechanism works exactly; it fits no data. The paper's one gesture at empirical grounding is a self-citation to Fama (1976) claiming capital markets are informationally rational, imported to license the analogy that managerial labour markets might be similarly rational. **No measurement instrument, no coding scheme, no sample, no observation base.** Fama's own closing hedge: "The extent to which the wage revision process imposes ex post settling up in any particular situation is, of course, an empirical issue." **(c) wiki synthesis** is marked inline.

## Correction: the decision-rights taxonomy is not in this paper

**This wiki's own brief for the `theory-of-the-firm` research job stated that Fama (1980) contains the decomposition of decision rights into initiation / ratification / implementation / monitoring — decision *management* against decision *control*. It does not.** The ingest checked the text specifically: neither "ratification" nor "initiation" occurs anywhere in it. That vocabulary belongs to the companion paper **Fama & Jensen, "Separation of Ownership and Control", *Journal of Law and Economics* 26 (1983)** — a different source, which **this wiki has not captured**.

Two consequences, both binding:

1. **The taxonomy is not imported here.** No version of it appears on this page or anywhere else in the wiki as a result of this batch. It is well known and the temptation to write it from memory is exactly what this note exists to block. If the wiki wants it, the 1983 paper has to be captured and read.
2. **What this paper actually contains is a coarser two-way split** — **management** (decision-making: coordinating inputs and executing the contracts among them, held by managers) against **risk-bearing** (the residual financial claim, held by diffuse security holders) — plus an informal, unlabelled notion of "control" meaning *disciplining* management, which is exercised jointly by the labour market and the board and is nowhere split into ratification against monitoring.

Read this paper as the **precursor argument** — why separation is efficient, via labour-market discipline — not as the source of the canonical taxonomy. Fama & Jensen (1983) is now on the acquisition list at [[open-questions]] Q113, alongside Weber and Wilson (1989).

## The three disciplining markets

**[model]** Ranked by Fama from cheapest and most-used to most expensive and last-resort:

| Channel | Mechanism | Fama's status for it |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal managerial labour market** | Lower managers benefit from displacing a shirking superior, so monitoring runs **bottom-up as well as top-down** | Asserted; "probably has good survival properties" |
| **External managerial labour market** | The manager's human capital is revalued on performance signals fed partly by the capital market; anticipated wage revision substitutes for ex post settling up | The formal model (eqs. 1–7) shows a case where this is exact |
| **Outside takeover market** | Bidder replaces management wholesale | Explicitly the **most expensive** channel, "discipline of last resort" |

**The board.** Fama's description is functional, not legal: the board is "the ultimate internal monitor of the set of contracts called a firm, whose most important role is to scrutinize the highest decision makers," and it possesses "a relatively low-cost mechanism for replacing or reordering top managers" — cheaper than a takeover. Its composition is derived from self-interest rather than virtue: the top managers on it are competing with each other for the top slot, so each has a private incentive to police the others. **Outside directors exist to close the failure mode that creates**: once the top-management group controls the board, it may collude to expropriate security-holder wealth instead, and outside directors are added as referees against precisely that. They are themselves disciplined by their own reputational labour market.

**[wiki synthesis]** The board-capture scenario Fama names is a within-firm instance of the wiki's **capture** vocabulary — a monitoring body captured by the agents it exists to monitor — arriving from a source with no relationship to the regulatory-capture literature at [[regulatory-capture]]. It is not goal displacement or ossification; there is no rule-fetishism content anywhere in this paper.

## "Ownership of the firm is an irrelevant concept" — what it does and does not mean

**[wiki synthesis]** This line is a standing trap, and the wiki has deliberately **not** filed it as a conflict with [[property-rights-theory-of-the-firm]]'s "ownership *is* the residual right of control." The two use "ownership" for different objects:

- **Fama's ownership** = ownership of the residual *financial* claim (equity), held by diffuse security holders **within a single already-constituted firm**. His claim is that holding it confers no decision rights: "control over a firm's decisions is not necessarily the province of security holders."
- **Grossman & Hart's ownership** = ownership of a physical, non-human *asset*, which confers residual *control rights* over that asset and hence bargaining leverage **across a firm boundary**.

Different objects sharing a word, exactly as with "residual claimant" against "residual control rights" at [[team-production-and-monitoring]]. No same-object contradiction can be constructed from the text of either source, so none is filed. This paper takes **no position at all** on where the firm's boundary falls — it contains no asset specificity, no make-versus-buy, no integration decision — and must not be routed into [[rival-firm-boundary-theories]].

## Scale, sector, and the one cross-sector claim

**[model]**

- **No headcount, budget or founding-age variable anywhere.** Scale nonetheless does structural work: the whole argument is scoped to firms whose ownership is diffuse enough that no individual holder has a stake worth personally monitoring, and Fama's own baseline contrast is the sole owner-manager, who has no incentive problem because he settles up with himself. So the paper implies — without measuring — that the need for market-based discipline is a function of **ownership diffuseness**, which correlates with but is not identical to size. Nothing about how the disciplining mechanisms change as a firm ages.
- **One explicit public/private claim, and it is narrow.** Footnote 5: institutions "like governments, schools, and universities" can forecast future managerial needs more reliably than corporations facing rapid technological change, and so "may be able to offer long-term contracts at relatively low cost," attracting more risk-averse professionals, while "the riskier employment offered by corporations attracts those who are willing to accept shorter-term contracts." A genuine contract-horizon sorting claim by sector, and the **only** place non-corporate institutions appear. **[wiki synthesis]** Note that it is a *selection* claim — the contract on offer determines who takes the job — which makes it one of the few selection-flavoured statements in this batch and bears on [[open-questions]] Q4. It is asserted with no evidence.
- **The disciplining argument is not extended to mutuals, nonprofits or bureaus.** That generalisation is not made here. Do not assume the board-plus-labour-market logic travels to organisations with no salable residual claim; [[team-production-and-monitoring]] predicts on other grounds that it will not.

## The parallel that produces the opposite welfare verdict

**[wiki synthesis]** Fama's mechanism and Holmström's career-concerns mechanism at [[career-concerns-and-managerial-risk-taking]] are built from the same ingredients: a multi-period labour market pricing an agent off a noisy performance signal, with no direct monitoring. They reach **opposite welfare conclusions** — Fama's approximates efficient full ex post settling up and is offered as why separation survives; Holmström's produces excessive conservatism and, under unverifiable private information, collapses to zero investment. Neither source addresses the other, the model setups differ, and no source states the contradiction. Recorded here as a parallel with opposite signs, not filed as a conflict, and worth a discriminating design if the wiki ever wants one.

## What is not established

- **Nothing is measured.** Fama says so.
- **The capital-market-rationality premise is imported by self-citation**, then extended to labour markets by analogy — "probably" — which the paper does not defend.
- **The affirmative tone outruns the argument.** Fama hedges repeatedly ("probably", "it is probably safe to say") and disclaims the empirical question, then concludes separation is efficient and has "good survival properties". Attribute the conclusion to the Chicago-school prior it comes from, not to evidence produced here.
- **No boundary content, no age content, no bureau content.** See above.
