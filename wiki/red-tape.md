# Red Tape

Bozeman's contribution is a definition sharp enough to argue with. **Organizational red tape** is a rule, regulation or procedure that (a) remains in force, (b) imposes a compliance burden, and (c) has **no efficacy for the rule's own stated functional object**. That is a deliberately narrow construction and it does real work: red tape is not "bureaucracy", not "regulation", and above all not "formalization", which Bozeman treats as a neutral organisational trait, the physiology rather than the pathology. Rules that impose burden *and* achieve their purpose are "white tape". Collapsing the distinction — treating rule volume as red tape — produces the incoherent notion of "good red tape" and destroys the concept. On top of that definition he builds an etiology: two origin pathways (rules born inefficacious, and good rules that decay into inefficacy) and an environmental model in which red tape rises with the number of external rule-promulgating entities and jurisdictional layers, offset by the quality of feedback from rule-affected parties back to rule-makers. **The word "risk" does not appear in the source.**

**Evidence tier note.** (b) **Definitional and theoretical framework.** No new data is collected or analysed. Tables 1 and 2 are explicitly framed as hypotheses "potentially amenable to test". The paper cites but does not execute Bozeman's own empirical programme (Bozeman, Reed & Scott 1992; Bozeman & Crow 1991; Kingsley & Bozeman 1992), which operationalised red tape behaviourally as procurement or task **delay** — a proxy the paper itself criticises for making "no essential distinction between red tape and white tape since there is no assessment of the reasons for delays or whether the delays are justified". Strongest cited evidence: Bozeman, Reed & Scott (1992), which found government ownership associated with more red tape *and* found privately-owned organisations with strong resource-dependence and communication ties to government also showed elevated red tape — which Bozeman reads as showing that ownership is not the causal agent, external political authority is.

## The etiology

**[model]** Two pathways, each with named mechanisms.

**Rule-inception red tape — born inefficacious.** *Inadequate comprehension* (designers misunderstand the means-end causality). *Self-aggrandizement / illegitimate functions* (a rule efficacious for an individual's private power, serving no sanctioned organisational purpose). *Negative-sum compromise* (a rule diluted across too many competing objectives serves none). *Overcontrol* (uncertainty and ambiguity trigger a formalisation response that oversteps the need). *Negative-sum process* (procedural and participatory values pursued past usefulness — mandatory participation by people with nothing to contribute).

**Rule-evolved red tape — good rules gone bad.** *Rule drift* (meaning lost to antiquity, personnel turnover, ritualised compliance). *Rule entropy* (meaning degrades as a rule passes through more organisations, levels and jurisdictions). *Change in implementation*, *change in the functional object*, *change in rule efficacy* as context shifts under a static rule. *Rule strain* (too many good rules exceed compliance capacity — "ten good rules may cause the organization... to interpret and apply rules less effectively, simply because of limited compliance capability"). **Accretion** — Bozeman's own term — rules piling up and interacting destructively even when each is individually sound: "rationality added to rationality may sum to irrationality". *Misapplication* through poor drafting or lost communication of purpose.

**[model — the environmental model, Exhibit 3]** Propositions 1.0/1.1: red tape increases with the number of external entities and the number of organisational levels and jurisdictional units promulgating rules for the focal organisation, via three channels — more opportunity for discretionary misapplication, more entropy, and less implementer "ownership" of rules. Proposition 2.0: red tape decreases with the quality and use of feedback mechanisms from rule-affected parties back to rule-makers. These are registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D42 and D43.

**[model — the typology]** Rule source (internal / external) crossed with locus of impact (internal / external) gives four kinds: *ordinary* red tape (internal origin, external impact), *intraorganizational* (internal/internal), *external control* (external origin, internal impact), and *pass-through* (external origin, external impact, the focal organisation merely a conduit). Bozeman also defines a rival unit of analysis — *stakeholder* red tape, where a rule is red tape relative to a particular stakeholder's functional object — and then argues against using it, as operationally intractable given the number of stakeholders with distinct objects. The paper argues for keeping levels of analysis separate rather than conflating them.

## Most of the causes are not government-exclusive

**[model — and this is the point most often lost when the source is cited]** Bozeman states that the general external-control model is "as applicable to private as it is to government organizations". Red tape on his account is a function of external-control density and stakeholder diversity, which *correlate* with government ownership but are conceptually separable from it. Only **two** causes are claimed inherent to government:

1. **Sovereign political authority.** Private organisations can exercise political authority "in proxy" but cannot possess sovereignty; sovereignty "carries with it legitimate coercive power of such force that citizens inevitably demand sharp constraints on officialdom and safeguards, usually procedural, against abuse" (crediting Kaufman 1977). Bozeman calls this "the only ineradicable difference".
2. **Breadth and interconnection of policy mission.** In competitive private markets one firm succeeding at another's expense is presumptively beneficial; among government organisations sharing a transcendent policy mission, the same zero- or negative-sum dynamic is presumptively red tape, with no market to arbitrate it. Bozeman argues this is why *accretion* is the most important government-correlated cause.

**[a — mixed]** The underlying public/private literature is reported by Bozeman himself as genuinely mixed. Buchanan (1975) found *private*-sector managers expressed **greater** rule adherence and commitment than public counterparts — directly against the folk assumption. Rainey (1979/1983), Baldwin (1990), Lan & Rainey (1992) and Kingsley & Bozeman (1992) found higher formalisation or perceived red tape among public managers, generally linked to external oversight-agency constraints rather than to ownership as such.

## Red tape is not a risk-aversion finding

**[wiki synthesis — the load-bearing caveat]** The word "risk" does not appear in this source. Bozeman theorises rule *efficacy* and *compliance burden*, not decision-makers' risk-reward payoffs. Three adjacent passages get cited as though they closed the gap, and none does:

- **Overcontrol** is described as "one of the most common responses to uncertainty and ambiguity... to seek control through formalization". That is a rule-*making* response to uncertainty by principals, not an account of agents avoiding risk in their own choices.
- The **Kaufman quote** Bozeman endorses — "Had we more trust in our public officers, we would feel less impelled to limit discretion by means of minutely detailed directions and prescriptions" — describes principals imposing discretion-limiting rules out of distrust. It could plausibly *produce* risk-averse agent behaviour downstream, but Bozeman does not draw that inference, and drawing it is the wiki's extension, not his claim.
- Officials' **strategic use of procedural delay** — slowing procurement or billing to preserve managerial discretion, using paperwork delay to resist anticipated cutbacks — is self-interested manipulation of red tape, not blame-driven caution.

The honest classification: this source supplies a **structural mechanism for institutional rigidity**, a plausible building block for a risk-aversion account, and no test of the link. "Bozeman shows large institutions are risk-averse because of red tape" is an unsupported leap.

## It is also not a size finding

**[wiki synthesis — gaps]** No claim here ties red tape to the focal organisation's headcount or budget. Every scale-adjacent claim is about the *environment*: entropy rises with the number of organisations, levels and jurisdictions involved in promulgating and applying a rule; Propositions 1.0/1.1 count external entities and layers. Rule strain is a capacity-versus-volume claim that could correlate with size but is not stated as a size effect. Large institutions plausibly attract more external controllers as they grow — but Bozeman neither makes nor tests that connection, and the wiki should treat it as an open inferential gap. The age claim is present but hedged: rule drift is tied to "organisational antiquity", immediately qualified with "Rule drift is not always a matter of the length of time that has passed since a rule's origins", with turnover, client-composition change and reorganisation offered as non-age triggers. See [[open-questions]] Q7.

## Levers, and one warning

**[model]** (1) Improve the quality and use of feedback from rule-affected parties to rule-makers — the paper's one clearly stated lever with a claimed causal direction, untested. (2) Reduce the number of external rule-promulgating layers and entities where feasible. (3) Analytically distinguish formalisation from red tape, so that reform targets genuinely inefficacious rules — Bozeman's polemical point against blanket red-tape-cutting drives that cannot tell red from white. Concrete design prescriptions are explicitly deferred to a companion piece; this is a diagnostic paper and says so.

**Balance note.** Bozeman rejects both stock explanations as under-theorised: the apologists' (red tape reflects government's higher accountability demands) and the critics' (red tape reflects the absence of a profit-motivated bottom line). He steelmans Kaufman's and Landau's counter-view — that apparent excess can be beneficial redundancy — before setting it aside for theory-building. His definition makes red tape pathological *by construction*; that is a stipulation, and it does not settle the empirical question of whether extensive government rules are on net harmful. He does not claim it does.

**Scope.** US, and specifically US-federal in its stakeholder taxonomy (Congress, OMB); the Constitution's fragmentation of authority is invoked as a structural source of white tape. Literature synthesis spans the 1960s to early 1990s. No claims about non-US regimes, authoritarian states, or pre-20th-century institutions.
