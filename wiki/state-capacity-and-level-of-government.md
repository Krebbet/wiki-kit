# State Capacity and Level of Government

Schleicher & Bagley argue that the "state capacity movement" has been diagnosing the wrong patient: it fixates on the federal government, when state and local governments do most of what "the state" actually does — roughly 19.9M+ state/local employees against a federal blended workforce of ~9.1M. Their positive account names three distinct pathologies operating at that lower tier: **nationalised elections** that strip local performance out of the signal by which local officials are selected and retained; a **procedural and participatory "tax"** whose fixed per-requirement cost falls hardest on the smaller agencies that state and local governments run, and whose access points are exploited by organised minorities against diffuse publics; and **structurally procyclical fiscal capacity**, since states can neither deficit-spend nor issue currency and so must contract exactly when recession raises demand for their services. For this wiki the page carries a second, more transferable payload: the source is the cleanest available instance of the **level-of-analysis substitution** that runs through the decay literature. Everything it describes as accumulating accumulates in an *external legal regime* dated to the 1970s and 1980s and applied uniformly to institutions of every age — a state agency founded last year and one founded a century ago face the identical little-NEPA and open-meetings requirements. Nothing in it is about a bounded organisation tracked through its own lifespan.

**Evidence tier note.** (b) **Institutional and legal-historical synthesis with citation-supported assertion** — a law-review article, not the authors' own primary empirical work, explicitly extending Bagley's prior *The Procedure Fetish* (2019). The components grade very unevenly and should not be cited as one thing:

- **Strongest — cited third-party quantitative work, none of it the authors' own.** Liscow & Brooks: US interstate highway costs per mile rose sharply and cannot be explained by wage or materials costs, with the residual attributed to rising "citizen voice" power since the 1970s. Campante & Do: cross-state correlation between capital-city isolation and corruption and worse public-spending outcomes. Caughey & Warshaw: a longitudinal measure of state policy against majority opinion — ~40% alignment on issue arrival, ~70% after three decades. Darr/Hitt/Dunaway and Gao/Lee/Murphy on the effects of newspaper closures on voting behaviour and municipal borrowing costs.
- **Weakest — statutory cataloguing.** Dozens of citations establishing which states have "little NEPAs", legislative vetoes, open-meetings hearing rights, weak-or-absent judicial deference doctrines, and loose standing rules. This establishes **cross-sectional procedural density**. It does not establish a trend, does not establish a causal effect on any outcome, and does not establish that these rules are more numerous now than at any earlier baseline. Alongside it sit illustrative anecdotes: San Francisco licensing driverless cars while failing to fill potholes, NYC subway capital costs, the NY Court of Appeals.
- **No falsification condition is stated.** The closest approach is an unelaborated concession that the procedural tax "may be worth paying if the result is... higher-quality or better-informed agency decisions." The authors never specify what evidence of higher decision quality would look like, never measure decision quality against procedural burden, and never name an observation that would count against the thesis — a high-procedural-density jurisdiction with high measured capacity, for instance. Record this as an evidentiary gap in a piece whose central claim is a design claim.

## The level-of-analysis critique — and the source's own repetition of it

**[wiki synthesis]** The article's opening move is a level-of-analysis indictment: claims about "American state capacity" are routinely made about the federal government and then generalised to a state that is, by headcount and by function, mostly not federal. Land use, infrastructure permitting, policing, schooling, courts and most service delivery sit below the federal tier.

Having made the charge, the source partly repeats it. It operates simultaneously at three levels without signposting the shifts:

1. **An entire governmental tier as an aggregate** — "state and local government" as a single object compared against "the federal government".
2. **A legal or procedural regime** — state APAs, little-NEPAs, open-meetings statutes, standing doctrine — applied across thousands of heterogeneous agencies that are not otherwise alike.
3. **Single-institution anecdotes** — the SF pothole contrast, the NYC subway, a state high court.

**No claim anywhere in the source is about a specific agency's internal trajectory over its own lifespan.** The unit is always the jurisdiction or the rule regime, never a bounded organisation tracked through time. This is not an institutional-lifecycle study and must not be filed as one.

## Three causal chains

**[model — each mechanism is stated, not derived; none is estimated]**

**1. Nationalised elections → weak accountability → underinvestment in capacity.** State and local elections are "second-order": voting is driven by national partisan cues rather than local performance. An official therefore faces no electoral penalty for poor service delivery and no reward for investing in bureaucratic quality — and capacity, on Besley & Persson's account which the source adopts, is precisely a deliberate investment that politicians must be incentivised to make. Aggravating conditions named: gerrymandering, worse at state than federal level (Seifter); term limits; an information-starved electorate (89% cannot name their state legislator, citing Rogers); and thinning local media, with the newspaper-closure studies supplying the one piece of quantitative support for the information limb.

**2. Procedural and participatory density → capture by organised minorities, plus a resource tax on agencies → degraded output.** Small motivated groups — NIMBYs, chambers of commerce, incumbent unions — organise more easily than diffuse publics; the source cites Olson explicitly for the asymmetry. They then exploit hearing, comment and litigation rights that formally exist to protect the public interest. The framing the article turns on:

> "It's useful to think of that as imposing a tax on government action... As with any tax, however, taxing more means getting less."

**[empirical — cited, not run here]** Participation in these processes is not representative: Einstein, Palmer & Glick find participants skew wealthier, older, whiter and more likely to own their homes. The source's own vocabulary for the pathology: "procedure fetish", "hyper-proceduralism", "agency ossification", and "overparticipation" (Lemar).

**3. Fiscal procyclicality.** States cannot deficit-spend and cannot issue currency; balanced-budget rules and debt limits force budgets to contract exactly when a recession raises demand for state services. The mechanism is fiscal-structural rather than behavioural — but note the authors' own hedge, which belongs on their side of the ledger: the *strength* of these paper constraints is "a subject of substantial debate", since states find ways to skirt them, even as the aggregate effect is asserted. Related items in the same section: "scoop and toss" pension underfunding as disguised deficit spending, and Baumol cost disease compounded by occupational licensing restricting labour supply.

## The size claim, stated precisely

**[model]** This is a genuine size mechanism, cleanly stated, and one of very few in this wiki: **the absolute cost of a fixed procedural requirement — a hearing, a FOIA response, a lawsuit — is roughly size-invariant, so its effective tax rate scales inversely with the size of the institution bearing it.** State and local agencies are on average far smaller than their federal counterparts, so an identical rule burden falls proportionally harder on them.

Two things this does not say, and the distinction is the whole reason the claim is quotable. It is **synchronic and cross-sectional**: it compares a small agency to a large one at a moment in time. It says nothing about what happens to a given agency as *it* grows, and nothing whatever about what happens as it ages. See [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]], where this is registered as its own axis precisely so it cannot be read off as an age effect.

## Regime age is not institution age

**[wiki synthesis — the batch's cleanest instance of the substitution]** Everything the source describes as having accumulated has accumulated **outside** the institutions it burdens.

The procedural apparatus — state APAs, "little NEPAs", open-meetings statutes, liberalised standing, weak-or-absent deference doctrines — is characterised as "the ideological residue of the 1970s and 1980s": a wave of environmentalist, civil-rights and consumer-protection-era legal reform layered onto administrative structures that already existed, followed by a distinct second wave of Reagan-era disinvestment and contracting-out. That is accumulation **in a legal regime**, and the regime is imposed uniformly on institutions of every age. A state agency created last year and one created a century ago face the identical little-NEPA and open-meetings requirements, filed in the same courts under the same standing rules.

The source supplies **no mechanism by which an individual agency's own founding date or years-in-existence matters**, and never tests one. Note that its single most citable quantitative time series carries the same structure: Liscow & Brooks on highway costs since the 1970s is a claim about a *regime* — the growth of citizen-voice legal tools — applied to a *category* of infrastructure projects. It is not a claim about the age of any highway agency, and the age of the agencies building the roads is not a variable in it.

State this plainly, because the temptation to read it the other way is strong: a piece whose entire subject is things getting worse over calendar time contains no institution-level age variable at all.

## The tension the source surfaces against itself

**[empirical]** The article's own best longitudinal evidence cuts against its headline. Caughey & Warshaw find state policy converging on majority opinion ~40% of the time when an issue arrives and **~70% after three decades**. Slow — but that is eventual responsiveness, not the comprehensive unaccountability the nationalised-elections argument implies.

The authors surface this themselves rather than burying it, conceding that "the process of getting to majority opinion can take decades", and do not resolve it. Recorded here as a self-surfaced internal tension, on their own side of the ledger, not as an external rebuttal.

## Economic production

**[empirical — cited third-party work; the causal attributions are the authors']** The production channel is the strongest applied content in the piece.

- **Housing.** Land-use procedural regimes — zoning variance hearings, with little-NEPA environmental review layered on top of open-meetings hearing rights — are named as the *primary* mechanism restricting housing supply and driving the affordability crisis.
- **Infrastructure.** Procedural and litigation burden drives US construction costs far above international peers (Liscow & Brooks) and stalls interstate transmission buildout, since each line requires approval from every state — sometimes every county — it crosses.
- **What is explicitly *not* the obstruction.** The source rules out the usual institutional-economics suspect by name: US property rights are strong (14th most secure globally) and stable. The obstruction runs entirely through procedural process.

**[wiki synthesis]** Functionally this makes procedure a rent-protection mechanism for incumbents — established homeowners using hearing rights to block new supply that would erode the scarcity value of what they already own. The source describes the pattern in detail but does not use "rent-seeking" as its own vocabulary for it; the label is this wiki's, the mechanism is theirs.

## Public/private: silent, not negative

**[wiki synthesis]** Section IV poses the public/private contrast directly — why is the private sector so good at delivery when public services are so poor — and rejects a cultural-determinism answer, citing that American public infrastructure in the 19th and early 20th centuries *outperformed* Britain and Germany. The answer offered is the structural one above.

But the entire causal apparatus is public administrative law by construction: state APAs, FOIA analogues, little-NEPAs, open-meetings laws, standing doctrine, judicial deference regimes. None of it could mechanically apply to a private firm. The private sector appears only as a comparison baseline, never as a second domain under the same mechanism. **The source therefore supplies zero evidence in either direction on whether procedural or veto-point accumulation degrades private institutions.** Silent, not negative — and the distinction matters for a wiki whose brief includes what is invariant across the public/private divide.

## Levers, none evaluated

**[model — named by the authors, effectiveness not assessed by them]**

- Subsidise local news, publicly or philanthropically.
- Build state- and local-focused political organising; YIMBY and Black Lives Matter are cited as groups attempting exactly this.
- Electoral-law reform letting voters distinguish national from local party brands.
- Extend federal administrative-law reform momentum — *Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County* (2025), and the end of *Chevron* deference under *Loper Bright* — into "largely ignored pockets of state administrative law".
- Challenge the ideological commitment to maximal public-participation rights, which they locate specifically on the political left.
- Raise bureaucrat pay and legislative staff funding.

**The caveat they attach to their own prescriptions is worth more than the prescriptions.** Invoking Posner & Vermeule's "inside/outside" problem, they concede that the same political dysfunction they diagnose as the *cause* of low capacity is likely to block the *reforms* meant to fix it. **[wiki synthesis]** Treat this as a general caution applicable to every institutional-reform lever this wiki records: a lever whose adoption requires the capacity it is meant to create is not yet a lever. It is a description of what would help.

## Scope and priors

**Scope.** United States only; contemporary, with the article discussing the 2024 election. Historical contrast to America's own late-19th and early-20th-century record, and brief non-systematic gestures at Britain, Germany and unnamed poorer-but-better-governed democracies. No cross-country regression, no systematic comparative design — international comparison is illustrative, not evidential. Coverage spans the full range of state and local functions and says almost nothing about federal agencies except by contrast.

**Ideological priors.** David Schleicher (Yale, property and urban law) and Nicholas Bagley (Michigan, author of *The Procedure Fetish*). The piece self-identifies as writing *in solidarity with but correcting* the state-capacity movement and the abundance agenda (Klein & Thompson); it cites the Niskanen Center approvingly and explicitly rejects DOGE-style headcount cutting as resting on a mistaken model of where inefficiency comes from. This is good-governance institutionalist liberalism — adjacent to but distinct from state-capacity libertarianism — wanting a **more capable, not smaller** state, achieved by pruning specifically 1960s–70s-vintage proceduralism. That target is one more associated with the left's own internal critics than with deregulators, and the authors are explicit about it.

**Where the argument outruns its evidence.** The claim that state administrative law plagues states and localities *more* than the federal government is argued mainly by demonstrating that comparably strict or stricter procedural rules **exist** at state level. That is evidence of density parity or superiority. It is not evidence of comparatively worse *outcomes*, which is the claim being made.
