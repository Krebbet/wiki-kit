# Decision Rights and the Authority Theory of the Firm

Gibbons distils the theory-of-the-firm literature into four elemental formal models — **rent-seeking** (Williamson; Klein, Crawford & Alchian), **property-rights** (Grossman-Hart-Moore), **incentive-system** (Holmstrom-Milgrom), and **adaptation/authority** (Simon 1951; Williamson 1971/73/75/91) — and shows they are **essentially orthogonal rather than nested**. The fourth is the one genuinely new to this wiki and is this page's main subject: decisions are non-contractible even ex post, and the *right to decide* cannot itself be renegotiated once the state is revealed (the opportunity is fleeting), so parties pre-allocate a pure decision right to a "boss" who then acts self-interestedly. Gibbons proves that this delivers "a coherent elemental theory of the firm **without specific investments**" — which means asset specificity is not necessary for the phenomenon it was introduced to explain, and it means Williamson's own corpus contains two distinct theories rather than one. Two cross-cutting distinctions come with it and are the most reusable content in the source for this wiki's framework: **payoff rights against decision rights** as the object a governance change reallocates, and the **control branch against the contract branch** — whether integration confers real fiat or is merely a descriptive label on a cluster of ordinary contracts.

**Evidence tier note.** **(b) theory synthesis, entirely.** Gibbons's own third caveat is that he will "discuss theory, essentially ignoring evidence and testing." No new empirical work; the empirical literature is cited and not reproduced. Precision varies *within* the source and he says so: property-rights and incentive-system theories are exact formal derivations; the adaptation theory is formalised here for the first time by borrowing others' machinery (Aghion-Dewatripont-Rey 2002; Hart-Holmstrom 2002); and **rent-seeking has never been fully formalised as a unified theory** — the section title ends in a question mark. The one running case (GM–Fisher Body) is explicitly flagged by Gibbons as possibly "fact, myth, or some combination," and he declines to adjudicate it. **(c) wiki synthesis** is marked inline.

## The adaptation / authority theory

**[model]** The elemental structure:

1. Some decisions are **non-contractible even ex post** — they cannot be written into an enforceable agreement at any stage.
2. The opportunity to decide is **fleeting**: by the time the state is revealed there is no time to renegotiate who decides.
3. Therefore parties must **pre-allocate the decision right itself** — a pure right to decide, with no accompanying payoff consequence.
4. The holder then decides **self-interestedly** once the state is revealed, so the decision is imperfectly tailored to the state; the loss is the gap between her preferred action and the joint-surplus-maximising one.
5. The boss is selected ex ante to **maximise expected total surplus**, not because she is more entitled to authority. Authority is efficient precisely because *someone must decide unilaterally* once uncertainty resolves.

**No specific investment is required anywhere in this chain.** That is Gibbons's headline structural result about it, and its consequences for the wiki's open conflict are recorded at [[rival-firm-boundary-theories]].

**What the elemental model does not contain**: any removal mechanism. It is one-shot; the subordinate has no ex post recourse. **[wiki synthesis]** That is a striking omission for a theory of authority, and it marks the boundary between this account and [[formal-and-real-authority]], where the subordinate's information is precisely what gives him leverage against a formal-authority holder who cannot afford to investigate.

**The extension that detaches it from firm boundaries.** "Contracting for Control" (Aghion-Dewatripont-Rey; Baker, Gibbons & Murphy 2004a) applies the same machinery to *any* two parties passing decision rights by contract, "with fixed boundaries" — i.e. decision-rights allocation detached entirely from ownership and integration, and therefore from whether the parties are inside one firm at all. That is what makes this theory usable on institutions generally rather than on make-or-buy.

## The two cross-cutting distinctions

**[model]** These are the most directly reusable content for this wiki's framework, and both are Gibbons's own.

**1. Payoff rights against decision rights** (his Table 1) — what does a governance change actually reallocate?

| Theory | Reallocates |
|---|---|
| Incentive-system (Holmstrom-Milgrom) | **Pure payoff right** — ownership changes what you are paid, not what you can do |
| Adaptation / authority | **Pure decision right** — ownership changes what you can do, not what you are paid |
| Property-rights (Grossman-Hart) | **A blend** — a decision right whose *exercise* determines payoff via ex post bargaining |
| Rent-seeking (Williamson) | Turns on **alienable against inalienable** decision rights: integration can unify alienable rights; rights inhering in human capital always stay with their holder |

The last row is a hard constraint Gibbons draws out as the theory's own limitation: there is a ceiling on how much power any formal ownership change can confer.

**2. Control branch against contract branch** (Section 4B) — a second, orthogonal split, and a genuinely different axis from anything currently in [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]]:

- **Control branch** (Coase 1937; Simon 1951; property-rights; adaptation): integration *really does* give greater control — fiat is real.
- **Contract branch** (Alchian & Demsetz 1972; Jensen & Meckling 1976; Cheung 1983): integration changes nothing — "the word firm is merely descriptive, a collective noun denoting a particular cluster of otherwise ordinary contractual relationships" (Masten 1988). Alchian & Demsetz's own formulation, on [[team-production-and-monitoring]]: the firm's power to settle disputes by fiat "is a delusion."

The elemental incentive-system theory sits in the **contract** branch: ownership does not change the agent's action space, only her payoff function.

**A third axis Gibbons names and does not develop: the legal regime.** Masten (1988) and Williamson (1991) distinguish employment law (the employer holds control rights over *how* work is done) from commercial law, and note that courts apply a **forbearance doctrine** inside firms — they will not hear disputes between divisions — while adjudicating disputes between firms. Which rules of enforcement apply is a different variable from all four elemental theories, each of which holds the legal regime fixed. It is the same observation Williamson's contract-law axis carries on [[governance-structures]], arriving here as a residual the formal theories exclude.

## Internal rent-seeking and influence costs

**[model]** The source's other genuinely new item for this wiki, from Milgrom & Roberts (1988/90) plus Gibbons's own Section 3D model (a career-concerns / signal-jamming lobbying game).

The claim: **granting someone a decision right invites lobbying directed at them.** Power attracts influence-seeking as a direct structural consequence, with a derived comparative static — equilibrium lobbying intensity rises with how much the lobbyist's own payoff depends on the decision. The efficient allocation of control must therefore net out *both* the self-interested distortion of the decision *and* the lobbying cost that allocating it induces.

**[wiki synthesis]** Three things follow that the wiki should keep straight:

1. **Rent-seeking does not disappear on integration; it relocates.** The dissipation that [[rent-seeking-and-the-welfare-cost-of-transfers]] models as competition for an external prize reappears inside the organisation as competition for a decision. Gibbons imports Tullock's (1980) contest model verbatim as the formal core of the *between*-firm rent-seeking theory; the influence-cost model is its intra-firm counterpart, derived rather than asserted.
2. **This is a formal derivation of what [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]] and [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]] assert** — that a locus of authority attracts pressure. Those pages carry the claim for multi-principal and veto-point settings on the strength of argument; here it falls out of a signal-jamming model.
3. **It is a second, independent route to bureaucratic rule-boundedness.** [[multitask-incentive-theory]] already carries Prendergast's influence-cost derivation of seniority rules and fixed pay bands as a response to a corruptible evaluator. This is the same mechanism reaching the same institutional form from organisational economics rather than from personnel economics. Neither has been tested.

## What each theory says a governance change costs

**[model]** Gibbons's account of how governance affects total surplus, kept per theory:

- **Rent-seeking**: resources wasted contesting an appropriable quasi-rent — pure loss.
- **Property-rights**: under- and over-investment relative to first best, because ex ante investment incentives track surplus *share*, not marginal product.
- **Incentive-system**: distorted multitask effort allocation when the measurable signal is misaligned with true output.
- **Adaptation**: decisions imperfectly tailored to the state, because whoever decides maximises her own payoff and not joint surplus.

Two named failure modes are worth adopting as vocabulary. **"The cost of control is the loss of initiative"** — Gibbons's own aphorism, a corollary of "using a formal instrument to stop one hold-up problem typically creates another," which is the crisp form of the "integration is never a free lunch" line on [[property-rights-theory-of-the-firm]]. And **"drone employees"** — his name for the property-rights theory's own poverty as an account of internal organisation: it contains only "solo entrepreneurs" and employees who own nothing, face no modelled incentives, and therefore do nothing. That is a critique of a theory's incompleteness, not an empirical claim about firms.

**Low-powered employee incentives, re-derived.** The incentive-system theory delivers weaker optimal contract slopes for employees than for independent contractors **by construction**: the employer, not the employee, captures the asset's non-contractible value, so the optimal contract need not distract the employee with ownership-linked incentives. This is a second derivation of the result [[multitask-incentive-theory]] already carries from unmeasurability — same conclusion, different route, and the two have not been tested against each other.

## Scale and age: a structural silence

**[model]** **Headcount, budget, hierarchy layers and firm age never appear as variables anywhere in this essay** — flagged rather than passed over. The apparatus is two-party and one-asset or one-decision-right throughout; even integration of more than one asset is explicitly bracketed as future work. This is a structural silence rather than a null finding, and it is the same silence [[property-rights-theory-of-the-firm]] and [[multitask-incentive-theory]] both carry. Four elemental theories of the firm, and not one has a size term.

## Public and private

**[model]** Gibbons is **silent on public against private specifically**. Three things push toward generality without being evidence for it: the introduction claims these theories are at work "not only in industrial organization and organizational economics, but also in corporate finance, development economics, economic history, international trade, labor economics, political economy, and beyond"; **Simon's 1951 foundation for the adaptation theory is a theory of *employment*, not of firms** — it applies to any principal/subordinate authority relation wherever a decision must be delegated because ex post renegotiation is infeasible; and the "Contracting for Control" extension applies to any two parties passing decision rights with boundaries fixed. All of that is scope generalisation by construction of the models. None of it has been tested across sectors.
