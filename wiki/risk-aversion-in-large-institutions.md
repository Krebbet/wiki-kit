# Risk Aversion in Large Institutions

The research job behind this page was framed as "why are large institutions risk averse?" — a question that presupposes its answer. **This batch does not support the presupposition.** Zero of the eight sources test institutional *size* against risk-taking behaviour. Holmström explicitly rejects a size-based (risk-bearing-capacity) account for firms "of even modest size"; Bozeman's drivers are environmental, not size-based; Hammond and Kahneman & Lovallo have no size variable at all; Kahneman & Lovallo's authors deny the general reading outright; Manso's result holds for risk-*neutral* agents and so is not about risk preference; Hammond's central theorem undercuts the veto-point intuition rather than supporting it. The one source that directly *measures* risk aversion — Nicholson-Crotty et al.'s randomized survey experiment on 300 real managers — tests the sector version of the hypothesis and finds against it. What this batch actually contains is a set of well-specified mechanisms that would produce timidity *if* timidity obtains, all of them keyed to individual payoffs or to external control density rather than to size, plus one direct measurement that cuts the other way. The honest statement of where the wiki stands is: **the affirmative case here is mechanism-assertion, and the single direct measurement is negative.**

**Evidence tier note for the batch.** (a) Empirical, with a real design: **one** source — Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty & Webeck (randomized survey experiment, n=300). (a) Qualitative synthesis with an institutional interest: **one** — the OECD OPSI working paper, which must be read as advocacy (see below). (b) Formal models: Holmström, Manso, Hammond, and the aggregation calculation in Kahneman & Lovallo. (b) Argumentative or definitional theory: Hood's chapter and Bozeman's paper, both of which are frameworks proposing hypotheses rather than testing them. **Nothing in the batch measures a structural variable and a behavioural outcome on the same subjects.**

## Two crossed distinctions the sources themselves keep separate

The batch only stops looking contradictory once two orthogonal questions are pulled apart. The first is about the **level at which the mechanism operates**: does timidity arise because an individual's personal payoff from a risky decision is asymmetric, or because the organisation's structure constrains what it can do irrespective of anyone's payoff? The second is about **what is being claimed**: that risk aversion is a function of institutional *size*, or that it is a function of *sector* (public versus private). These are independent, and the literature routinely collapses them.

**Mechanism level.**

| | Individual-payoff mechanism | Structural mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Sources** | Hood (blame risk, career payoffs, negativity bias); Kahneman & Lovallo (loss aversion, narrow framing); Holmström (reputational signalling to a labour market); Manso (moral hazard under a mis-specified contract); OECD (no financial upside capture, real career and legal downside) | Bozeman (external-control density, rule accretion, entropy); Hammond (veto points and preference divergence); Kahneman & Lovallo's *organisational* half (groupthink, winner's-curse project selection, review cadence); Manso's cited Seru result (headquarters reallocating away from failing units) |
| **What it predicts** | Timidity varies with tenure security, proximity to review, verifiability, contract form, and who captures the upside | Timidity — if any — varies with rule density, layers of external control, and the geometry of blocking coalitions |
| **Evidence** | Three formal models, one argumentative chapter, one qualitative synthesis. No behavioural measurement | One definitional framework, one formal theorem whose result is "anything can happen". No behavioural measurement |

Note what the structural column does *not* contain: any source that both counts a structural variable and observes cautious behaviour. Bozeman never uses the word "risk". Hammond never uses the word "risk". The structural family is, in this batch, a theory of *rigidity* that has not been connected to *timidity*.

**Claim type.**

| | Size claim | Sector claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Asserted by** | Nobody, in any tested form. Manso's Section VIII is the only gesture — large corporations cannot credibly promise failure tolerance below the executive level — and it is one HBS case plus two practitioner books, with no size parameter in the model. Kahneman & Lovallo's hierarchy-depth compounding is the authors' own explicitly untested extrapolation | Hood (public-sector evidence only; private extension asserted); Bozeman (two of many causes government-inherent; the rest general); OECD (explicitly and only about government); Hammond (public bureaucracies in democratic systems, no private analogue offered) |

The size row is the finding. A batch assembled to explain why large institutions are risk averse produced no source that measures institutional size against risk-taking, and one that argues against the standard size mechanism.

## The individual-payoff family

**[model / assertion]** Four sources converge on one shape: the decision-maker bears the downside of a risky decision and captures little or none of the upside, so declining is privately rational even where the institution would gain. They differ in who imposes the asymmetry.

- **[[blame-avoidance-and-negativity-bias]]** — the audience imposes it. Negativity bias makes blame weigh more than credit; fire-alarm oversight makes only failure visible; the officeholder's re-election, promotion and tenure ride on it. Hood's moderators are tenure security, proximity to the next accountability event, and the density of the external scrutiny ecology. None is size. Public-sector evidence only.
- **[[isolation-errors-and-portfolio-framing]]** — the decision-maker's own utility function imposes it. Loss aversion plus narrow framing make each gamble be judged alone, which prices a portfolio 27% below its pooled value in the paper's own model. But this source pairs the timidity with an equally-evidenced **bold-forecast** bias, and the authors deny the general reading in terms: "we do not claim that an objective observer would describe managerial decisions as generally risk averse." Private-sector evidence only.
- **[[career-concerns-and-managerial-risk-taking]]** — the labour market imposes it. Projects are tests of talent, so a risk-neutral manager suppresses ability-revealing positive-NPV projects to protect his inferred ability. **Behaviour that looks risk averse with no risk preference anywhere in the model.** Where private information cannot be verified, the equilibrium is zero investment.
- **[[exploration-exploitation-incentive-contracts]]** — the contract imposes it. Pay-for-performance provably induces reversion to the safe known action when exploration is wanted. Manso states the result is unchanged for a risk-averse agent, "because the critical elements... are the likelihood ratios between the different action plans, not the agent's preferences." **This is a moral-hazard result, not a risk-preference result** — a distinction that matters because fixing risk preferences would not fix it.

**[wiki synthesis]** Holmström and Manso together make a sharp point that should survive whatever happens to the rest of this page: apparent institutional risk aversion does not require anyone in the institution to be risk averse. Two independent formal routes produce it from risk-*neutral* agents. Any explanation that reaches for a psychological trait is reaching past two simpler ones — and, symmetrically, observing timid behaviour tells you nothing about which of the three mechanisms is running.

## The structural family

**[model]** Two sources, neither of which connects its structure to behaviour.

- **[[red-tape]]** — Bozeman's rules that impose burden and achieve nothing, arriving by two routes (born inefficacious; decayed into inefficacy through drift, entropy, strain and accretion). Red tape rises with the count of external rule-promulgating entities and jurisdictional layers, falls with feedback quality. **Most of these causes are general to any externally-controlled organisation, not government-exclusive**: Bozeman says the model is "as applicable to private as it is to government organizations" and names only two government-inherent causes, sovereignty and the interconnection of policy missions. He reports the public/private literature as genuinely mixed and cites Buchanan (1975) finding *private* managers expressed greater rule adherence than public ones. The word "risk" never appears.
- **[[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]]** — Hammond's spatial model of the core. The intuitive story — more veto points, more gridlock, more caution — **is undercut by his central result**: veto-point count and autonomy are not monotonically related once preference divergence is allowed to vary, and all 720 rank-orderings across six systems are logically achievable. The word "risk" never appears here either, and even the bridge to caution cuts both ways: a *narrow* core means any bureaucratic deviation can be overturned, which would itself produce defensive status-quo bias. Do not cite this page as support for the veto-point story.

## What has actually been measured

**[a — empirical, and the only real test in the batch]** Nicholson-Crotty, Nicholson-Crotty & Webeck (2019) ran a between-subjects randomized survey experiment on **n = 300 actual US managers**, 150 public and 150 private, fielded via a Qualtrics panel in May 2017, pre-tested on a Prolific sample, with presentation order randomized. Three manipulations, subjects randomly assigned to condition in each:

1. **Framing** — the Asian Disease Problem, positive (lives saved) versus negative (lives lost) frame.
2. **Status-quo bias A** — three contractor bids for a $10,000 one-month job, neutral versus a stated prior preference at 15% / 20% / 25% cost savings (adapted from Burmeister & Schade 2007).
3. **Status-quo bias B** — three software packages, neutral versus a stated incumbent package A, B or C.
4. **PSM** — public service motivation, measured (not manipulated) on Kim's 12-item scale.

Design caveat that must travel with the result: subjects were **not** randomly assigned to sector. Sector is a self-reported, screened characteristic, so the public/private comparison is an observed moderator of a randomized manipulation, not itself a randomized factor. PSM likewise is measured, so the PSM-risk relationship is observational within experimental cells.

**Results.**

- **Framing** (Table 1, overall χ² = 61.64, p = 0.000). Under the *positive* frame, no significant sector difference in choosing the risky option: public 24.32% vs private 27.63%, n.s. Under the *negative* (loss) frame, public managers chose the risky option **significantly more**: 76.62% vs 63.51%, **p < .10**. That is a reversal, not merely a null.
- **Status-quo bias A** (Table 2, χ² = 40.75, p = 0.000). *Private* managers were significantly more likely than both the neutral group and the alternative-status-quo groups to choose the status quo in all three conditions. Public managers exceeded the neutral group in only 2 of 3 conditions and never significantly exceeded the alternative-status-quo comparison.
- **Status-quo bias B** (Table 3, reported χ² = 40.75, p = 0.000). Private managers more likely to choose the status quo versus control in 2 of 3 treatments; public managers showed **no significant status-quo effect in any comparison**.
- **PSM** is sector-variant even though behaviour largely is not: public mean .19 vs private −.20, p < .05. Higher PSM associates with the risk-*seeking* choice specifically under loss framing (.10 vs −.18, p < .05) and not at all under gain framing or in either status-quo experiment. The authors note this runs *opposite* to Buurman et al. (2012) and Tepe & Prokop (2018), which reported PSM associated with greater risk aversion.

**Authors' own caveats, recorded in full because they constrain how far this result can be pushed.** (i) **Power / Type II error**, addressed directly in their note 9: "the smaller N dictated by our desire to use actual public managers may be large enough to detect status quo bias but not large enough to confidently say that a lack of observed difference between public and private managers is not a type 2 error." Their mitigation — that the *direction* is consistently one way across conditions — is an argument, not a resolution. They add: "we encourage caution in the interpretation of the status quo bias results because of our relatively limited sample size and the challenges of interpreting null findings." (ii) **Validated but not tailored vignettes**: using previously-published manipulations buys confidence that differences are not design artefacts, at the cost of scenarios that "obviously do not mimic those decisions perfectly or for every subject"; they call for future work tailored by occupation (police, teachers, budget officers, case workers). (iii) The Asian Disease Problem is famous enough that some respondents may know it, which could damp the manipulation; no reason is given to expect this to differ by sector, and it is not tested. (iv) **Nonprofit managers are excluded** from the sample entirely.

**Scope.** US managers only, May 2017, experienced and senior: mean age 46, >25 years in the workforce, >10 years in post, median managing 100–249 people, roughly a third managing more than 1,000. **[wiki synthesis]** That last statistic is worth dwelling on: the sample is drawn almost entirely from managers *inside large organisations*, and it still finds no sector effect. It does not thereby test size — there is no small-organisation comparison group — but it does mean the null cannot be dismissed as an artefact of studying small bodies.

**[wiki synthesis]** This result gets no standalone page deliberately. It is one experiment on two behavioural constructs, and its value here is comparative: it is the only place in the batch where the presumption meets a measurement. It does not refute Hood, Bozeman or Hammond, because none of them measured behaviour — it removes the empirical warrant that their presumption was borrowing. See [[presumed-vs-measured-public-sector-risk-aversion]].

## The OECD source, and why it is filed as advocacy

**[a — qualitative, institutional advocacy; not evidence on par with the rest]** The OECD Observatory of Public Sector Innovation's working paper names risk aversion "the most consistent and powerful factor limiting the consideration of innovative options" in government. That sentence is the most quotable line in the batch and the least supported one, and the page records it as such.

- **Evidentiary base**: a qualitative synthesis of **5 of 38 member states** — Canada and Brazil in depth, Israel, Denmark and Latvia as lighter "scans" — built from interviews, workshops and desktop research, non-random and small-N. The report concedes "there will inevitably be limitations in direct data and comparisons", and its own Annex A evidence scaffolding is "not exhaustive" with "interview and coding protocols... to be developed at a later stage". The framework's evidentiary apparatus is, by its own account, not yet validated.
- **Citation structure**: heavily self-citing to prior OECD frameworks and declarations (2017a, 2019a, 2020a, 2021a) — a largely closed loop. The producer's institutional mandate is to promote public-sector innovation, giving it a direct interest in the claim that risk aversion is a real and solvable barrier addressable by OECD-designed frameworks. Normative "must" language recurs as imperative rather than as derivation.
- **A hypothetical case is presented as illustration**: "Box 2, Country X" is explicitly invented to demonstrate how to apply the framework. It has zero evidential value and must never be coded as a case finding.
- **Its mechanisms are sector claims, not size claims**, and it says so: public-sector innovation "is distinct from the private sector, which principally relies on market-based feedback mechanisms, such as sales and profits." The core stated mechanism is the payoff asymmetry — "in the public sector there is no expectation of appropriating financial gains... there may be less incentives to actually take risks in the public sector as costs may outweigh benefits in personal calculations by civil servants" — plus career-limiting-consequence fear (Brazil interviews), rule rigidity (Brazil), selection filtering by middle managers on "personal characteristics and social capital" rather than merit (Canada's "clay layer"), institutional memory of prior punishment (Israel), and external scrutiny intensity (Latvia procurement). It supplies **zero** evidence that risk aversion scales with headcount, budget, or hierarchy depth. The report also declines to rank or reconcile its own mechanisms, conceding they "are informed and shaped by a range of interconnected factors which are not easy to unravel."
- **It carries its own counter-evidence**, which is to its credit and should not be dropped. Meijer & Thaens's (2021) "innovation paradox" names real perverse effects of public-sector innovation — instability, absence of democratic control, waste of public money, disruption of power balance, unforeseen security risks — so "less innovation" is not automatically "worse performance". The Danish Innovation Barometer reportedly shows **~80% of workplaces in Nordic countries introduced at least one innovation in a two-year period**, self-reported, which sits awkwardly against the framing that governments are systematically non-innovative. And in the Danish free-municipality experiment held up as a lever, the report notes people at the political level "were fired for failures".

**[wiki synthesis]** Use this source for the *taxonomy* of mechanisms officials describe, and for its country vignettes as illustrations. Do not use it as evidence that the phenomenon obtains, and never place its headline sentence alongside Nicholson-Crotty's experiment as though the two were comparable measurements.

## The counterweights, which the batch supplies itself

**[a / model]** Any page claiming institutional process is uniformly a brake on risk-taking is contradicted from inside this batch. Kahneman & Lovallo name **groupthink** (dissent suppressed, bad news filtered, optimism mutually validated) and **winner's-curse project selection** (where projects compete for internal funding on their forecasts, the survivors are by regression to the mean the most over-optimistic) as organisational mechanisms producing excess *boldness* in exactly the settings the veto-point and red-tape literatures treat as cautious. Their organisational outcome data all sit on this side: process plants at >2× estimated cost, >80% of start-ups short of planned market share, acquirers not earning positive returns. Hood's rural-railway example points the same way from the other direction: blame avoidance produced *more* regulation and *more* apparent caution while raising net risk. Caution, safety and good outcomes come apart.

## What nobody measured

**[wiki synthesis — the gap, stated as the design that would close it]**

1. **No source measures institutional size against risk-taking behaviour.** Not one. The size claim in the job title is untested here in both directions.
2. **No source measures a structural variable and a behavioural outcome on the same subjects.** Bozeman theorises rule density and never observes a choice. Hammond derives core size and never observes a choice. Nicholson-Crotty observes choices and never measures blame exposure, veto-point count, or rule density. The two halves of the argument have never been run on the same people.
3. **No source establishes the aggregation step.** Hood, Kahneman & Lovallo, Holmström and Manso are all individual-level. Whether many career-concerned individuals compose into an institution that behaves cautiously — or whether the institution's portfolio averages their timidity away, which is precisely what Kahneman & Lovallo's own pooling arithmetic would imply — is asserted by nobody and tested by nobody.
4. **The baseline is never specified.** "Risk averse" relative to what? Relative to expected-value neutrality, to a comparable private firm, to the same institution's past, or to what an outside observer would call optimal? Kahneman & Lovallo are the only source that fixes a baseline (the pooled-portfolio value), and against that baseline their answer is that the net direction is indeterminate.

These are recorded as [[open-questions]] Q30–Q34.
