# Punctuated Equilibrium and the State

**One of a paired, rival-models set.** Read with [[gradual-institutional-change]], which states the opposite position, and with [[rival-models-of-institutional-change]], the open conflict between them. Neither page is the wiki's adopted position.

Krasner's 1984 review essay contrasts pluralist and "statist" theories of the state, then closes with his own synthesis: **punctuated equilibrium** as the model of how state institutions change — long stasis, broken by rapid crisis-driven reorganisation that sets a path, followed by re-rigidification until the next crisis. Three things must travel with the citation. It is a **metaphor explicitly borrowed from evolutionary biology** (Eldredge & Gould), and Krasner says so — "can serve as an appropriate metaphor", "to borrow a term from another discipline". It states **no ex ante criterion** for when a shock produces a punctuation rather than being absorbed. And his own strongest cited case data, Skowronek's periodisation of US state-building, **do not clearly support the "short burst" claim** they are offered to illustrate. The stability half of the argument is stronger than the change half: the sunk-cost mechanism is a genuine comparative-cost argument, not an assertion.

**Evidence tier.** (b) review essay contributing a theoretical metaphor, over (a) other people's case histories. Krasner presents no primary evidence. The six reviewed works are Nordlinger on state autonomy, Geertz on nineteenth-century Bali (interpretive ethnography), Skowronek on US administrative development 1877–1920 (archival institutional history — the strongest and most detailed of the set), Dahl's *Who Governs* (the pluralist foil, a single-city case study), Tilly's edited volume on European state formation, and Trimberger's four-case comparison of "revolutions from above". **No formal model, no cross-country regression, no natural experiment anywhere in this source.** The weakest evidence is illustrative quotation — a single 1983 Alaska secession-commission remark used to stand for enduring American localism.

**Unit of analysis: the state at whole-society level**, taken as a totality — bureaucratic apparatus plus institutionalised legal order. Krasner names bureaucratic-politics analysis as a *rival, lower* unit and rejects it: such approaches "parcel the state into little pieces, pieces that can be individually analyzed... and that float in a permissive environment". Where organisation-level entities appear — the Interstate Commerce Commission, the Civil Service Commission, the Bureau of the Budget, the General Accounting Office, the army against the National Guard — they are instances of the state-level story, not objects of independent organisational analysis. Everything here is one level up from this wiki's usual unit and needs translation before import.

## Why state structures persist between crises

**[framework — the stronger half of the argument]** Three mechanisms, kept distinct:

1. **Power holders select their own successors.** Elite and institutional self-reproduction; France's *grandes écoles* is the named case.
2. **Incumbents resist change that would undermine "budgetary support, policy scope, and personal status".** Quoting Skowronek, institutions become "self-perpetuating perversions of that purpose" — goal displacement with a self-interest mechanism attached.
3. **Sunk costs, as a comparative-cost argument.** This is the mechanism worth keeping. The sunk asset is not physical capital but **accumulated information, trust, and shared expectations** that make exchange and coordination cheap. New institutions would have to rebuild that stock from nothing. So "even if there is widespread societal dissatisfaction with a particular set of institutions, it may be irrational to change them", because the *variable* costs of continuing with the old exceed neither the fixed *nor* the variable costs of the new — only the sum. Institutional stasis is rational and suboptimal at once.

After a crisis, institutions "rigidify" and reproduce themselves "more [in response] to their own needs than to those of their domestic society or the international environment" until the next one. Krasner states this as the general pattern across all six reviewed studies, not as a case-specific finding.

**[wiki synthesis]** Mechanism 3 is structurally the same claim as North's increasing returns on [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]], reached independently and pitched at whole-state scale: each additional year of accumulated informational and relational capital raises the switching cost. It is a monotonic-with-age lock-in claim. It is also, like North's, given no threshold, no rate, and no falsification condition.

## What triggers a punctuation

**[framework — the weaker half]** Crisis, defined via Skowronek as "a sporadic, disruptive event that suddenly challenges a state's capacity to maintain control and alters the boundaries defining the legitimate use of coercion". Two pathways:

- **Pattern I — internally generated.** Domestic society and economy change faster than state institutions ("public stasis and private dynamism"), producing incongruence but no immediate forced adjustment. The running example is industrialisation outrunning the nineteenth-century American state.
- **Pattern II — externally generated.** International or military threat forces the state to raise extraction from society, provoking resistance. Prussia after Jena 1806; Russia after the Crimean War; Tilly's war → tax → bureaucracy chain; Trimberger's four revolutions from above.

Krasner ranks them, and the ranking is a claim rather than a typology: **"Such external pressures are almost certainly more compelling than internal ones."**

**Every mechanism of change in this source is exogenous-shock-driven.** There is no discussion anywhere of change arising from actors' incremental reinterpretation or layering of existing rules in the absence of a shock. Krasner does not carve out conditions under which gradual continuous change is expected instead, and he states the abrupt pattern as general: "Institutional change is episodic and dramatic rather than continuous and incremental." He explicitly rejects functional explanations that would predict smooth adjustment: "The state as administrative apparatus and legal order will not smoothly adjust to changes in its domestic environment." This makes him a clean, undiluted representative of the abrupt/exogenous pole — he hedges toward gradualism nowhere.

## The falsifiability problem, stated plainly

**[wiki synthesis, on the source's own materials]**

**No ex ante criterion for punctuation versus absorption.** Skowronek's crisis definition supplies qualitative descriptors — suddenness, disruption, a challenge to control, a shift in the boundaries of legitimate coercion — and no threshold of magnitude, duration or intensity that would let an observer classify an *unfolding* shock. Worse, whether "the boundaries of legitimate coercion" actually moved is typically only assessable after the fact, by looking at whether institutions changed — which is the outcome the framework exists to explain.

**No operational marker for a critical juncture.** No duration threshold, no magnitude threshold, no rule about admissible sources of shock. Source of shock cannot itself be the marker, because Krasner's own examples are heterogeneous: military defeat (Jena, Crimea), diffuse societal and economic drift (US industrialisation — not a discrete event at all), and elite-driven military coup (Trimberger's cases).

**Applied retrospectively throughout.** An episode is called a crisis or punctuation because institutional change is observed to have followed it; a period is called stasis because institutions are observed not to have changed. There is no case anywhere in the source where the theory is used to predict in advance that a given shock will or will not produce a punctuation. Krasner also does not engage the falsifiability critique that dogged Gould & Eldredge's biological punctuated equilibrium — he cites their work only for the gradualist/punctualist framing, so the source neither raises nor rebuts the objection. It imports the label.

**Net: this functions as a post-hoc classification scheme, not a predictive theory.** Before it could be pitted against a gradual/endogenous account on falsifiable terms, it would need an independent ex ante rule for what counts as a critical juncture, and this source supplies none.

## The durations, and how his own case undercuts them

**[wiki synthesis — the priority theme]** Krasner never gives a measurable duration for either phase. The language is uniformly qualitative: "short bursts of rapid institutional change followed by long period[s] of stasis"; "rapid change during periods of crisis followed by consolidation and stasis". No number of years, no decay rate, no marker for how long a punctuation lasts or how long re-solidification takes.

**The one place actual dates appear cuts against the framing.** Skowronek periodises US state-building as:

| Period | Length | Skowronek's characterisation | Krasner's implied phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1877–1900 | 23 years | "state building as patchwork" | stasis / pre-punctuation |
| 1900–1920 | 20 years | "a more powerful set of state institutions was created" | the punctuation |

The "punctuation" is **20 years** against a "stasis" of 23. It is not obviously shorter, and not obviously more sudden. Krasner does not reconcile this, and the case evidence he leans on hardest does not clearly support "punctuation" as brief relative to "equilibrium". Recorded as loose use of time, not as a refutation — the periodisation is Skowronek's and was not built to test Krasner's metaphor.

Lipset & Rokkan's three critical junctures (Reformation; Democratic Revolution after 1789; Industrial Revolution) are cited as the branching-tree exemplar with no duration for the juncture itself — only the claim that its *outcome* persists for centuries.

## Branching trees and economic structure

**[framework — three instances, all path-dependent rather than efficiency-selected]** Krasner's own summary of the mechanism: once a critical choice is made, "it cannot be taken back".

- **Tilly's war → tax → bureaucracy chain.** Military competition raises revenue need → higher taxation → an extensive bureaucracy to collect it → that extractive capacity, once built, is redeployed to non-military uses, and "extraordinary" war levies become permanent revenue streams. Institution creation as a byproduct of shock, not of design.
- **Sabel/Piore/Zeitlin on Fordism against flexible specialisation.** Mass production with unskilled labour on special-purpose machines was not technologically inevitable; flexible specialisation with skilled labour on general-purpose machines was a live alternative. The outcome was set by the initial distribution of property rights and income in Britain and the US, after which political and economic institutions co-evolved to lock in mass markets.
- **Braudel on free cities** outcompeting the state in medieval Italy, Flanders and Germany as the origin condition for Western capitalism — a small initial institutional difference with large long-run consequences for economic structure.

**[wiki synthesis]** Note what the second one does to scope. The Fordism case is about **firms and industrial organisation**, not the state, and Krasner imports the branching-tree logic into it without qualification. So the source contains an implicit public/private invariance claim for the *lock-in mechanism specifically* — smuggled in via a single non-state example rather than argued. Treat it as an open extension, not a demonstrated generality. See [[open-questions]] Q3.

## Statist against pluralist, and what the essay is arguing for

**[model]** The essay's frame is a contest of two conceptions, presented through Benjamin & Duvall's four-way taxonomy of "the state": state-as-government, state-as-bureaucratic-apparatus-and-legal-order, state-as-ruling-class, state-as-normative-order.

- **Statist view.** Decision rights sit with state officials whose selection and removal is internal to the apparatus — bureaucratic promotion, elite educational tracks — not through societal accountability channels. Officials' resources ("legality", administrative position) are conferred by the office rather than carried in from society. Institutional structures determine which combinations of resources are even possible: Krasner's example is that the same political will produces different outcomes depending on whether an apparatus like MITI exists to enact it.
- **Pluralist view**, steelmanned at length through Dahl. Decision rights are diffuse, held by a "political stratum" whose resources come mostly from society (jobs, information) and are carried in and out of office; leaders are constrained by shifting societal coalitions rather than by durable structure.
- **Marxism gets one dismissive clause** — "it will not be further considered here" — against roughly 1,500 words of sympathetic pluralist exposition. A real asymmetry in how seriously rival camps are treated, and worth recording as an ideological prior: Krasner is a leading exponent of the statist camp and cites his own prior application of it.

**Krasner's judgment:** pluralism understates institutional constraint; nominal accountability (elections, "the democratic creed") does less explanatory work than actual constraint by administrative apparatus and enduring beliefs.

**Nordlinger's three types of state autonomy**, which are the essay's cleanest power axis and are registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D59:

| Type | Condition |
|---|---|
| I | The state acts **against** divergent societal preference |
| II | The state **converts** divergent preference into convergent preference, then acts |
| III | The state acts under **pre-existing** non-divergence |

Skowronek's four dimensions for describing a state, also state-level and needing translation before use on a single organisation: concentration of authority at the national centre; penetration of institutional controls from the centre throughout the territory; centralisation of authority within the national government; specialisation of institutional tasks and roles within government.

## Failure modes named

Institutions as "self-perpetuating perversions of that purpose" — goal displacement with an explicit self-interest mechanism. The post-1920 US federal apparatus as a **"hapless administrative giant"** that "spawn[s] bureaucratic goods and services but... defie[s] authoritative control and direction", with agencies operating under "parallel sets of controls pitted against each other" and the military showing "a proliferation of semi-independent and competing power centers" rather than unified command. The ICC "gutted by a series of Supreme Court decisions" — judicial veto killing a regulator's effective mandate almost immediately after its creation.

**[wiki synthesis]** The "hapless giant" description is state-scale veto-point proliferation, and it is purely descriptive: Krasner offers no formal test of the veto-point/autonomy relationship. Compare [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]], where Hammond proves that the count alone predicts nothing once preference divergence varies. Do not read Krasner's narrative as evidence for a count-based account.

**No levers.** The essay names no design intervention, reform or recommendation anywhere. It closes with a research agenda — how do institutions perpetuate themselves, what is the duration of lags between environmental and institutional change, when do institutions fail to change even at risk of the polity's survival — rather than prescriptions. Recorded because the absence is itself informative: the wiki's third theory-heavy batch again supplies no playbook.

## Scope

Predominantly Western industrialised states (US 1789–1980s, France, Germany, Russia, Sweden and Britain on welfare-state diffusion), plus four non-Western "revolution from above" cases (Japan, Turkey, Egypt, Peru) and one pre-modern non-Western case (nineteenth-century Bali). Mostly nineteenth- and twentieth-century state formation and administrative development. The underlying comparative-politics literature is explicitly a 1970s–80s reaction against 1950s–60s American behaviouralism — a point Krasner makes carefully, noting that pluralism's dominance was partly an artefact of computing and statistical methods becoming available rather than purely of intellectual merit.

**Regime type is not restricted**, and that is notable: the punctuated-equilibrium claim spans democracies, nineteenth-century monarchies and military-bureaucratic authoritarian transitions without qualification.
