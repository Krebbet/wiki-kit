# Transaction Costs

Transaction costs are the mechanism the New Institutional Economics uses to link institutions to economic performance: institutions determine the cost of measuring what is being exchanged, of enforcing the terms, and of monitoring agents, and those costs determine which gains from trade are realisable at all. North's chain runs institutions → transaction and transformation costs → realised gains from trade → specialisation and division of labour → growth; where institutions fail to lower those costs, the result is not slower growth but a different equilibrium, typically redistribution and rent extraction rather than production. Libecap extends the same logic to the *political* process: it is the transaction costs of political bargaining, not of market bargaining, that most often block the definition and exchange of efficient property rights, and his oil, fisheries and groundwater cases put quantified magnitudes on the loss. The strongest caveat comes from inside the tradition: Libecap states outright that "transaction costs are not clear in aggregate studies of economies" — the evidence is far stronger at the case level than at the cross-country level.

**Evidence tier note.** North's material here is (b) theoretical framework illustrated by institutional history; the JEP essay presents no model, no regression, and no natural experiment, and offloads its largest comparative claims to earlier books. Libecap's paper has two layers that must not be merged: his summary of **North is second-hand** (a sympathetic retrospective, not new testing), while his **own** oil / fisheries / water case material is primary institutional-history evidence with quantified magnitudes drawn from his and coauthors' peer-reviewed work. Where Libecap is cited below, the layer is marked.

## The cost categories

**[framework — North]** North names four distinct costs that institutions raise or lower:

1. **Information / measurement costs** — the cost of ascertaining the attributes of what is traded and the performance of agents. His negative case is the Suq (North African / Middle Eastern bazaar), which he characterises as lacking "institutions devoted to assembling and distributing market information": no price quotations, no standardised weights and measures. His positive case is medieval and early-modern Europe developing printed price lists, exchange-rate manuals, and standardised weights, measures and units of account.
2. **Enforcement costs** — the cost of making terms bind. North treats enforcement as a gradient running from personal, kin-based and reputational enforcement, through voluntary merchant courts and codified mercantile law, to impersonal third-party enforcement by the state. He describes "the gradual blending of the voluntaristic structure of enforcement of contracts via internal merchant organizations with enforcement by the state" as an important part of the story — i.e. private and public enforcement are substitutes and complements on the same axis, not different kinds.
3. **Agency costs** — the cost of monitoring agents once trade scales beyond kin networks. Named twice in the JEP essay: "a classical problem of agency" in long-distance kin-based trade, and "recurrent crises over the problem of agency" in the Spanish colonial bureaucracy.
4. **Risk / uncertainty pricing** — converting uncertainty into actuarially priced risk, via marine insurance and portfolio diversification, so that large capital commitments become possible.

**[framework — North]** The institutional innovations he treats as the documented backbone of the argument are specific: usury-law evasion, the bill of exchange and its negotiability, standardised marine insurance, the commenda evolving into the joint-stock company, and merchant law with mercantile courts. These are the citation-backed part of the essay (De Roover 1945; Udovitch 1962; Lopez & Raymond 1955; Greif 1989) and are its strongest evidence.

## The transaction sector grows with the economy

**[empirical — one measurement, cited by North]** Wallis & North (1986): the share of US GNP absorbed by the transaction sector — resources spent coordinating exchange rather than transforming inputs — rose from roughly 25% in 1870 to 45% in 1970. North reads this as evidence that specialisation and economic maturation systematically increase the resource share consumed by transacting institutions.

**[wiki synthesis]** This cuts two ways and the source does not disambiguate. It is consistent with transacting institutions becoming *more valuable* as the economy specialises, and equally consistent with them becoming *more expensive per unit of coordination achieved*. A single measurement of a share cannot separate the two. Treat the number as a fact about resource allocation, not as evidence of institutional efficiency.

## Transaction costs are also, and mainly, political

**[primary case evidence — Libecap]** Libecap's contribution is to locate the binding transaction costs in the political arena. His mechanism chain:

1. A valuable, non-uniform, poorly-boundable resource generates positive transaction costs — heterogeneous parties cannot credibly value or verify each other's differential claims, and cannot cheaply monitor or enforce.
2. Private bargaining over new property rights therefore breaks down or drags for years to decades (unitization deals took 4–9 years to negotiate across seven Texas oil fields).
3. Parties facing concentrated losses turn to the political process.
4. Politicians and bureaucrats **are not residual claimants** on the resource rents, so they respond to the best-organised and most politically influential constituency — frequently the numerous small high-cost incumbents rather than the efficient low-cost producers.
5. The resulting regulatory substitute for property rights (prorationing, effort controls, revocable permits) preserves high-cost output, preserves the regulator's discretion, and leaves large persistent rent losses uncorrected.

Step 4's public-choice premise (politicians respond to concentrated organised interests over diffuse ones) is imported from Peltzman, Becker and Olson, not independently tested in this paper.

**[primary case evidence — Libecap] Quantified magnitudes:**

| Loss | Magnitude |
|---|---|
| US oil, excessive drilling, 1914 | ~25% of production value, ≈ $1.34bn in 2018 dollars |
| US oil prorationing costs (Adelman, 1960s) | ~$2bn per decade; ≈ $380bn present value over 10 years at 5%, 2018 prices |
| Global fisheries, inefficient effort (World Bank) | ~$80bn/year |
| Property-right insecurity | US ITQ lease/sale price ratio ≈ 2× New Zealand's, implying markets price in roughly a 50% probability of future erosion of the right |

That last row is the most transferable finding on the page: it is a **market-priced measure of institutional credibility**, and it generalises well beyond fisheries. See [[credible-commitment]].

**[primary case evidence — Libecap]** The within-source comparison is the cleanest causal material available: facing the same underlying unitization problem, the Texas Railroad Commission (elected officials, responsive to the Texas Independent Producers Association — thousands of small, high-cost, locally organised producers) retained unanimity rules, while Oklahoma's agency, facing weaker small-producer political power, relaxed unanimity at the behest of larger firms. Same problem, different organised-interest configuration, different institutional outcome.

## The failure that is not slow growth

**[framework — North]** North is explicit that institutional evolution has no default toward efficiency: "private profitability has been enhanced by creating monopolies, by restricting entry and factor mobility, and by political organizations that established property rights that redistributed rather than increased income." The alternative to productivity-raising institutions is not the absence of institutions but a functioning rent-extraction equilibrium. His structural-trap case is the Suq, where he argues the *missing complementary institutions* — legal structure, court system, and the political institutions that would create them — are what prevent voluntary organisations from evolving to reduce information asymmetry.

**[framework — Williamson, contrasting]** Williamson's discriminating-alignment hypothesis states that transactions are aligned with governance structures "so as to effect a (mainly) transaction cost economizing outcome" — a presumption of efficiency that North explicitly rejects. This is a genuine disagreement about what the theory predicts, not a difference of emphasis; see [[efficiency-of-institutions-north-vs-williamson]] and [[governance-structures]].

## The evidence problem, stated by the tradition itself

**[empirical caveat — Libecap, self-flagged]** Libecap's abstract concedes that "transaction costs are not clear in aggregate studies of economies." The transaction-cost account is well supported at the level of a bounded resource with a traceable bargaining history and a documented regulatory response; it does not show up cleanly in cross-country regressions. He is also explicit that extrapolating from natural-resource sectors (US fisheries are ~0.01% of GDP, chosen precisely for tractability) to developed economies in general is conditional: "*If* the observed costly political response to open access losses is characteristic of regulation in general, then welfare losses permeate developed economies as well."

**[wiki synthesis]** This is the honest state of the evidence and bears directly on [[open-questions]] Q15. The micro-mechanism is well documented; the macro claim that institutional quality drives national growth rests, in these sources, on narrative comparative history (North) plus an explicit admission that the aggregate statistical record is unclear (Libecap).
