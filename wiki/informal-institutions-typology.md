# Informal Institutions Typology

Helmke & Levitsky supply this job's anchor framework: **informal institutions are "socially shared rules, usually unwritten, that are created, communicated, and enforced outside of officially sanctioned channels."** Their four-way typology crosses two binary conditions — whether the relevant *formal* institution is effective or ineffective, and whether actors' goals served by the informal rule are compatible or conflicting with what the formal rule is meant to produce — yielding **complementary, accommodating, competing, and substitutive** informal institutions, each with a precise defining criterion rather than a loose family resemblance.

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** — a conceptual/typological paper (2003 Kellogg working paper, later *Perspectives on Politics* 2004), not a hypothesis-testing empirical study. All case material is illustrative citation to others' single-case studies, which the authors themselves flag as a methodological limit of the field: research here is "either abstract theory (N=0) or single case studies (N=1)."

## The typology, exactly

**[model]**

| | Effective Formal Institutions | Ineffective Formal Institutions |
|---|---|---|
| **Compatible Goals** | **Complementary** — fill gaps left by formal rules, without violating them | **Substitutive** — achieve the outcome formal institutions were meant to but failed to produce |
| **Conflicting Goals** | **Accommodating** — violate the *spirit*, not the letter, of formal rules; a second-best strategy | **Competing** — structure incentives incompatible with formal rules; to follow one, actors must violate the other |

**What informal institutions are NOT**, stated precisely to prevent the category becoming a residual catch-all: not *weak institutions* (formal rules ignored has to mean a *competing* rule actually structures behavior, or it's just non-institutional non-compliance); not mere *behavioral regularities* (removing a hat in church, unlike removing a coat in a restaurant, "responds to an established rule... the violation of which generates some kind of external sanction" — the latter is comfort-seeking, not norm-following); not *culture* broadly (informal institutions are defined via shared *expectations*, not shared *values*); not *informal organizations* (clans, mafias — following North, rules are separated from players).

## Origins and change — a direct complication of "informal = slow"

**[model]** Informal institutions split into **reactive** (endogenous, created in direct response to formal-rule incentives — filling gaps, mitigating effects, substituting, subverting) and **spontaneous** (independent of, often predating, formal structures — custom law, kinship norms). This split predicts *different* change dynamics: reactive institutions (complementary, accommodating, competing) track changes in formal *design*, often rapidly (their own example: the 1974 US House committee-rule change); substitutive and competing institutions track changes in formal *effectiveness*; only spontaneous institutions are genuinely slow-moving by default, via cultural evolution — and even these can "tip" rapidly via belief-updating and coordination cascades (their example: the end of Chinese foot-binding). **[wiki synthesis]** This directly complicates any blanket claim that informal constraints change more slowly than formal ones (see [[fast-and-slow-moving-institutions]]) — the blanket claim only holds for the spontaneous subtype, and even there, not always.

## Registered as a new dimension

**[wiki synthesis]** The 2x2 is directly scoreable: for any informal practice attached to a formal institution, code (i) whether the relevant formal rule is currently effective/enforced, and (ii) whether the actors' goals served by the informal practice converge or diverge from what the formal rule is meant to produce.
