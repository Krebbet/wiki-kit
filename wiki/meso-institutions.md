# Meso-Institutions

Claude Ménard proposes **meso-institutions** — regulatory agencies, public bureaus, protocols, guidelines, and private coordination devices such as Interbranch Organizations — as a third analytical layer sitting between **macro-institutions** (systemic rules and norms; North/Davis-North's "institutional environment") and **micro-institutions** (individual actors, organizations and transactions; Williamson's "institutional arrangements"). The claim is that macro rules do not translate into micro outcomes on their own: something has to **translate** them to local or sectoral context, **monitor** compliance, and **enforce** it, and that something is the meso layer. Where it is absent, functionally incomplete, or fragmented across competing bodies, macro policy underperforms at the point of contact with micro actors; where it works, transaction costs fall and coordination improves.

**Evidence caveat, stated up front.** This page is built entirely from Ménard's introduction to a *Journal of Institutional Economics* symposium — a framework essay, not an empirical paper. The case material it cites (Brazilian Forest Code implementation, the Italian tomato supply chain, EU bird-conservation policy in the Netherlands, urban water provision across seven Asian cities) is **Ménard's own secondhand paraphrase of five companion papers this wiki has not read**. Every "finding" attributed to those cases below is one remove from the underlying data, several are explicitly labelled by their own authors as untested "conjectures," and none carries an experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy in what this source reports. The whole page is therefore marked predominantly `**[model]**`; nothing here is tagged `[empirical]`, because this wiki has not verified any of the underlying data itself.

## The layer distinction and its lineage

**[model]** Ménard's macro/micro starting point is the same dichotomy already on this wiki at [[what-is-an-institution]]: North's institutional environment (the systemic rules of the game) versus Williamson's institutional arrangements (governance structures for individual transactions). His move is to insert a third layer for the devices that carry a macro rule down to the micro level: regulatory authorities and public bureaus on the public side, Interbranch Organizations and comparable contract-based coordination bodies on the private side. He cites Acemoglu & Johnson's "Unbundling Institutions" (2005) as a direct intellectual forerunner motivating the project. **Note on provenance:** this wiki does not hold "Unbundling Institutions" itself — it is a different paper from the AJR corpus already represented here at [[institutions-as-fundamental-cause]] (the Handbook chapter on economic institutions as fundamental cause). The forerunner citation is Ménard's characterization, not independently checked against the 2005 paper.

**[model]** Also cited, per the summary: Williamson (2000) differentiating institutional layers by time span — echoing this wiki's existing interest in institutional layers as sitting at different temporal remove from micro action, cf. [[governance-structures]].

## The three-function decomposition

**[model]** A meso-institution's job is decomposed into three functions, presented as the test of whether it is doing its job:

1. **Translation / interpretation / adaptation** — converting a systemic rule into something workable in a specific local or sectoral context.
2. **Monitoring** — checking whether the rule is actually being followed.
3. **Enforcement** — sanctioning non-compliance.

Ménard treats these as jointly necessary: gaps in any one produce implementation failure independent of the macro rule's own text. The Brazilian Forest Code case (de Oliveira & Schnaider, secondhand) is offered as an illustration — two state-level meso-institutions failing to fully perform translation/monitoring/enforcement, compounded by federalism, is presented as explaining cross-state compliance variation more than a decade after adoption. As with all case material on this page, treat this as Ménard's paraphrase of a paper this wiki has not read, not as verified evidence.

## Polycentricity and the multiplicity problem

**[model]** Ménard reports that "almost all cases" in the symposium involve **multiple** meso-institutions in the same domain, producing overlapping or competing mandates and a horizontal coordination problem on top of the vertical translate/monitor/enforce problem. This is presented as an extension of Ostrom's polycentricity concept — [[polycentric-governance]] — to the meso layer specifically, and Pascucci (2025, cited secondhand) is described as explicitly combining the meso-institutions frame with Ostrom's socio-ecological-systems framework and a **subsidiarity** principle (delegate decision rights to the organization or community closest to the problem). Structurally, multiple overlapping meso-institutions in one domain looks close to veto-point proliferation as covered at [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]] — Hammond's finding there is that veto-point *count alone* predicts nothing without preference divergence, which is a useful check on any temptation to read "more meso-institutions" as automatically worse; Ménard's source does not test this interaction, so the two claims sit adjacent, not merged.

**[model]** A related, narrower contrast: Splinter & Dries (Netherlands bird conservation, cited secondhand) report that shifting from centralized bureaucratic monitoring/enforcement to a decentralized, voluntary-contract-based collective mechanism improved local adaptation to an EU-level conservation law. This is one mixed-method single-country case, not a controlled comparison, and is not framed by the source itself as a general claim that decentralization beats centralization — only that it scored better on the local-adaptation margin in this one instance.

## Public/private invariance

**[model]** Ménard explicitly treats public regulatory agencies/bureaus and private Interbranch Organizations as **structurally equivalent** instances of the same meso-institutional category, both performing the same translate/monitor/enforce functions — an Interbranch Organization is characterized as "a complex set of contracts deeply embedded in a macro-institutional set of rules." This is a direct, explicit public/private invariance claim, relevant to this wiki's running interest in what is and isn't invariant across that divide (cf. Dixit's "of degree, not kind" claim at [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]], not verified against this source directly). But the support offered is weak: one public-sector example (regulatory agencies/bureaus) and one private-sector example (the Italian tomato-sector IBO, Martino & Polenzani, cited secondhand) from different countries and sectors, juxtaposed rather than compared within a matched design. Treat the invariance claim itself as `**[model]**`, corroborated only loosely by parallel description, not demonstrated.

## Economic production: the transaction-cost claim

**[model]** The IBO case is used to argue that meso-institutions lower the transaction costs of coordinating actors across a supply chain — a direct application of Williamsonian transaction-cost logic (see [[transaction-costs]]) to a new institutional form sitting above the individual firm. The mechanism: the IBO performs translation/monitoring/enforcement of EU/Italian rules under a "subsidiarity principle," reducing what individual supply-chain participants would otherwise have to negotiate and verify bilaterally. Evidence base, per the summary: one process-tracing single-sector case study, described by its own authors as supporting a "conjecture," not a tested causal claim — this is markedly thinner than the case-level evidence [[transaction-costs]] already carries from Libecap's oil/fisheries/water work, and should not be read as adding to that evidence base, only as an application of the same logic to an unmeasured new case.

## Failure modes named

**[model]**
- **Absence of a needed meso-institution** — outcome quality tracks the presence, flaws, or absence of the meso layer (water-provision case, Jensen & Menard, secondhand — the strongest-design case in the batch per the summary: a structured 7-city comparison with a pre-tested instrument, still not independently verified by this wiki).
- **Functional gaps** — partial fulfillment of translation, monitoring, or enforcement (Brazilian Forest Code case, above).
- **Polycentric overlap/competition** — multiple meso-institutions with overlapping or competing mandates, adjacent to the veto-point-proliferation failure mode at [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]].
- **Rigidity of centralized bureaucratic monitoring** relative to more adaptive collective/voluntary mechanisms — implied, not named as "failure" by the source itself (Splinter & Dries, above).

## What the source does not supply

**[wiki synthesis]** No accountability chain: the source asserts enforcement as a defining *function* of a meso-institution but never characterizes *who selects or removes* the people running one, or the strength/formality of accountability, as a *dimension* of variation. No scale or age data anywhere — no headcount, budget, hierarchy-depth, or years-since-founding for any meso-institution discussed. No quantified transaction-cost magnitude comparable to Libecap's figures at [[transaction-costs]]. And a notable pattern flagged in the underlying summary: as editor of the symposium he introduces, Ménard reports uniformly positive/confirmatory results for every article cited, with no null or mixed finding surfaced — a self-citation-heavy, single-author framing risk of the same kind already flagged for Frey at [[motivation-crowding]].
