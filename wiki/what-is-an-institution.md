# What Is an Institution

Three foundational sources give three different answers, and the difference is not terminological. North defines institutions as the humanly devised rules — formal rules, informal constraints, and the enforcement characteristics of both — and separates them sharply from *organizations*, the players who act inside them. Ostrom defines them as *rules-in-use*: the working rules that structure a specific action situation, decomposable into seven rule types and applying identically to markets, firms, families, legislatures and agencies. Williamson does not define institutions at all; his unit is the *transaction*, and a firm is simply "the inclusive set of transactions for which the decision is to make rather than buy" — a governance structure, derived rather than primitive. All three converge on the idea that an institution is a constraint structure that changes the cost of interacting, and that enforcement is part of the definition rather than an add-on. They diverge on the object being analysed: a whole society's rule-set (North), a meso-level governance regime for a definable resource or service (Ostrom), and a bundle of transactions under a common governance mode (Williamson). This page states the three definitions, marks where they are and are not compatible, and takes a first pass at [[open-questions]] Q1.

**Evidence tier note.** Everything below is either (b) theoretical framework or (c) wiki synthesis. None of the four sources behind this page is an empirical identification study; they are theory statements and histories of thought. Ostrom's lecture is the only one that reports systematic empirical work, and none of that work bears on the *definition* — it bears on which institutional features predict performance (see [[polycentric-governance]]).

## North: rules of the game vs. players

**[framework]** North's definition, stated verbatim in the 1994 framework paper: institutions "consist of formal rules, informal constraints (norms of behavior, conventions, and self imposed codes of conduct) and the enforcement characteristics of both." Enforcement is carried out by the first party (self-imposed codes), the second party (retaliation), or a third party (societal sanction or state coercion). The degree to which behaviour actually matches the rule depends on the effectiveness of that enforcement — so a rule with no enforcement is not, on this definition, doing institutional work.

Against this he sets organizations: "groups of individuals engaged in purposive activity" — a firm, a political party, a regulatory agency, a school or college. Institutions are the rules of the game; organizations are the players. The framework paper is explicit that the two are causally coupled in one direction and then feed back: the institutional matrix defines the opportunity set, which determines which organizations come into existence and what skills they invest in. North's own illustration: "If the highest rates of return in a society are to be made from piracy, then organizations will invest in knowledge and skills that will make them better pirates."

A third level sits above both: the *institutional matrix* or framework — the whole interlocking system of formal rules, informal constraints and enforcement for a society.

**[wiki synthesis]** The 1991 JEP essay, which is the more widely cited statement, never actually defines the distinction, and North moves between the economy-wide level ("the institutional framework of western Europe"), the level of specific institutional instruments (bill of exchange, commenda, marine insurance, merchant courts), and the level of organizations (merchant guilds, trading companies, the crown, colonial bureaucracies) within single paragraphs, without flagging the shift. Anyone building on the JEP essay has to record which level a given claim operates at; the essay will not do it for them.

## Ostrom: rules-in-use

**[framework]** Ostrom's object is the working rule-set that structures a particular *action situation*, analysed through the IAD (Institutional Analysis and Development) framework, which is explicitly built to move between nested levels: the individual action situation, the governance regime for a single resource (an irrigation system, a fishery, a forest, a metropolitan police department), the coupled resource-plus-governance-plus-users social-ecological system, and meta-analytic aggregation across many instances of the same kind.

The decisive move is decomposition. Rules-in-use are not a single thing but seven types (the "grammar of institutions", Crawford & Ostrom 2005), each a separate design axis: boundary rules (who may enter or exit a position), position rules (what positions exist and how many hold each), choice rules (which actions attach to which position), information rules (channels and mandated disclosure), scope rules (which outcomes may be affected), aggregation rules (how individual choices map to collective outcomes — majority, unanimity), and payoff rules (how benefits and costs are distributed across positions). Empirical coding found 27 variants of boundary rules and 112 of choice rules in the field, which is itself an argument against treating "the institution" as an atom.

Ostrom's framework is explicitly claimed to span the sectors that North's vocabulary keeps apart: the same IAD structural language applies to markets, firms, families, community organizations, legislatures and agencies alike.

## Williamson: governance structures

**[framework]** Williamson refuses the question. Following Commons ("the ultimate unit of activity … is a transaction") and Coase, he states flatly: "The unit of analysis in the transaction cost economics setup is the transaction." A *governance structure* — market, hybrid, or hierarchy — is a syndrome of three discrete attributes (incentive intensity, administrative command and control, contract law regime) against which transactions are aligned. The firm is what you get when you aggregate the transactions for which the make decision beat the buy decision; it is not an ontological primitive. See [[governance-structures]].

The consequence is that a corporation, on Williamson's account, is not a *kind of thing* at all — it is a boundary drawn around a set of transactions by an alignment calculation. That is a genuinely different object from North's "organization" and from Ostrom's "governance regime."

## Where they agree

**[wiki synthesis]** Three points of real convergence, not verbal overlap:

1. **Institutions are constraint structures that alter the cost of interaction.** North: institutions determine transaction and transformation costs. Williamson: governance modes differ in their costs and competences. Ostrom: rules-in-use determine the payoff structure of an action situation.
2. **Enforcement is constitutive, not supplementary.** North builds enforcement characteristics into the definition. Ostrom's design principles put monitoring and graduated sanctions at the centre and find local monitoring the strongest single correlate of good resource condition. Williamson makes the *contract law regime* — court-enforced legal rules versus private ordering and forbearance — one of only three attributes that define a governance mode.
3. **The explanatory variable is the rule-set, not the legal label.** Ostrom states this most sharply and backs it: officially protected government forests showed no statistical difference in density from non-protected forests (Hayes 2006, 76 vs. 87 forests). Williamson's remediableness criterion is explicitly designed to apply "in any sector whatsoever — public, private, nonprofit, etc." North applies the same transaction-cost logic to merchant self-enforcement and to state courts, and describes their "gradual blending."

## Where they are talking about different objects

**[wiki synthesis]** Take one institution — a federal agency — and run it through all three:

| Source | What the agency *is* | What varies |
|---|---|---|
| North | An **organization**: a player, not a rule. The rules are the statutes, administrative law, and norms it operates under. | Its behaviour is downstream of the institutional matrix that created its opportunity set. |
| Ostrom | A **governance regime**: a bundle of rules-in-use (boundary, position, choice, information, scope, aggregation, payoff rules) structuring a set of action situations. | All seven rule types independently. |
| Williamson | A **hierarchy**: the set of transactions internalised under unified ownership, with weak incentive intensity, strong fiat, and forbearance rather than court-enforced law. | Where the make-or-buy boundary sits, given asset specificity and disturbance frequency. |

These are not three descriptions of the same thing at different resolutions. North's account makes the agency a *consequence* of institutions; Ostrom's makes it an *instance* of them; Williamson's makes it a *derived aggregate* of transactions. A wiki page that says "institution" without saying which of these it means is ambiguous in a way that matters for what it predicts.

## Q1: is there a minimal definition?

**[wiki synthesis — first pass, not settled]** The question in [[open-questions]] Q1 is whether one definition covers a federal agency, a corporation, and a village irrigation association without becoming vacuous.

A candidate assembled from the three sources: *an institution is a durable set of rules-in-use, together with the enforcement that makes them bind, structuring repeated interaction among a defined set of positions.* It is minimal in that it names only three components — rules, enforcement, positions — and each is separately observable. Test:

- **Federal agency** — passes: enabling statute plus internal procedure (rules), inspectors general, courts, appropriations and appointment power (enforcement), a defined position structure.
- **Corporation** — passes: charter, bylaws, employment contracts (rules), fiat plus forbearance law, with courts declining to hear intrafirm disputes (enforcement), a defined position structure.
- **Village irrigation association** — passes, and Ostrom's Nepal cases show the enforcement leg can be entirely informal (local monitoring by users, graduated sanctions) with 25% of coded fishery subgroups having no formal organization at all.

What the candidate costs: it dissolves North's institution/organization boundary. On this definition a chartered organization *is* an institution, because its charter and internal procedure are a rule-set with enforcement. North needs that boundary because his causal story runs from rules to players; if players are also rules, the arrow is not well-defined. Ostrom does not need the boundary and does not maintain it. Williamson has no use for either category.

**What would settle it:** the unit that carries the predictive load. If the behaviour of a given body is better predicted by its rule-set than by the identity and selection of the people in it, the rules-in-use definition is the right primitive and North's separation is a modelling convenience. If the reverse, North's separation is load-bearing and the minimal definition above is too permissive. That is [[open-questions]] Q4, and none of these four sources tests it. Q1 is therefore moved to `partial`, not `answered`.
