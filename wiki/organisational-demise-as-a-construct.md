# Organisational Demise as a Construct

Searing (2020) is a small (n=8), qualitative comparative case study of mental-health nonprofits in upstate New York, and its value to this wiki is not its sample size but its central finding: **organisational "death" is not the clean, binary, dateable event that hazard-rate and event-history models (including [[liability-of-newness-empirical-hazard-evidence]]) implicitly assume.** Of eight organisations selected to balance "alive" and "not alive" at intake, only one of the five non-"alive" cases was a clean closure. The rest fell into three intermediate categories Searing proposes as a replacement for the field's usual binary: **Resurrected**, **Reincarnated**, and — most usefully named — **Zombie**.

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]**, n=8, qualitative, non-random ("most similar" case selection), single subsector (mental health, NTEE code F) and geography (three upstate NY cities). The author states the sample's substantive findings should not be generalised beyond mental-health nonprofits; the **classification scheme** (Alive/Resurrected/Reincarnated/Zombie/Dead) is offered as more broadly portable than the substantive results, and that is the distinction this wiki should preserve when citing it.

## The typology

**[empirical]**
- **Alive** — operational as of the study date.
- **Zombie** — *"a nonprofit that has not formally disbanded. However, the organization no longer attempts to pursue its mission... the 'body' of the organization is still there, but the organization is no longer accomplishing a purpose."*
- **Resurrected** — the same legal entity (same EIN, same mission) loses and regains tax-exempt status.
- **Reincarnated** — most of an organisation disbands and re-forms as a new entity with a similar purpose and largely the same actors. Searing narrows this relative to Hager et al.'s (1996) broader use, which had also included mergers, acquisitions, relocations and tax-status changes.
- **Dead** — disbanded, no longer functions in any form.

Against Hager's (1999) prior binary-leaning benchmark (37 of 73 exits were clean closures, about half), Searing's sample shows far more ambiguity: **"only one of the five nonprofits that was deemed 'not fully alive' was a clear closure."**

## Why this matters for mortality-hazard evidence elsewhere in the wiki

**[wiki synthesis]** [[liability-of-newness-empirical-hazard-evidence]] measures death via self-reported disengagement in a panel of entrepreneurial ventures — a more behaviourally grounded measure than a registry-lapse proxy, but still a binary "still operating / not" coding. Searing's finding does not invalidate that measure, but it does mean any wiki page citing an organisational mortality *rate* should carry the caveat that the underlying "death" count may conflate genuinely dissolved organisations with ones that persist in a zombie state (formally alive, functionally defunct — which a self-report survival panel would likely code as "alive") or that reincarnate under a new legal shell (which the same panel would likely code as "dead," understating true mission/function continuity). Both directions of misclassification are possible, and neither is quantified by any source this wiki currently holds. This is the caveat attached to [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D124**.

## What actually predicted status in this sample

**[empirical]**, qualitative coding of 8 semi-structured interviews (55–110 minutes each, Feb–Mar 2015), analysed via Miles & Huberman's process (98 open codes → 6 axial meta-codes: Mission, Management & Governance, Conflict, Networks, Financial Resources, Role of Government):

- **Internal conflict** tracked mortality: the two interviewees reporting the highest internal conflict were both from fully-or-mostly-dead organisations; the two reporting no internal conflict were both fully alive.
- **External conflict** tracked survival in the opposite direction: *"the presence of external conflict may serve as a galvanizing force for the organization since all nonprofits that were able to clearly be marked as 'alive' experienced some form of external conflict."*
- **Mission-target completion** — nonprofits whose mission targeted a specific regulation were more likely to close via "mission completion" when the target resolved, but also more likely to reincarnate under a related target if other conditions were favourable.
- **Financial resource dependence**, the traditional focus of the nonprofit-demise literature, played a *smaller* role than expected: financial distress looked more symptomatic of governance/mission/human-capital problems than a root cause in this sample. Government funding dependency was sometimes used by organisations to **build** legitimacy and networks against a powerful funder — the opposite of the standard "victim of dependency" resource-dependence-theory reading.
