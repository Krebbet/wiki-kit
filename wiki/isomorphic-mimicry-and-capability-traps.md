# Isomorphic Mimicry and Capability Traps

Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock's diagnosis: state-capability reforms in developing countries routinely fail because governments and donors settle into **isomorphic mimicry** — adopting the *form* of best-practice institutions (laws, org charts, procedures, indicator scores) to secure external legitimacy and funding, without building the *function*. Because legitimacy and resource flows are decoupled from demonstrated performance, nobody has an incentive to build real capability, and the result is a self-reinforcing **capability trap** in which capability stagnates or deteriorates for decades while reform rhetoric and development resources continue to flow. The paper then proposes **Problem-Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA)** as the alternative. **Diagnosis and prescription have starkly different evidence bases and this page keeps them apart: the diagnosis rests on roughly five hand-picked cases with no base rate anywhere in the paper, and the prescription is explicitly untested by the authors at time of publication.**

**Evidence tier: (b) conceptual synthesis illustrated by a small number of qualitative cases.** In full, what the paper actually offers: procurement reform in Liberia, Afghanistan and Sudan, described narratively and not quantified; African public financial management reform, citing Andrews (2011), an empirical study **not reproduced here in numeric detail**; one anonymised African education ministry (a sixth donor project in a string over 20 years, capability still absent — n = 1); Afghanistan state-building (a decade, ~2,000 US deaths, over $500bn, a state the authors judge likely *less* capable than Afghanistan's in the 1970s — one dramatic case); and a single footnoted quantitative reference, Denizer, Kaufmann & Kraay (2011) on ~6,000 World Bank projects, cited only to show that implementation-quality measures correlate with project outcomes generally — **not** evidence about the prevalence of isomorphic mimicry. **No count, percentage or systematic sample of how many reform programmes exhibit mimicry or traps appears anywhere in the paper.**

**Selection on the dependent variable: confirmed, and the authors do not address it.** The failure cases are chosen to motivate the argument; cases where donor-driven best-practice reform *did* build genuine capability are not reported or discussed, other than a single unexplained mention of South Korea as an "obvious spectacular success... notwithstanding". This is the mirror image of the problem in [[experimentalist-governance]] and [[ambidexterity-as-a-dynamic-capability]]: those two examine successes and no failures, this one examines failures and essentially no successes. See [[open-questions]] Q72.

## Diagnosis: the mechanism

**[b — a genuine mechanism claim, running through incentives, not a correlation]** The chain, in the authors' order:

1. Donors and international financial institutions tie funding and legitimacy to the adoption of externally defined "best practice" — laws, procedures, organisational structures — decided ex ante and evaluated on short, visible timelines.
2. This creates **scripts**: the Worldwide Governance Indicators, Doing Business, PEFA. They define what counts as legitimate reform and they score **inputs and compliance**, not outcomes.
3. Local leaders and organisations gain legitimacy, funding and career advancement by adopting the forms, regardless of whether the forms fit the context.
4. Because legitimacy and resources are decoupled from performance, there is no incentive — and often no possibility — to build actual capability.
5. Repeated across many projects, this aggregates into a **capability trap**, self-reinforcing because once sprung, no single agent, organisation or leader has an incentive to break out unilaterally.

The analysis is explicitly three-level and treats the interaction as the point: **agents** (frontline workers choosing compliance or malfeasance vs. positive performance; leaders choosing private gain vs. public value), **organisations** (balancing isomorphic pressure to look compliant against demonstrating real performance), and the **ecosystem** of donors, regulators and funders that rewards either agenda-conformity or functionality and is either closed or open to novelty. The cost of that design choice is that it is hard to say which level is doing the causal work in any given case.

**[c — wiki synthesis] Where this sits in the wiki's existing vocabulary.** "Isomorphic mimicry" is borrowed and adapted from DiMaggio & Powell — see [[institutional-isomorphism]] — and the decoupling of formal structure from actual activity is Meyer & Rowan's central object, see [[institutional-myths-and-decoupling]]. What Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock add is a *funding* mechanism: mimicry here is not merely legitimacy-seeking in a field, it is the rational response to a resource flow conditioned on visible form. The mechanism is also, unmistakably, a multitasking result in a domain that does not cite Holmstrom & Milgrom — donors reward the measurable and visible (laws passed, agencies created, indicator scores) and the unmeasured dimension gets no effort. See [[multitask-incentive-theory]].

**Distinguish from [[red-tape]]:** Bozeman's red tape is rules that impose burden and achieve nothing; isomorphic mimicry is form adopted *because* an external audience rewards the form. Different causes, sometimes the same artefact.

## Scale, age, and why the trap does not wear off

**[b — asserted, illustrated]**

- **Scale amplifies it.** "Effects are amplified where the organization or field undergoing change is large, deconcentrated and informal" — larger, more distributed organisations have harder diffusion, more heterogeneous interests among distributed agents, and top-down reform works progressively worse as scale rises.
- **Repetition does not erode it.** The African education ministry (six large projects, 20 years, objective never achieved) and Afghanistan (a decade of intensive, well-resourced effort with capability arguably regressing below a 1970s baseline) are offered as evidence that repeated reform cycles do not accumulate into capability and may entrench the trap, because continued isomorphic "success" removes the pressure to change. **This is a claim that the age of a reform effort does not predict escape.** It is two cases.

## Power: who the state is actually accountable to

**[b]** Nominal accountability runs to citizens and legislatures. Actual accountability, on this argument, runs to donors and IFIs who control the legitimacy and resource flows that determine organisational survival, and decision rights over what counts as legitimate reform sit with the "development community" via conditionality and indicator regimes. Frontline workers are structurally excluded from the reform conversation and have no incentive to contribute. The authors also turn the analysis on the World Bank itself: "certain organizational stakeholders have the power to veto actual or potential changes" to Bank practice (citing the 1992 Wapenhans Report) — one sentence, one citation, offered as a reason mainstream development organisations struggle to reform their own operating model despite being staffed by, in the authors' words, "amazingly dedicated and intelligent people". The critique is from inside the establishment, not an outside attack: they credit the standard donor model with "resounding, unqualified success" on logistical tasks.

**[b] The task distinction that bounds the whole argument.** Donor-led approaches "nearly always succeed" on **logistical** tasks — scaling a technologically known solution with low implementation discretion: roads, schools, sanitation, vaccination — and on removing distortive policy (foreign-exchange rationing) by conditionality. They founder on **state-capability building**, which requires high-discretion, face-to-face, context-specific implementation. Any use of this page's diagnosis has to carry that boundary with it.

## Prescription: PDIA, explicitly untested by its own authors

**[b — and the authors say so]** The four principles:

1. **Problem-driven, not solution-selling** — start from a locally nominated and defined performance problem, decomposed with tools like the "5 Whys" or Ishikawa diagrams, rather than importing a packaged best-practice solution. The procurement example is the illustration: sole-source contracting is linked to corruption, cost and quality problems, but a single competitive-bidding law is explicitly rejected as *the* lever, because it is one branch of the cause tree among several.
2. **An "authorizing environment"** (Moore 1995) that permits experimentation and "positive deviance", favouring small, cheap, quick incremental steps — "bricolage" — over one optimal solution.
3. **Tight, iterative feedback loops** for real-time experiential learning, distinguished by the authors from both traditional M&E (long lag, end-of-project) and RCT-style field experiments (which hold context fixed by design).
4. **Broad engagement across agents** — leaders, technical staff, frontline implementers — to make reforms viable and legitimate, since diffusion fails under centralised top-down control (citing Jin, Kim & Srivastava 1998 on decision centrality lowering intra-organisational diffusion rates).

**The evidentiary status, in the authors' own words.** "Our aim beyond this article is to use PDIA methods in particular interventions, and to gather accounts of where they may already have been introduced, the better to learn from the grounded experiences of others." PDIA is forward-looking, self-described as "tentative", and **had not been tested at time of writing**. Its supporting concepts are imported by resemblance from domains with separate, non-institutional evidence bases: learning organisations (Senge, private-sector management theory), positive deviance (Marsh et al. 2004, nutrition research in Vietnam), muddling through (Lindblom 1959), commons governance (Ostrom), institutional entrepreneurship. The parallel approaches named as validation-by-resemblance — Cash on Delivery aid, iScale, World Bank results-based financing — were themselves new and unevaluated then.

**[c — wiki synthesis]** So the page's asymmetry is deliberate: the diagnosis has cases and a coherent incentive mechanism, and the prescription has neither. In [[reform-levers]] the four PDIA principles sit in the pure-prescription tier for exactly this reason, quoting the authors' own framing. Note also the internal tension nobody in the paper resolves: principle 2 recommends quick wins and small steps, while [[political-economy-of-public-sector-reform]] reports from its own Ghana and Tanzania cases that quick wins do not reliably cascade into broader reform.

## The corroborating gap, from a different source

**[c — wiki synthesis; a mechanism-level connection, not a citation conflict]** Bunse & Fritz's 75%-vs-47% gap — three-quarters of World Bank operations rated successful against under half of countries showing genuine transformation, attributed by those authors to selecting politically easy, non-rent-threatening components — looks like an instance of exactly what is diagnosed here: **measured success tracking form-compliance rather than function.** The two sources are independent and neither tests the other; what the pairing supplies is a quantified version, from inside a donor institution, of a gap Andrews et al. can only illustrate. It corroborates the *diagnosis*. It supplies nothing at all to PDIA.

## What is not established

- **No prevalence, anywhere.** How common is isomorphic mimicry? The paper does not say, and its evidence could not answer.
- **No public/private claim.** Entirely developing-country states, ministries and agencies engaged with international donors. The borrowed concept (DiMaggio & Powell) was formulated across organisational fields including private ones; this application is 100% public-sector. Silent on whether capability traps or PDIA apply to firms.
- **No developed-country claim.** Explicitly not extended to OECD public sectors.
- **South Korea is named as an exception and not analysed** — the one place where the argument invites a test and does not take it.
