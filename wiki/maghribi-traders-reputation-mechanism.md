# Maghribi Traders' Reputation Mechanism

Greif's account of eleventh-century Mediterranean Jewish merchant agency relations is the canonical case of an informal institution substituting entirely for absent formal enforcement. Merchants hired overseas agents whose true conduct (prices, damages, bribes) was unobservable; courts could not verify agents' claims or track down an emigrated cheat, and litigation was slow and legally constrained. The **coalition** — a closed, identity-bounded network of traders — solved this not through ethics or internalized honesty but through **multilateral reputation**: any coalition member who caught an agent cheating shared that information with the whole network, and the whole network permanently refused to employ the cheater, not just the wronged merchant.

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]**, from Cairo Geniza documents — merchant letters, contracts, and dispute records rediscovered in a Fustat synagogue — cited by archive and document number throughout. The reputation/coalition logic itself is presented verbally, not as a formalised game with explicit payoffs (Greif directs readers to companion working papers for that); treat the mechanism as **[model]** and the letters as the **[empirical]** support for it.

## Why multilateral beats bilateral

**[model]** In a simple two-party reputation mechanism, a merchant pays an agent a wage premium and threatens to fire him permanently if caught cheating — but the threat's force is capped by that one relationship's remaining value, and short-relationship agency (the norm in long-distance medieval trade) makes the threat weak. The coalition's multilateral punishment — cheat one member, lose access to all of them — makes the threat "robust with respect to the length of [the agent's] association with a specific merchant," sustaining honesty even in short-duration relationships a bilateral mechanism could not discipline. Coalition boundaries are endogenous to the information network: a non-member merchant would have to pay a coalition-member agent *more* than a coalition merchant would (the member agent retains the option of returning to coalition employment even after cheating an outsider) — so coalitions stay closed as a direct consequence of the reputation mechanism's own logic, not by rule or charter.

## The evidence, concretely

**[empirical]** A 1055 letter reports an agent's Jerusalem embezzlement reaching Maghribi traders "as far away as Sicily," who "canceled their agency relations with him" — network-wide, not bilateral, response. A ca. 1050 letter about a dispute with an agent states "You know that he is our representative [so the conflict] bothers us all" — direct textual evidence that third parties, not just the wronged principal, had a stake in an individual agency dispute. A 1059 Sicily letter records buyers paying an agreed higher price for illegally-early-season flax "solely out of fear of losing their reputations."

## A network-density condition, stated but not formalised

**[wiki synthesis]** The mechanism implies a testable structural precondition Greif does not himself quantify: multilateral reputation requires an information-transmission network connecting potential employers to an agent's history, so its reach — and hence the maximum sustainable coalition size — is bounded by that network's density. No source in this wiki states or tests a minimum-connectivity threshold for when multilateral (rather than bilateral) reputation becomes viable.
