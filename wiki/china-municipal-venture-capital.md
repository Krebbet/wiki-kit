# China's Cities as Municipal Venture Capitalists

Southwood & Rivaton (Works in Progress, 2026) argue that China's growth owes less to Beijing picking winners than to thousands of city and provincial governments that behave like risk-loving venture-capital funds: cadres are promoted on local growth, localities keep a large share of the revenue they raise (fiscal retention, land-lease income, SOE and equity stakes), so they take equity positions, zone and subsidise land, and build industrial clusters around anchor firms, while Beijing supplies cheap domestic capital, prunes overinvestment and polices local protectionism. They claim the model is not uniquely Chinese: 1950s-60s American "growth machine" cities had the same incentive structure. The piece is an interpretive essay that stitches together cited third-party figures and anecdotes; it contains no original data analysis, and its causal claims are tier (b).

**Evidence tier note.** (b) **Argumentative essay** in a popular magazine (not peer-reviewed). Its facts are second-hand, hyperlinked to news, government, IMF/World Bank/FRED and a handful of academic papers; none were checked here (the essay was read, not its citations). The data it leans on, per claim: the IMF's ~85% subnational share of public expenditure; one promotion study (growth 3.3 pp above a predecessor raises promotion odds 40-50%, 2000s); a provincial marginal-retention series (about 75% post-1980, near 90% by 1990); a Shaoxing 2008 scorecard (54 of 100 points on economic development); city equity cases (Hefei/BOE, NIO, CXMT; Wuhu/Chery; Ningde/CATL); IMF subsidy estimate (~2% of GDP vs ~0.3% US); Brandt et al. on entrants driving ~three quarters of 1998-2007 manufacturing productivity growth; capital-efficiency ratio and auto-capacity utilisation figures. The inference from these to "cities act as venture-capital funds" is the authors' framing. There is no portfolio-level return data: success cases are named, failures (apart from overcapacity) are not enumerated.

## Claims

- **[model]** Subnational governments, not Beijing, are the locus of industrial strategy. Support: the IMF's ~85% subnational expenditure share (a measured fact the essay cites) and the origin stories of Huawei, BYD, DJI, Geely, Vivo as locally nurtured private firms. The inference that Beijing played "no role" is asserted, not tested; the essay concedes central subsidy and tax support for strategic sectors (batteries, EVs).
- **[model]** The mechanism is an incentive structure: a cadre scorecard weighted to local economic performance, age-ceiling promotion windows, high marginal fiscal retention and land-lease revenue, which makes mayors behave "like company directors" who earn equity returns and tax base from clusters. The components are the same ones [[regionally-decentralized-authoritarianism]] treats as a tournament; the essay adds the fiscal-retention and land-capture layer and the venture-capital metaphor. The promotion-growth correlation it cites is **[empirical]** third-party work, though the essay does not report the design, and the same literature disputes how much promotion actually tracks growth.
- **[empirical]** (second-hand, anecdotal) Hefei's reported public investment of roughly $25bn over the decade to 2023 returned earnings of nearly $9bn; NIO (24% stake) returned an IRR above 30%; Wuhu holds 21% of Chery. These are cherry-picked winners drawn from company filings and press; no denominator of failed bets, no risk-adjustment, "reported earnings" not realised returns. Not evidence of a good portfolio.
- **[model]** Clustering is the main payoff: land-owning governments capture spillovers (land values, taxes), so they supply public goods and cheap zoned land that benefit all firms in the cluster (Kunshan, Shaodong lighters, Danyang lenses). Presented as the single most important explanation of China's city success; no test against alternatives (cheap labour, FDI, export access).
- **[model]** The system is fuelled and policed centrally: financial repression (capped deposit rates, capital controls) channels household savings to cheap corporate credit; Beijing then prunes overcapacity (steel, coal, property) and polices local market fragmentation. The forced-transfer reading of repression and the corporate interest-burden contrast (under 1% of turnover vs 5.3% US) are measured figures from official statistics; the causal link to the municipal tournament is asserted.
- **[empirical]** (second-hand) Costs it concedes: growth misreporting (young mayors reported growth 3.4 pp higher than just-too-old mayors with no corroboration in night lights, electricity, entry or productivity); overcapacity (autos ~50% utilised); a US-to-China capital-efficiency gap of nearly 2x; corruption, argued to be mostly deal-greasing rather than extractive, which is an assertion.
- **[model]** The model is general: postwar US "growth machine" cities (87% of local taxes from property; ~70% of revenue raised locally) behaved the same way; the West's decline reflects fragmented governance (101 municipalities around the Bay Area), capped property-tax revenue and lost local incentive. Conclusion: restore incentives, not centralised planning.
- **[wiki synthesis]** The thesis is a competitor to the top-down reading in [[developmental-state-and-embedded-autonomy]] (Wade's centralised pilot agency, [[koreas-economic-planning-board]]) and an extension of the level-of-analysis point in [[state-capacity-and-level-of-government]]: capacity sits in the sub-national tier, so national-state-capacity indices mis-locate it. It also reframes the SOE question in [[soe-privatization-vs-restructuring-in-china]]: much of the "state" in Chinese enterprise is municipal equity, not central ownership, and the Party-control channel in [[party-organisation-control-of-chinese-soes]] operates inside this local-return calculus.

## Steelman and limits

**Steelman.** The account is mechanism-first and fits what the other China pages describe separately: promotion tournaments ([[regionally-decentralized-authoritarianism]]), local control of land, and equity stakes that give officials residual claims, which [[property-rights-theory-of-the-firm]] says should sharpen incentives. It correctly identifies a measurable asymmetry (cash subsidies ~2% of GDP, local share of spending) and it names Beijing's disciplining role rather than claiming pure decentralisation. The US-1950s comparison is a useful discipline: it asks which incentive variables differ, not which culture.

**Limits.**
- **Survivorship and denominator.** Named cases are winners. The same incentive structure also produced the overcapacity, local-government financing-vehicle debt and fabricated GDP the essay lists; the net return across all municipal bets is unmeasured. "Risk-loving" is a description of the incentive, not a demonstrated portfolio property; cadres face heavy downside from failure but also from non-participation in the tournament, which is closer to herding than to venture-style diversification.
- **Metaphor vs mechanism.** Venture capital has fund-level return discipline and limited-partner exit; a mayor has a 3-5 year rotation (see [[chinese-cadre-rotation-and-the-iron-triangle]]), so returns accrue to successors and the tournament rewards visible activity ([[multitask-incentive-theory]]). The essay's own fudged-GDP finding is the multitask problem.
- **Confounds.** Land-lease revenue and bank credit may drive investment more than cadre incentives; hard-to-separate from the national savings regime the essay itself describes. Beijing's "agnosticism" about which firm wins is asserted; the Hefei and Shanghai cases include central coordination and approval.
- **Corruption and rent-seeking.** The claim that corruption mostly accelerates projects is asserted without evidence and sits uneasily with [[rent-seeking-and-the-welfare-cost-of-transfers]]; the wiki's pages on the rent-seeking side have not been tested against this claim.
- **Transferability.** The West-should-copy conclusion depends on whether the fiscal and land-ownership preconditions (state control of land supply, no property rights against zoning, party discipline) can be reproduced under rule-bound democratic institutions; the essay does not address [[credible-commitment]]-type constraints on local governments.
- **Source type.** Non-peer-reviewed, advocacy-adjacent magazine essay (a progress-studies publication); authors include a tech founder and book author. Treat as a hypothesis generator, not as evidence.

No open conflict file exists on the municipal-growth theme; the closest, [[constraint-vs-capacity-as-the-investment-mechanism]], concerns constraint on the ruler versus state capacity as the growth mechanism and this essay sits on the capacity side, without data adding to either position.
