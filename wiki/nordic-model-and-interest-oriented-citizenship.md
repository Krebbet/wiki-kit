# Nordic Model and Interest-Oriented Citizenship

Kettunen argues the Nordic governing model's distinctiveness is not "strong state vs. markets" or "cooperation vs. conflict" but a specific historical construct: **"interest-oriented social citizenship"** — social citizenship built around recognised, organised, *conflicting* interests (chiefly labour vs. capital) rather than around individual rights. He explicitly rejects both Esping-Andersen's "decommodification" thesis (regulation as removal from market) and Trägårdh's "statist individualism" (a strong state directly liberating individuals, bypassing civil society): Nordic labour-market regulation, in his reading, makes labour a more *complete* commodity by removing coercive constraints on how it is sold, strengthening workers' bargaining position as market actors rather than exempting them from the market.

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** throughout — the author states this directly: "This article is not an analysis of new empirical findings, but a historical reinterpretation of facts mostly well-known." Read as one contribution to a live, disputed scholarly framing, not settled description — it argues directly against two other influential, likely better-known frameworks (Esping-Andersen, Trägårdh).

## Symmetry as constructed, not natural

**[model]** The mechanism: workers and employers are institutionally constructed as formally parity-based "parties" to collective bargaining — explicitly *not* claimed to reflect underlying material equality (Kettunen cites Offe & Wiesenthal's "two logics of collective action" against that reading) — and this formal parity becomes the basis for extending democratisation by widening the range of issues subject to collective bargaining. Three combined principles structure Nordic industrial relations: parity-based collective agreements, employer "direction rights" paired with employee participation, and joint acceptance of production rationalisation.

## An alternative axis to rights-based citizenship

**[wiki synthesis]** This is a genuine candidate for testing whether the wiki's implicit accountability vocabulary is Anglosphere-specific. Where Anglo-American and EU frameworks typically centre individual *rights* against the state, Kettunen's Nordic case centres *interests* mediated through collective, voluntary organisations, with the state facilitating and enforcing bargaining outcomes rather than granting rights directly. If a wiki accountability axis assumes individual-rights-based citizenship as the mechanism connecting state to society, this source describes a functioning alternative — collective/associational interest-representation as the primary channel, with individual rights layered on later (partly via EU accession).

## Even within "corporatism," the mechanisms differ

**[model]** Kettunen distinguishes the Nordic union-embedded shop-steward bargaining model explicitly from the German *Betriebsräte* (works-council) model — a caution that a single coarse "corporatism" axis would conflate at least two structurally different co-determination mechanisms within continental/Nordic Europe alone, before any Anglo-American comparison is attempted.

## Historicity as the paper's real subject

**[model]** Post-1980s, Kettunen argues the symmetry/virtuous-circle model eroded as transnational capital gained a Hirschman-style "exit" option that weakened national "voice" channels, producing **institutional conversion** (Thelen's term): the same welfare/industrial-relations institutions persist structurally while being repurposed to serve "competition state" functions — a shift, in Kettunen's framing, from *compromise* (mutual recognition of particular, non-universal interests) to *consensus* (a predefined common interest, EU-style "social partner" language). **[wiki synthesis]** "The Nordic model" is itself shown to be a discursively and competitively produced label — a caution against treating any of this wiki's "the X model" comparative-governance shorthand (Nordic, German, Singaporean) as a neutral pre-theoretical descriptor rather than itself a contested political and rhetorical artefact.
