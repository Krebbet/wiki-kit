# Selectorate Theory and the Winning Coalition

Bueno de Mesquita, Morrow, Siverson & Smith formalise "who a leader must satisfy to survive" into two countable institutional quantities and a ratio: the **selectorate (S)** — everyone with an institutional say in choosing the leader — and the **winning coalition (W)** — the minimal subset of S whose sustained support keeps the leader in office. The **W/S ratio** drives the whole model: as W grows relative to a fixed resource pool, private goods per coalition member get diluted, making public-goods provision the comparatively cheaper way to hold a coalition together — leaders in large-W systems (paradigmatically, democracies) are pushed toward broad public-goods provision; leaders in small-W systems (autocracies, especially ones with large S — "universal suffrage" with rigged elections) are pushed toward concentrated private-goods patronage.

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** — this captured source is the formal-theory journal article; its empirical testing (Polity-based W/S proxies against ~30 outcome variables) is reported only by citation to a companion book manuscript not held by this wiki, so treat the specific coefficients as **[empirical, second-hand]** and the mechanism itself as **[model]**.

## The loyalty norm: why small-W systems are stable, not just exploitative

**[model]** A coalition member's loyalty to the incumbent is driven by the risk of exclusion from a *challenger's* future coalition. Because a challenger's true affinities are unknown until he takes power, a defector's probability of being retained is exactly **W/S** — the smaller this ratio, the larger the risk of exclusion, and the stronger the incentive to stay loyal even when the incumbent could offer more. This lets a small-W leader spend *less* to retain support than the coalition's nominal bargaining power would suggest, and explains persistence directly: **"the average tenure in office of all autocrats is about twice that of all democrats."**

## Leader-optimal institutions: large S, small W

**[model]** Leaders prefer the largest possible selectorate paired with the smallest possible winning coalition — in the authors' own words, **"autocratic regimes with universal suffrage (implying rigged elections)"** — because a large S maximises the exclusion risk (and hence loyalty) facing any given coalition member, while a small W minimises how many people must actually be paid off. Those inside S but outside W, and everyone outside S entirely, always prefer the opposite (larger W, smaller S) — i.e., they favour democratisation. Coalition members' own preference over W is non-monotonic: small increases from a very small W hurt existing members (dilution dominates), but past a turning point further increases help them (their own bargaining leverage over the leader strengthens) — offered as an explanation for a "king vs. court" wedge as monarchies expand.

## A candidate general-purpose accountability ratio — and its limits

**[wiki synthesis]** W/S is, in principle, the cleanest single-number candidate this wiki holds for "how broad-based versus narrow-and-capturable is this institution's accountability structure" — generalisable beyond nation-states to a corporation's board (minimal voting bloc needed to unseat a CEO, over total voting shares), a party (minimal winning faction over total convention delegates), or a bureaucracy (minimal coalition within an appointing/oversight body). Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D129**, with the scope limit stated plainly: **the source itself only demonstrates regime-level operationalisation, via Polity proxies (REGTYPE, XRCOMP, XROPEN, PARCOMP for W; LEGSELEC for S) — not a validated method for counting W and S directly in a board, a bureaucracy, or a party.** Extending the ratio to non-state institutions is this wiki's own inference, untested by any source held here, and the model's own simplifying assumptions (all selectorate members interchangeable, a clean binary in/out of W, a stable identifiable minimal coalition) are stronger than what most real institutions would satisfy on inspection.

## Extensions asserted, not derived, in this source

**[model]** Small-W leaders are described as "reverse Robin Hoods" — maximising tax revenue to redistribute to a privileged coalition, since the surplus available lets them compensate the coalition for the tax cost. Corruption in small-W systems is linked to three distinct motives: complacency (eliminating it is itself a public good nobody in the coalition benefits from), deliberate use as a cheap reward mechanism, and outright kleptocracy. These are stated, not tested, in this article.
