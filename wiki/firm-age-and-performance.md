# Firm Age and Performance

Coad, Holm, Krafft & Quatraro (2018) is the editorial introduction to a *Journal of Evolutionary Economics* special issue on firm age and performance — not a standalone empirical study, but a synthesis of six companion empirical papers plus a literature review, and the wiki's best available overview of how the age-mortality literature has moved since Hannan & Freeman. Its central methodological claim complicates any simple age-decline story: age effects are typically **non-linear and front-loaded**, concentrated in a firm's first five to seven years, rather than a smooth monotonic function running the length of an organisation's life.

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** for the editorial's own framing arguments; the substantive findings it reports are **[empirical]** at one remove — six separate studies it summarises, not data the authors of this piece collected themselves. Table 1 of the source is the single best pointer if any of the six companion papers need separate ingestion later.

## Age causes performance, not the reverse — and why that isn't the same as "no selection effect"

**[model]** The editorial takes a firm position on a causality question that a lazier framing conflates: **"The causal arrow runs from age to performance... because the only interpretation of a correlation between age and performance is that age causes performance."** It explicitly rejects treating survivorship/selection bias as *reverse* causality (contra Loderer et al. 2017) — performance affects whether a firm *survives to be observed* at a given age, it does not affect the firm's age itself. **[wiki synthesis]** This is a genuinely useful distinction for the wiki to carry: a correlation between age and performance in a balanced panel of known survivors is not automatically confounded by selection in the way a correlation between, say, incentive intensity and effort might be — but it does mean age-performance studies restricted to survivors systematically omit the population (the two-thirds of a cohort that dies within five years, per the finding below) where the age effect is strongest.

## The headline mortality statistic, and where growth concentrates

**[empirical]**, reporting Anyadike-Danes & Hart (2017) on ~239,000 UK firms born in 1998, tracked 15 years: **"Two thirds of firms die within the first five years after birth, and although survival chances improve after age 5, only 10 percent of the cohort survive to age 15."** Among survivors, growth is likewise front-loaded — "average growth paths of surviving firms are pretty much flat" past the first five years — leading the editorial to prefer **"neither-up-nor-out dynamics"** over the conventional "up-or-out" framing (Haltiwanger et al. 2013): most surviving firms neither grow fast nor exit, they persist near-flat.

**[wiki synthesis]** Read against [[liability-of-newness-empirical-hazard-evidence]]'s 40-month hazard window (0.031→0.015), this UK cohort statistic is directionally consistent — steep early mortality, declining thereafter — and extends the horizon out to 15 years, where the age effect appears to have largely resolved rather than continuing to decline indefinitely.

## Two named liabilities, not one continuous decline

**[model]**, drawing on the ecology tradition (Hannan & Freeman) as elaborated in the issue's companion review (Coad 2017): **liability of newness** (early-life fragility from lack of resources/legitimacy) is distinct from **liability of obsolescence** (late-life fragility from failing to track a changing market or demand environment). The summarised Pellegrino (2017) paper on Spanish innovation barriers ties young-firm disadvantage specifically to financial-resource access and legitimacy, and older firms instead to obsolescence — two different mechanisms operating at opposite ends of the age range, not one monotonic curve.

## A caution about naive age-decline regressions

**[model]**, reporting Cucculelli (2017): the *raw* correlation between firm age and product innovation is negative, but turns **positive** once CEO tenure and time-since-last-innovation are controlled — meaning an uncontrolled age-innovation regression can manufacture an apparent age-decline effect that is really an omitted-variable artefact of CEO inertia. **[wiki synthesis]** This is a direct methodological warning against citing any bare age-performance correlation on this wiki without checking what the source controlled for — exactly the kind of caution [[measurement-validity-framework]] exists to enforce.

Also reported: Grazzi & Moschella (2017) on Italian exporters find *young* exporting firms are **more** resilient to exchange-rate shocks than established ones, and show stronger age-selection effects on growth — a case where the naive "young = fragile" reading is directly contradicted within the same special issue.

## Policy conclusion

**[model]** "One-size-fits-all policies supporting firms' performances are not likely to be effective. Industrial policies should be designed by considering firms' and product life courses, sectors of activity, ownership structure, and type of performance indicator that is targeted." — the editorial's own stated implication, offered as a position, not as a source-backed finding.
