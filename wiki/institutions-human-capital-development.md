# Institutions, Human Capital and Development

Acemoglu, Gallego & Robinson supply this wiki's fullest independent rebuttal of [[schooling-and-institutional-quality]]'s claim, and it is two rebuttals in one: a **fully independent re-identification** using different instruments and a different institutions measure, and a **direct reversal of the historical premise** Glaeser et al.'s mechanism narrative depends on. Once both human capital and institutions are properly instrumented, human capital's coefficient on log GDP per capita collapses from ~25–35% (naive OLS) to as low as 0.069–0.094 — statistically indistinguishable from zero and consistent with microeconomic Mincerian wage returns (6–10%) — while institutions' coefficient stays large and precise (1.32–1.46) across every robustness check.

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]** — cross-country (62 former colonies) and cross-regional (670 sub-national regions, 48 former colonies) 2SLS/LIML regressions, with new instruments purpose-built to avoid the identification objections raised against the classic AJR settler-mortality design.

## An independent instrument set, not a re-run of Glaeser et al.'s own

**[empirical]** For institutions: log settler mortality (capped at 250/1000) and log 1500 population density — AJR's classic instruments, in their corrected form. For human capital, genuinely new instruments: **protestant missionary presence per capita in the 1920s** and **primary school enrolment in 1900** — validated by a falsification test showing missionary presence is uncorrelated with *prior* (1870) enrolment but strongly correlated with *later* (1940) enrolment and 2005 schooling. **[wiki synthesis]** This directly answers item 3 and 4 of [[schooling-and-institutional-quality]]'s own "what would strengthen or kill it" list (sub-national variation; a natural-experiment-style schooling instrument not chosen by the government whose institutions are the outcome) — not with a replication of Table 12, but with a parallel, independently-designed test of the identical causal question.

## The identification critique of Glaeser et al.'s own instrument

**[model]** The paper directly disputes the French-legal-origin instrument [[schooling-and-institutional-quality]]'s Table 11 horse race depends on: "it is not clear why French legal origin should be an attractive instrument for human capital or the type of institutions that AJR focused on," and — citing Acemoglu & Johnson (2005) — French legal origin, conditional on settler mortality and population density, has no explanatory power for expropriation risk or executive constraints, but does affect *contracting* institutions (court efficiency) — a different construct entirely from the property-rights/expropriation-risk institutions the debate is actually about. This is a construct-validity objection to the instrument, not merely a re-run with different data.

## The historical premise, reversed

**[empirical]** Glaeser et al.'s mechanism narrative assumes European settlers "brought" human capital to the colonies that became inclusive institutions. This paper's own literacy data show the opposite: **Spanish conquistadors were more literate than British North American settlers.** Lockhart (1972): 76.6% of Pizarro's conquistadors could sign their name, 51% could read and write — a selected urban, noble-second-son elite. Galenson (1981): only 41.2% of a sample of indentured laborers who settled British North America could sign their contracts (1683–84); Grubb (1990) reports ~54–60% literacy in 1600s Virginia. **"The historical evidence suggests that the exact opposite of this claim seems to be true: the conquistadors who colonized South America were more educated than the British and other Europeans who colonized North America."** The paper's own summary of Glaeser et al.'s premise, immediately followed by its rebuttal: *"It is plausible, but it turns out not to be correct."*

## No causal effect the other way, either

**[empirical]** Testing whether human capital causes institutions (the reverse of AJR's usual direction): once settler mortality and population density are controlled, instrumented schooling's coefficient on institutions becomes insignificant and "half the time even has the wrong sign," while settler mortality itself remains negative and significant. **"There is no causal impact of human capital on institutions"** — this paper's own stated conclusion, from the same design used to find institutions robust in the forward direction.
