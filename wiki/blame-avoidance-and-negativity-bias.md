# Blame Avoidance and Negativity Bias

Hood's argument is that officeholders face a personal risk — **blame risk** — that is distinct from the institution's or society's risk, and that they act on it because blame is weighted far more heavily than credit. Two things produce that asymmetry: negativity bias in the audience (losses weighted roughly 2–4× equivalent gains, borrowed wholesale from Kahneman & Tversky), and a monitoring design that only activates on failure — McCubbins & Schwartz's "fire alarms" rather than "police patrols", so success goes unnoticed while failure is punished. The result Hood names is a **mediocrity bias**: "it seems to pay better to be average than to be excellent." Officeholders then invest in blame avoidance, and the organisational patterns that follow — fragmented delivery, protocolised judgement, opaque accountability trails, service avoidance — are the *aggregate, unintended byproduct* of individually rational career protection, not a design anyone chose. Crucially for this wiki's framing question: the mechanism contains no size variable at all. On its own terms it predicts risk aversion rising with proximity to an accountability event, with weakness of tenure protection, and with the density of the external scrutiny ecology — not with headcount, budget, or hierarchy depth.

**Evidence tier note.** This is an **argumentative and typological opening chapter, not a study**. Its own contribution is a synthesis and a three-way typology of blame-avoidance strategies (presentational / agency / policy, Table 1.1); it reports no new data. The negativity-bias premise is (a) empirical but cited second-hand from psychology. The core causal step — blame avoidance → organisational pathology — is **asserted, not evidenced, in this chapter**. Strongest evidence cited: James & John (2007) on English local-government election results against Audit Commission performance ratings, finding voters punish exceptionally poor performance but do not reward exceptionally good performance; and Borraz (2007) on French sewage-sludge policy, same asymmetry. Weakest, and the chapter's rhetorical centrepiece: a single interview quote from an anonymous German federal bureaucrat. One cited dispute is left live rather than resolved — Kernell (1977) on dissatisfied voters turning out more, contested by Radcliff (1994).

## The mechanism, stated precisely

**[model / assertion]** The chain has two inputs and one output.

1. **Individual payoff asymmetry.** Officeholders care about blame because it bears on re-election, promotion, bonuses, job retention and reputation — "Managers will care about blame if they think it will reduce their prospects of promotion, bonuses, staying in their current jobs." This is a career-payoff claim about a person, not a structural claim about an organisation. It is a special case of the residual-claimancy asymmetry the register carries as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D8: the officeholder bears the downside of a decision and captures none of the upside.
2. **Monitoring design.** Legislative oversight operates by fire alarm — reactive, triggered only by failure — rather than by police patrol, continuous and proactive. Success is therefore invisible to the accountability system and failure is not. Hood treats this as a *choice about how a legislature allocates its own monitoring effort*, not an inherent property of bureaucracy.
3. **Output: blame-avoidance investment**, in three families. *Presentational* (framing, timing, excuse-making). *Agency* (delegating blameworthy tasks to lightning rods, building complexity and partnership arrangements that diffuse responsibility, rotating officeholders so accountability lands on successors). *Policy* (protocolisation and automaticity replacing professional discretion; service avoidance — the doctor who declines the high-risk patient).

**[wiki synthesis]** The agency family is worth separating from the others because it inverts a standard assumption. Ordinary principal-agent analysis treats the weakening of the accountability chain as *drift* — something that happens to a principal. Here the officeholder deliberately engineers the distance. That is the opposite direction of causation from agent drift, and it means an observed opaque accountability trail is evidence of design, not neglect.

## What moderates blame sensitivity

**[model — Hood's own named moderators]** None of these is size.

- **Tenure security and selection mechanism.** "A judge or bureaucrat (or professor) whose continuing tenure does not depend on reelection or reappointment can normally afford to be more relaxed about blame than one in different circumstances." This is a direct source-stated causal link from accountability structure to risk-aversion intensity, explicitly independent of size.
- **Time proximity to the next accountability event.** "A politician who has just won a landslide victory or is about to retire from office is likely to be less concerned with blame than one on the eve of an election in a close race." Blame aversion is a state, not a trait of the office.
- **Density of the external scrutiny ecology.** The only scale-flavoured claim in the chapter, and it is about the *environment*, not the institution: "the more journalists, lobbyists, scientific experts, compensation lawyers, elected politicians, and their sidekicks there are... the greater will be the demand to discover and act upon such instances." The prediction is that blame-avoidance investment grows *over historical time* as the risk industry grows — a modernity claim, not a size claim.
- **Blame culture vs. tolerance culture.** Named as an organisational-climate axis, with the high-reliability-organisation literature (Sagan 1993) cited as evidence that blame concentration varies systematically by organisation type.

## Failure modes named

**[assertion, in the source's own terms]** Mediocrity bias (fire-alarm oversight rewards average over excellent). Defensive fragmentation and non-integration between organisations. Rigid, "inflexible and unintelligent" risk regulation that can raise net risk while lowering blame exposure — Hood's rural-railway example, where safety-driven closures push travellers onto more dangerous roads. "Crooked and ambiguous accountability trails" that defeat post-failure attribution. Protocolisation and automaticity substituting checklists and formulae for judgement. Service avoidance.

Note the direction of the regulation example: blame avoidance produces *more* rule-making and *more* apparent caution while making the underlying outcome worse. Caution and safety come apart.

## What this chapter is not evidence for

**[wiki synthesis — gaps]**

- **It is not evidence about institutional size.** Hood nowhere argues that larger institutions are more risk averse. No headcount, budget, or hierarchy-depth variable appears. A claim that "large institutions are risk averse because they are large" cannot cite this chapter; at most it can cite the scrutiny-density argument, which is about how many watchers exist in the society, not how big the watched body is. See [[risk-aversion-in-large-institutions]] and [[open-questions]] Q6, Q30.
- **The private-sector extension is asserted, not shown.** The chapter states the logic "often extends to private or independent sector providers of public services too" and then supplies no private-sector evidence at all. Every case is public: French sewage sludge, English local government, US Congress, a German federal bureaucrat, UK and US party politics, EU institutional design. The mechanism as specified is itself public-sector-shaped — electoral accountability, legislative fire alarms, media scrutiny of officeholders — and the chapter does not attempt the mapping onto a board and shareholders. See [[open-questions]] Q3.
- **The historical-increase claim is speculative and Hood says so.** Weaver's claim that political negativity bias rose in the US and other developed democracies in the 1970s–80s is reported and then immediately qualified: "We have little direct survey evidence for changing negativity bias."
- **The reform lever is undetermined by the author's own statement.** On red-tape-busting and burden-versus-risk weighing, Hood writes that "it is at least an open question as to whether such mechanisms in practice counter or augment blame-avoidance imperatives in government." Record as a stated limitation, not a validated lever.
- **Scope.** Mature Western democracies, late 20th and early 21st century: UK, US, France, Germany, EU. No developing-world, authoritarian, or pre-20th-century cases.

**Balance note.** The chapter is unusually even-handed for its topic — it states upfront that blame avoidance "can have positive as well as negative effects sometimes", that negativity bias "does not always seem to sweep all before it", and that countervailing trust-preserving processes must exist. It is not a polemic against bureaucracy, and should not be cited as one.
