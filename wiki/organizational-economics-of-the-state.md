# Organizational Economics of the State

Besley, Burgess, Khan & Xu's review argues that the economics of bureaucracy has built rigorous evidence at one level — the individual bureaucrat and the principal-agent dyad, via field experiments — and almost none at the level that determines whether a state actually works: **the organisation as a system of many agents, departments, task assignments and complementarities**. Their own words: "focusing on solving specific principal-agent problems… may give an incomplete picture of what it might take to create an effective state." This page carries that systems-level material — multiple principals, departmental architecture, independent agencies, task interdependence, and the boundary between the state and the firms and NGOs it deals with — plus the authors' own cross-country "Weberian Facts". **The systems-level section is explicitly agenda-setting, not evidence.** The authors say credible evidence there is "relatively scarce", because large-scale randomisation across arms or layers of a bureaucracy is impractical and because organisation-level studies are, by definition, limited in the number of units available for inference. Read it as a map of what is not known, written by people who say so.

**Evidence tier note — this is a review paper and the tiers differ sharply by section.**
- **[empirical, the authors' own — and the only original empirical content in the paper]** Section 2.2's "Weberian Facts": V-Dem cross-country panel regressions, 5-year periods, country and year fixed effects, lagged dependent variable, N ≈ 1,600–1,634, plus cross-sectional plots. The authors state flatly that these are "only conditional correlations… regardless of the direction of causality."
- **[empirical, reviewed — others' work]** The bulk of Section 3: dozens of RCTs and quasi-experiments on personnel. That material is carried on [[personnel-economics-of-the-state]], not here.
- **[model]** The equation-1-to-5 principal-agent formalism is the authors' organising lens for classifying the literature, **not tested in this paper**. Aghion & Tirole's real/formal authority and Holmstrom & Milgrom's multitasking are cited, not re-derived.
- **[institutional history, secondary]** Japan's MITI, Korea's EPB, Northcote-Trevelyan (1854), the Pendleton Act (1883) — narrative cited from others, and explicitly flagged by the authors as "qualitative and thus serves mainly to identify areas of work", not as mechanism.
- **[wiki synthesis]** Section 4, the organisational-economics agenda, contains **no new evidence at all**. Everything in the "systems level" section below is a design question with a citation attached, not a finding.

## Four levels, and the seam the paper is about

**[model]** The paper's formalism is a ladder, and its argument is that the literature has climbed only the bottom two rungs:

| Level | Object | Formal statement | Evidence status per the authors |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Individual bureaucrat | y_it = g(θ_i, e_it, ν_J(i,t)) — type, effort, task features | Strong: RCTs on measurable frontline roles |
| 2 | Principal-agent dyad in a department | y_it = g(θ_i, e_it, **E_it**, ν_J(i,t)) — once the *principal's* own effort matters: double moral hazard, real vs. formal authority | Strong to moderate |
| 3 | **The organisation as a system** | Y_t = F(y_1t, …, y_Nt) — task assignment, coordination, complementarities across agents | **"Relatively scarce", "less travelled"** |
| 4 | National bureaucratic quality vs. GDP | The Weberian Facts | Descriptive, non-causal by the authors' statement |

**Do not treat findings from one level as applying to another.** The authors do not, and the seam between levels 2 and 3 is the paper's thesis.

## The Weberian Facts

**[empirical — the authors' own, and correlational by their own statement]** Three facts from V-Dem's de facto measures of meritocratic recruitment and of rigorous, impartial administration:

- **Fact 1 — persistence.** Country fixed effects alone explain **73% of the overall variation** in bureaucratic quality across the panel; most variation is cross-sectional, between countries, not longitudinal within one. The two sub-indices correlate at **r = 0.54** within country.
- **Fact 2 — levels.** Bureaucratic quality is positively correlated with GDP per capita; **no high-income country in the sample scores below the 42nd percentile** on the bureaucracy index.
- **Fact 3 — changes.** *Improvements* in bureaucratic quality correlate with subsequent GDP growth, in a panel with country and year fixed effects and a lagged dependent variable. **Strongest for meritocratic recruitment (coefficient 0.023–0.025, significant); weaker and insignificant for rigorous administration (0.004–0.013, ns).** The aggregate result is robust to country-specific linear trends. OPEC and oil-rich countries are dropped from the GDP exercise.

**[wiki synthesis — the level-of-analysis caveat, which is this wiki's standing one]** Fact 1's persistence is **national and regime-level, not institution-level**. The duration variable is calendar time at the level of a country, not an institution's own founding-to-now age. That is the same substitution recorded against Heckelman on [[institutional-sclerosis]] and against Fukuyama on [[political-decay-and-vetocracy]], and it is the subject of [[open-questions]] Q61 and Q90. **This source supplies no organisational-age evidence.** It does assert, without measuring, that "once selected, bureaucrats tend to remain within an organization" — a low-exit-rate claim used to motivate why skill-mismatch matters, not a finding.

**The same proxy problem recurs.** "Weberian" here means a V-Dem expert-coded index, as it means an expert survey scale in Evans & Rauch. **This wiki holds no text by Weber** — the only open copy is a scanned image with no text layer — so no source in this batch can be cited for what Weber argued. See [[weberian-structure-and-growth]].

## The systems level: what the paper proposes and does not test

**[model / wiki synthesis — every item below is a design question with citations attached, and none is a result]**

**Multiple principals.** Overlapping principals "pull agents in different directions in ways that are counterproductive" (Dixit 1997; Gailmard & Patty 2012). The paper adds concrete public instances — overlapping departmental responsibility, and the layering of politician over senior bureaucrat over frontline staff — to the mechanism already on [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]]. It is also explicit about the chain: "politicians are principals when it comes to bureaucrats, they are agents themselves when it comes to citizens." In non-democracies accountability is "even harder to discern"; the substitute mechanism cited is China's provincial-governor promotion tournaments tied to growth performance.

**Departmental architecture: M-form vs. task interdependence.** Multidivisional structure — some divisions functional, some geographic — is presented as a coordination response "as the scale of enterprise increases" (Chandler 1977; Qian, Roland & Xu 2006). **Asserted, not tested here.** Against it sits **O-ring interdependence** (Kremer 1993): where a delivery chain has complementary components, one weak link fails the whole system regardless of how good the others are. The paper's illustration is a UK bridge safety-monitoring system disabled because the local council paying its electricity bill stopped paying. This is the sharpest systems-level failure mode in the source and it has no evidence attached.

**Centralisation vs. decentralisation.** The named tradeoff is adaptation against coordination, with **fragmentation and inability to coordinate** as the cost of decentralising. Unresolved.

**Independent regulatory agencies.** Justified by **two distinct failure modes** — short-termism, and time-inconsistency — rather than by a general preference for insulation. The authors then undercut the design: whether the political process can credibly commit to leaving an agency independent is "moot", and doing so is "easiest when there is respect for the rule of law." That is the same commitment problem the agency is meant to solve, relocated one level up. Judicial selection and central bank independence are given as cases where the line between political office and bureaucracy is a **deliberate design choice, not a natural category**.

**The state/firm/NGO boundary.** Besley & Ghatak (2001) and Hart, Shleifer & Vishny (1997) apply the Grossman–Hart–Moore residual-control-rights model to the choice between government and NGO or private provision of public services — the same apparatus [[property-rights-theory-of-the-firm]] carries, pointed at a public-provision question.

## Capture as an information channel, in both directions

**[model / reviewed evidence — the most usable idea in the systems section]** The paper treats the firm-to-bureaucrat information flow as **one channel with two possible outcomes, not two phenomena**. Firms can supply regulators with genuinely needed private information — cost structure, pollution, market conditions — that no regulator could otherwise obtain, **or** bribe them to misreport it for favourable treatment. Which outcome you get depends on incentives, not on whether the channel exists.

The paper reports both readings of that channel without adjudicating: Stigler (1971) and Peltzman (1976) for the capture view, Leff (1964) and Bardhan (2017) for the "efficiency-enhancing under bad policy" view. Frye & Shleifer's (1997) invisible-hand / helping-hand / **grabbing-hand** typology is imported as the applied case, with post-Soviet Russia coded grabbing-hand: rent extraction, dysfunctional legal system, predatory regulation.

**[empirical — one counter-datapoint worth recording, and scoped]** Brown, Earle & Gehlbach (2009) find privatisation in post-communist Russia was **more** effective in regions with **larger** bureaucracies, which they attribute to better institutional support and fewer corruption opportunities. One country, one period, and not a general claim by these authors — but it is a size-positive result, and it cuts against a simple "bigger bureaucracy means more capture" framing. Recorded against [[functional-vs-rent-seeking-growth]] and [[bureaucratic-growth-and-parkinsons-law]] as a datapoint, not as a refutation.

**[wiki synthesis]** This framing is a genuine addition to [[regulatory-capture]], which currently carries capture as something an industry *acquires*. Here the same contact that produces capture is also the only route by which a regulator learns what it needs to regulate — so severing it is not costless, and the design question is about the incentives attached to the channel rather than about its existence.

## Public/private: two precise claims, and where the source is silent

**[model / wiki synthesis]** The paper is unusually careful here, and its claims should not be aggregated into a verdict:

- **A direct non-invariance claim, stated as a methodological caveat.** Applying the AKM wage-decomposition framework — built for competitive labour markets where wages reflect marginal product — to bureaucrats is flagged as theoretically shaky: "wages in the public sector setting typically reflect differences in *positions*, and transfers are not driven by market forces." This is an explicit claim that public compensation is **not market-clearing** in the way the private-sector manager-fixed-effects literature assumes.
- **A direct parallel claim, on hiring discretion.** The negative selection effects of discretionary appointment in the public sector are said to "resonate with the findings in private sector settings, where managers who hire against test recommendations select applicants with lower subsequent retention" (Hoffman, Kahn & Li 2018). The authors draw this parallel themselves: **discretion degrades hire quality when principals can act on private incentives, and that is not public-sector-specific.**
- **An asserted difference in objective function**: "In contrast to private firms where the main goal is profit-maximization, public organizations often pursue a wider range of objectives, involving elements of welfare maximization." Cited to Besley & Ghatak, Le Grand and Tirole; **asserted, not measured**.
- **The load-bearing sentence on personnel, and it is a negative finding attributed to somebody else.** Restating Finan, Olken & Pande: "standard recommendations stressing the importance of incentives and selection are mostly confirmed in public sector settings… there is limited evidence that financial incentives crowd-out or lead to the recruitment of less able workers." That is a *no-crowd-out* result, and it is a citation, not new work by these authors. Bears on [[motivation-crowding]].

**Where the source is silent, and this matters for Q3**: it reports **no** quantified public/private comparison of pay-performance sensitivity, turnover, or selection effects. Every comparative claim above is either a theoretical caveat or a qualitative "resonates with". Do not read a general invariance or non-invariance finding out of it.

## What this source does not establish

**[wiki synthesis]**

- **The systems-level argument is an agenda.** Section 4 contains no test of anything. The authors flag five areas where new work is needed and are candid throughout — "little evidence", "relatively scarce", "less travelled", "moot".
- **No causal claim at country level.** The Weberian Facts are conditional correlations by the authors' own statement, and the direction of causality is unresolved.
- **No organisational age or size regression.** Fact 1 is national persistence; the M-form material is about hierarchy design, not a headcount-to-behaviour relationship.
- **Self-citation dependency, flagged.** At least three of the paper's own cited empirical results (Xu 2018; Xu, Bertrand & Burgess 2020; Xu 2021) are by one of the four co-authors, and Besley & Persson's state-capacity programme supplies the backdrop.
- **Ideological placement, stated because it is unusually explicit.** The authors position themselves **against two named camps**: the public-choice tradition (Tullock, Niskanen, Stigler, Peltzman, Djankov et al.), which they say generated "negative stereotypes" of bureaucrats as "self-interested and venal" and "marginalized" the study of bureaucracy within economics; and the Weberian vision of bureaucracy as mission-driven and transformational, which they treat sympathetically but say lacked microeconomic grounding until the RCT and administrative-data wave. Their own programme is presented as the empirical synthesis. **Read this page against [[rational-actor-accounts-of-bureaucratic-behaviour]] and [[rent-seeking-and-the-welfare-cost-of-transfers]], which are the camp being argued against** — and note that the argument is about method and prior, not about a specific contested finding, because the public-choice sources contain no findings to contest.
- **Scope.** The paper is explicitly confined to "the economics literature", with a pointer to Pepinsky, Pierskalla & Sacks (2017) for the political-science complement. Its RCT evidence is overwhelmingly developing-country field experiments from the 2010s; its historical material is Anglo-American civil-service reform and East Asian developmental-state narrative. The authors date their own evidence base: "Had we written this review twenty years ago, we would have found relatively few studies of bureaucracy and development within economics to draw on."
