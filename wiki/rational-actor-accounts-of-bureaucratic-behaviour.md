# Rational-Actor Accounts of Bureaucratic Behaviour

Two public-choice-adjacent statements of what bureaucrats do and why, paired here because neither stands alone and both are **entirely without empirical content**. Downs (RAND, 1964) supplies the structure: a definition of "bureau" built on four structural criteria rather than on ownership, a five-type typology of officials derived from a single self-interest axiom, and a compendium of roughly twenty asserted hypotheses about hierarchy, information distortion, personnel policy, territorial conflict and bureau life-cycle. Wilson (1967) supplies one sharp idea in a magazine essay: "the bureaucracy problem" is five *incompatible* problems — accountability, equity, efficiency, responsiveness, fiscal integrity — which trade off against one another, and **fiscal integrity wins by default because it is the only one anybody can observe**. The two share a method (argue from assumed motives to organisational consequences) and a limitation. **Downs states outright that his document is "denuded of all explanatory material" and contains no citation to any study, no regression and no case tally; Wilson's piece is a *Public Interest* "Comment" with no data, no analysed case and no citations.** Everything on this page is tier (b). Nothing on it is evidence.

**Evidence tier note — read this before citing anything below.**
- **Downs (1964): [model], pure.** A condensed extract of a 180,000-word book, built from one axiom (officials are utility-maximisers) plus three environmental assumptions (costly information, bounded decision capacity, ineradicable uncertainty). The strongest thing on offer is internal logical consistency. **There is no data anywhere in the document** — the single number in it, an aside that bureaucracy covers "probably over 18 per cent of the U.S. work force", is unsourced. Every hypothesis is stated with identical declarative confidence regardless of whether it follows from the axioms or is simply asserted, and the compendium items C.7–C.11 read as bare assertion with no visible derivation. Downs's own framing — "some of these conclusions will seem obvious… others will be more novel" — does not tell you which.
- **Wilson (1967): [model] at best, and several claims below even that.** An argumentative essay. Its illustrative material is anecdote used rhetorically (McNamara's DoD; the FBI/IRS contrast drawn purely on scandal history — "the FBI is good because it hasn't had any, the Internal Revenue Service is bad because it has"; the cross-ideological Friedman/Tobin endorsement of the negative income tax). Its weakest passages are unsupported historical analogy (Henry II's clerks) and a pseudo-formal claim asserted with no derivation (foreign-policy difficulty rising "with the square of the number of countries, and probably with the cube of the speed of communications"). Named agencies — State, FBI, IRS, DoD — appear for a sentence each and are never analysed.

## Provenance correction: Wilson's 1967 essay contains no agency typology

**[wiki synthesis — a correction to this wiki's own records]** The typology commonly attributed to "Wilson" — production / procedural / craft / coping agencies, generated by crossing action-observability with outcome-observability — **is not in this source and no version of it is**. It comes from Wilson's 1989 book *Bureaucracy*, which this wiki does not hold. This 1967 essay predates it by twenty-two years and is a different and much less formal piece of thinking.

Consequences, recorded so they are not re-derived later:

- [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]] carries the typology as "Wilson's action × outcome observability typology" — correctly, because it is sourced there **through Dixit's citation of Wilson**, not from Wilson. That remains the wiki's only route to it.
- [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D50** has had its provenance note corrected accordingly: the axis stands, its attribution is second-hand via Dixit only, and the primary text this wiki now holds is confirmed *not* to contain it. The row was not deleted.
- **Do not treat this source as corroborating, elaborating or supplying D25/D50.** Wilson's observability claim here is a different one — see below.

## Downs: the structural definition, and why it is not a public-sector theory

**[model]** Downs defines "bureau" by structural criteria, explicitly not by ownership. The criteria our capture records are: it is **large** (defined by a threshold — top-ranking members know less than half of all members personally); its personnel are **hired, promoted and retained on assessed role-performance**, not by ascribed characteristics and not by periodic election by an outside constituency; and its **output is not evaluated by voluntary market transactions**, so it cannot use profitability to judge its own activities. The non-market-output criterion does the most work in the paper.

The generality claim is unusually explicit and unusually sharp:

- Named examples span the Roman Catholic Church, the University of California, the Soviet central planning agency, the US State Department, the New York Port Authority and the Chinese Communist army.
- **General Motors as a whole is *not* a bureau. "The public relations department of Chevrolet" *is***, because its own specific output cannot be market-evaluated even though its inputs can.
- A person can be a "bureaucrat" while working for a non-bureaucratic organisation — Downs's example is Sears, Roebuck — as long as *that individual's* output is not market-evaluable.
- The one named exception proves the accountability criterion: the Catholic Church counts as a bureau "except for the Pope, who is elected."

**[wiki synthesis]** By construction this is a general-organisation theory running on **market-evaluability of output**, not on the public/private line — the same relocation of the axis that Niskanen's footnote 3 and Bozeman's external-control model perform from other directions. It bears directly on [[open-questions]] Q3 and Q25. The important caveat is that the generality is asserted at the *definitional* level only: every worked example in the compendium of hypotheses is government or quasi-government, and Downs supplies **no private-sector-only illustration of any specific hypothesis**.

## Downs: five types of official

**[model]** From Central Hypothesis 1 (officials are utility-maximisers) and Central Hypothesis 2 (what they maximise), five types:

| Type | What is maximised | Relevance elsewhere in this wiki |
|---|---|---|
| **Climbers** | Power, income, prestige — promotion and aggrandisement | The growth mechanism; see [[bureaucratic-growth-and-parkinsons-law]] |
| **Conservers** | Security and convenience; favour the status quo, oppose change and innovation | A named individual-level channel for [[risk-aversion-in-large-institutions]] — theorised, never tested |
| **Zealots** | A narrow policy or programme they are committed to | — |
| **Advocates** | A broader set of functions or their whole organisation | Territorial defence, C.7 |
| **Statesmen** | Society's general welfare | — |

Power is a goal in its own right for climbers, and instrumental for the other three of the latter types. The typology is individual-level; the "laws" below are organisation-level, and **Downs never states the aggregation rule connecting them**. Do not conflate the two.

## Downs: the hypotheses that matter here

**[model — all asserted, some with a stated mechanism and some without]**

- **Cumulative information distortion (C.1–C.2).** In a many-level hierarchy, officials filter information upward and downward to their own benefit, and the distortion **compounds rather than self-corrects**. Redundant channels and hierarchy-bypass devices are described as what bureaus already do about it (C.3) — reported, not recommended, not evaluated.
- **A proto blame-avoidance mechanism (C.2.c).** Top officials fear investigation and shape behaviour to avoid attracting it, *because they know* a large share of organisational behaviour is irrational relative to formal goals. Theory-only parallel to Hood; see [[blame-avoidance-and-negativity-bias]].
- **Rules and inertia as rational, not pathological (C.6).** Extensive rules are a rational response to a bureau's decision and communication problems. This is a 1964 statement of the distinction [[red-tape]] records from Bozeman — neutral formalisation versus burden — and is worth citing there as the earlier, theory-only version of it.
- **Territorial conflict as rational self-protection (C.7).** Where jurisdictional boundaries are ambiguous and bureaus are technically interdependent, turf conflict is a rational self-protective response to uncoordinated external decisions, not "petty jealousy".
- **Power shifts upward (Law 2 / B.2).** Unrestrained conflict at any level of a hierarchy shifts power upward — applied both inside bureaus and to local-versus-federal government.
- **Free services generate quasi-prices (Law 4 / B.4).** Requests for free services "always rise to exhaust capacity", and the bureau rations with informal quasi-prices: delays, demands for favours, lengthy forms.
- **Budget is an input, not an output (C.8).** A "small government budget" critique can be wrong on its own terms: the same budget could buy far more output if bureaus performed efficiently under full information.
- **Selective recruitment beats indoctrination (C.9).** Bureaus raise goal-homogeneity by recruiting for ideology rather than by indoctrinating after hire, and higher homogeneity lowers the cost of operating the bureau. This is an explicit mechanism chain, and it is the one hypothesis in the paper that points squarely at a variable [[personnel-economics-of-the-state]] measures with randomised evidence — Downs's version is pure assertion, theirs is randomised, and they reach compatible conclusions about job-advertisement content changing who applies.

## Downs and bureau age — theoretical, and the first of its kind in this wiki

**[model — and this is the single most consequential item on the page, for reasons that are about the wiki's record rather than about Downs's evidence]**

Compendium **C.11** states that bureaus have "predictable life-cycles" and, once established, almost never die "within a given historical era" — "as with politicians, few die and none retire." As they age they:

1. learn to be more efficient;
2. develop more and more extensive rules and regulations;
3. **shift their goals from performing their functions well to maintaining their organizational structures**;
4. become increasingly subject to inertia;
5. expand the scope of their functions.

Item 3 is goal displacement tied explicitly to **elapsed bureau age**. That matters because [[goal-displacement-and-bureaucratic-ritualism]] records Merton's mechanism as **explicitly age- and size-independent by his own construction**, and because six consecutive research jobs in this wiki have looked for institution-level age evidence and found none.

**Three things must be said in the same breath, or this reads as progress on the age question when it is not:**

- **Downs supplies no data of any kind**, here or anywhere in the document. C.11 is a bare assertion with no visible derivation — Downs gives the sequence of what happens with age but not why, beyond "as they grow older, they learn to be more efficient…".
- The disagreement with Merton is therefore **a conflict between two models, not between two bodies of evidence**. Both are undata'd. Filed as [[downs-vs-merton-on-age-dependence]].
- **Size is not the variable.** In this document size appears only as the definitional threshold for what counts as a bureau at all, never as a continuous variable inside any hypothesis. There is no Downs claim that larger bureaus behave differently from smaller ones.

**[wiki synthesis]** The correct statement of what changed: an age-dependent decay mechanism at organisational level now **exists in theory** in this wiki, where previously the wiki's own decay sources disowned the age reading unprompted. It has no measurement behind it, and Downs runs no test that a critic could fail. See [[open-questions]] Q60 and Q100, and [[decay-as-real-vs-decay-as-overstated]], where Downs sits on Position A as a model-only addition contributing zero data.

## Wilson: five problems that cannot be solved together

**[model]** Wilson's structure is a tradeoff claim. Each of five "bureaucracy problems" is the concern of a different constituency, and satisfying one constituency's criterion structurally works against another's:

| Problem | Whose concern | The tradeoff Wilson names |
|---|---|---|
| **Accountability** | The President's staff — a bureaucracy answering to a national power centre | More responsiveness to clients necessarily means less accountability to central directives |
| **Equity** | The courts — like cases treated alike, by rules known in advance | More equity means less responsiveness; general rules preclude exception-making |
| **Efficiency** | Businessmen, and McNamara-style analysts — properly, "an optimal relationship between objectives and resources", **not** spending less money | Fiscal-integrity discipline crowds out programme-budgeting reform |
| **Responsiveness** | Individual citizens, and Congress via casework | Only markets solve it, on Wilson's account; Congress's channel "works only occasionally" because Congress rarely has a clear collective will |
| **Fiscal integrity** | The political "outs" — no scandal, no misuse of funds | Wins by default (below) |

**The load-bearing claim: fiscal integrity dominates *because it is the only observable one*.** Everybody recognises a financial scandal when they see it; the other four require the programme's goals to be stated clearly first, and they usually are not. **Ease of observation selects the operative performance criterion, independent of which criterion actually matters for the programme's purpose.**

**[wiki synthesis]** This is a plain-language 1967 pre-statement of the multitasking mechanism — reward what is measurable, starve the rest — reached without a model and without a test; see [[multitask-incentive-theory]]. It is also a second, independently-arrived-at route into goal displacement with a **different driver from Merton's**: Merton's is rule-internalisation and esprit de corps, Wilson's is pure observability. And it is a precursor to Hood's blame-avoidance thesis, with fiscal scandal as "the all-consuming fear of responsible executives."

**Do not merge it into D50.** Wilson's claim here is about competition among *multiple, mutually incompatible criteria applied to the same agency*, where the most observable one wins by default — not about classifying an agency by how observable its own single output is. That is why the axis it suggests is registered as **`rejected`** at [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D103**, as likely subsumed by D25/D50/D51 with an explicit readmission condition.

## Wilson's lever, and what he argues against

**[model — argued, not evidenced]** The single lever: **define programme objectives clearly before administration begins.** Claimed to improve accountability, equity, efficiency and fiscal-integrity tolerance simultaneously — though not responsiveness. The evidence offered is one illustrative contrast: cash transfers / negative income tax (clear, measurable goal) against casework, counselling and community action (diffuse goals). Wilson himself predicts, correctly by his own irony, that the diffuse-goal approaches will be tried first anyway.

He argues **against** the standard reorganisation remedies by name: Hoover Commissions, management studies, consultants, coordinating committees, "czars". A secondary lever — better people — is raised and explicitly downplayed as necessary but insufficient, because the talent constraint is absolute and training cannot fix it. On which: Wilson's one genuinely sharp public/private observation is that talent scarcity is universal but its *denial* is government-specific — "everywhere except in government… the scarcity of talent is accepted as a fact of life", contrasted with universities and with private firms where good ideas fail because good executives "are both hard to find and expensive to hire." That is a claim about programme design outrunning executive supply, and it is asserted, not measured.

## Two smaller items, recorded so they are not lost

- **[model] A fourth presumption of public-sector risk aversion.** "The career civil service is not ordinarily attractive to people with a taste for risk" — a bare selection-effect assertion with zero support, offered to explain why responsiveness is structurally hard for tenured staff. It joins Hood, Bozeman, Hammond and the OECD on the presuming side of [[presumed-vs-measured-public-sector-risk-aversion]] and does not move it.
- **[model] Wilson's one scale claim is unoperationalised.** "*There are inherent limits to what can be accomplished by large hierarchical organizations*" — italicised in the original, with no threshold and no measure of "large". His second scale-flavoured claim (the foreign-office example) is about the combinatorial complexity of the *policy domain*, not the size of the institution managing it.

## The Weber gap

**[wiki synthesis — applies to every "Weberian" claim in this batch]** **Weber's own text is not available to this wiki** — the only open copy is a scanned image with no text layer, and OCR failed. Neither source here is a route to it. Downs cites Weber once and narrowly, for the point that bureaus require a money economy rather than barter. Wilson's single Weber reference is a bare, unelaborated "Max Weber had pointed out" that Soviet bureaucrats would work for themselves. **Do not reconstruct Weber's position from either.** See [[weberian-structure-and-growth]], where the same gap is stated against the batch's empirical sources.

## What this page does not establish

**[wiki synthesis]**

- **Nothing here is tested.** Not one hypothesis on this page has been checked against data by its author, and neither author claims otherwise.
- **Neither source contains a rent-seeking or budget-maximisation mechanism.** Downs explicitly does not discuss rent extraction; there is no budget variable in his growth account. See [[rent-seeking-and-the-welfare-cost-of-transfers]] for the batch's rent mechanism, which is a different literature that happens to share a camp.
- **Neither supplies levers this wiki can use.** Downs is descriptive throughout and recommends nothing. Wilson's goal-clarity lever is illustrated, not measured, and sits at tier (iii) of [[reform-levers]] on its evidence.
- **Scope.** Downs claims his environmental assumptions hold across "democratic, totalitarian, monarchical, traditionalist, or any other" society, then concedes his illustrations are 1960s-US: "that betrays only my own limitations, not those of the theory itself." Wilson claims no generality at all — 1960s US federal executive branch, written in the middle of Great Society programme expansion, and he never states whether his diagnosis is meant to transfer to private hierarchies even though several of his mechanisms are phrased generally enough to.
- **Ideological placement, stated because it matters when weighing this batch.** Downs names his lineage explicitly: Adam Smith forward, with Buchanan and Tullock and with Simon and March as antecedents. Wilson stages a cross-ideological alliance to position himself above faction, and is substantively sceptical of government administrative capacity and of reorganisation as a fix. Both sit in the camp that Besley, Burgess, Khan & Xu describe as having generated "negative stereotypes" of bureaucrats and as having marginalised the study of bureaucracy within economics — see [[organizational-economics-of-the-state]] for the other side of that argument, which is written by people who say so.
