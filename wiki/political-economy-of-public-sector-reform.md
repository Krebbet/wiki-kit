# Political Economy of Public Sector Reform

Bunse & Fritz's World Bank working paper is the wiki's first source that puts a number on how often deliberate public-sector reform works, and its central number is a **gap**: across ~134 World Bank public-sector-reform operations in FY2000–2010, roughly **75% were rated successful at the operation level, while only 47% of countries showed genuine public-sector transformation** (IEG 2011). The authors do not treat the higher number as the answer. They attribute the gap, in their own text, to the Bank selecting reform components that are politically easy and do not threaten anyone's rents — which is to say, the lever that raises the measured success rate is partly *responsible for* the gap between measured success and real change. Underneath sits a collective-action mechanism: reform costs are concentrated, immediate and borne by identifiable losers, while benefits are diffuse and take **10–20 years** to arrive, longer than almost any government's tenure.

**Evidence tier — two layers of very different strength, and the authors say so.**
- **(a) empirical, the stronger layer:** a near-census of the Bank's own public-sector lending — 134 rated operations across 128 potential client countries, 28 reviewed in depth, plus a Quality Assurance Group sample (N = 17) against an N = 180 baseline. Even here the authors repeatedly self-flag: "the sample size is too small to allow broad generalization," the evidence is "not hard evidence," and IEG ratings are composites that do not control for country difficulty or risk level.
- **(b) weaker, and the bulk of the paper:** a literature synthesis built almost entirely on single-country descriptive case studies (Brazil, Ghana, Tanzania, Turkey, Indonesia, Jordan, Singapore, Bangladesh). The authors' own verdict on it: "most research is not sufficiently systematic and comparative to allow clear and generalizable lessons," and they cannot "test their relative weight or eliminate those that may matter less."
- **The deep causal claims are not tested by the portfolio review.** The review tests correlations (attention to political economy against outcome rating); it does not test the clientelism and time-horizon mechanisms that carry the argument.

## The two rates, and the gap between them

**[empirical]** The paper's own framing of the disconnect is what makes it usable. Four levels of analysis are in play and it names them: country political-economy conditions; government commitment; the individual Bank *operation* (the unit IEG rates); and the reform *sub-type* (public financial management, civil-service reform, decentralisation, revenue, judicial, anti-corruption — each with distinct political cost).

- **~75% (two-thirds to three-quarters) of operations** rated satisfactory at the operation level.
- **47% of countries** showing genuine public-sector transformation over the same period.
- **78% of failed operations** cite political-economy obstacles in their completion reports — i.e. the failures are not mostly technical.
- Instrument type moves the rate: Development Policy Loans, whose conditions are front-loaded as prior actions, do better than Investment Loans, whose success depends on implementation after disbursement.
- Scope varies wildly and unsystematically: single operations bundle **2 to 17 distinct reform sub-issues**, at $2m to $100m. No size-to-success relationship is drawn.

**[wiki synthesis]** The load-bearing point is not the 75% and not the 47% but the *mechanism connecting them*, which the authors supply and which is self-incriminating: **measured success is partly an artefact of component selection.** Reform teams choose the technical, de jure components that do not eliminate anyone's rent stream, those components get done, the operation is rated satisfactory, and the state is not transformed. This is the only source in the batch where an institution documents its own success metric being decoupled from the thing the metric is supposed to track.

## Why reform is hard: the mechanism

**[b — mechanism with case illustration, not statistical test]**

- **Asymmetric cost/benefit timing.** Costs are concentrated, immediate, and land on identifiable losers — elites, clientelist networks, entrenched bureaucrats, and officials whose informal or illegal remuneration the reform is designed to remove (Harun 2007 on Indonesia). Benefits are diffuse, long-term, and accrue to whoever holds office later. Durable improvement in state functional capability takes **10–20 years** (Pritchett & de Weijer 2010, cited). Reform therefore requires cross-government persistence that the electoral cycle does not supply.
- **Limited access orders** (North, Wallis & Weingast 2010, cited extensively). Rent distribution is what buys elite peace; a reform that closes a rent channel is not a technical adjustment but a withdrawal of the payments holding the coalition together. This is a direct restatement of North's non-efficiency lock-in — elites preserve rent-preserving rules because they buy political peace, not because those rules are cost-economising. See [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]] and [[efficiency-of-institutions-north-vs-williamson]].
- **Veto players and announced-but-not-implemented reform.** Case evidence includes a loan blocked in parliament for 15 months, and completion reports criticising the Bank's own failure to anticipate veto points. The authors always pair veto-point *count* with *players who oppose the reform* — which is Hammond's interaction claim in the field rather than the naive count story. See [[veto-points-and-bureaucratic-autonomy]]. The failure mode has a name in this literature: Brunsson's "organization of hypocrisy", reforms "paid lip service" (Toonen 2001).
- **The autocrat's double bind** (Gehlbach & Keefer 2011, cited). Meritocratic civil-service reform lets an autocrat make credible commitments to loyal officials — but the same transparent, rule-bound apparatus lowers the cost of those officials organising against the regime if it reneges. Reform strengthens and threatens the ruler simultaneously. A sharp, source-specific reason credible commitment is not a free lever even for a ruler who wants it. See [[credible-commitment]].
- **Optimism bias on the donor side.** Implementing agencies systematically overestimate government commitment and underestimate resistance, and Bank teams have an internal incentive to downplay risk to get operations approved. The authors state this about their own institution.

## What opens the door

**[b — Kingdon-style theory plus case narrative; no base rate anywhere]** Crises, growth episodes, and external anchors (EU accession, post-conflict state-building, globalisation) periodically shift the cost-benefit calculus and open "windows of opportunity" that a policy entrepreneur can exploit. Brazil, Ghana and Tanzania are the narrated cases. **No systematic test is offered, and no count of windows that opened and were not used.** The independent corroboration for the shape of this claim is [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-policy-change]], which supplies the distributional evidence that policy change is episodic and the matching caution that agenda access is necessary but not sufficient.

## Named axes of variation

**[model]** Time horizon of reform benefits against political tenure; clientelism / limited-access-order intensity; veto-point count and intensity; electoral system (proportional and open-list vs. majoritarian); programmatic vs. non-programmatic parties; regime type; aid dependency; pre-existing state capacity; reform *salience to citizens* crossed with reform *difficulty / rent-threat* (treated as an independent 2×2, not one axis); **de jure vs. de facto reform depth**; scope of a single intervention; instrument type. Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D73 (reform-benefit time horizon vs. principal tenure) and noted in the D-row commentary for the rest.

## Levers, with their evidence attached

**[c — wiki synthesis of the source's own grading]** Kept in the source's own order, with what actually backs each. The full triage across the batch is at [[reform-levers]].

1. **Differentiate country context ex ante** (propitious / mixed / unlikely) rather than engaging uniformly. Backing: a portfolio correlation between WGI government-effectiveness and outcome rating that the authors themselves call "broadly" present but "less strong than might be expected."
2. **Concentrate effort on implementation during windows of opportunity** rather than lengthy up-front design. Backing: Kingdon's theory plus three case narratives. No base rate, no test.
3. **Ex-ante political-economy analysis at project design.** Backing: **the weakest in the paper** — two operations with such analysis are both currently rated satisfactory (n = 2), plus a QAG sample of 17 (9 responsive vs. 7 limited/unresponsive) in which responsive operations do better. The authors call this "not hard evidence" and pre-empt the obvious reverse-causality objection by noting no sign that responsiveness is targeted at easy countries. Note the standing incentive: the authors are Bank staff evaluating whether the Bank's own emerging political-economy practice pays off.
4. **"Quick wins" to build credibility.** Backing: **the source's own cases cut against it.** Ghana and Tanzania show quick wins not reliably cascading into broader reform, and the authors say so rather than overselling. This lever belongs in the prescription tier, not the evidence tier.
5. **Target technical / de jure components that do not threaten elite rents** as an easier entry point. Backing: real portfolio support — these components do succeed more often — paired with the authors' explicit warning that this is what produces the 75%/47% disconnect in the first place.

## The connection to isomorphic mimicry

**[c — wiki synthesis; a mechanism-level connection, not a citation conflict]** Bunse & Fritz's gap looks like an instance of exactly what [[isomorphic-mimicry-and-capability-traps]] diagnoses: measured success tracking **form-compliance** (a law passed, an agency created, a procedure adopted, an operation closed satisfactorily) rather than **function** (what the state can actually do). The two sources are independent — Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock cite Andrews (2009) on "isomorphic facades" and Bunse & Fritz cite the same concept in passing — and neither tests the other. What makes the pairing worth recording is that Bunse & Fritz supply from inside a donor institution the quantified version of a gap that Andrews et al. can only illustrate with hand-picked cases. Keep the direction of support straight: this is the *diagnosis* corroborating across two sources, not the PDIA prescription gaining any support.

Distinguish this from [[red-tape]]: an isomorphic façade is legitimacy-seeking mimicry, red tape is a burden imposed by control costs. Different mechanisms, kept separate in the wiki's failure-mode taxonomy.

## What is not established

- **No private-sector claim of any kind.** This is World Bank public-sector-governance lending only, predominantly low- and middle-income countries, FY2000–2010. Nothing here transfers to firms without independent evidence.
- **The 75% and the 47% are not measured on the same object.** One is a rating of Bank operations, the other an IEG judgement about countries. The paper is unusually explicit that this is a level mismatch — which is what makes the gap interpretable at all, but it also means the two numbers cannot be differenced into a clean "reform failure rate."
- **The mechanisms are untested.** Clientelism intensity, time horizons and limited access orders carry the argument and are evidenced by case narrative.
- **Institutional prior.** World Bank staff, one a serving Senior Governance Specialist, reviewing the Bank's own portfolio and assessing whether the Bank's own practice is worth expanding. The paper is nonetheless self-critical on its headline number, which counts in its favour and does not remove the incentive.
