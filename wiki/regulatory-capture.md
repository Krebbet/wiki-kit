# Regulatory Capture

Three sources on one page, deliberately, because all three of this batch's ingest summaries proposed the pairing independently and because the disagreement between them is more informative than any one of them alone. **Stigler** supplies the canonical model: regulation is *acquired* by the industry it regulates and operated primarily for that industry's benefit, because concentrated groups can organise cheaply relative to their stakes while diffuse publics cannot. **Carpenter & Moss** accept that capture is real and reject that it is general: it is a matter of degree, its diagnosis in the existing literature routinely fails basic evidentiary standards, and they propose a strict three-part test plus a strong/weak/corrosive typology intended to make targeted prevention possible instead of reflexive deregulation. **Novak** attacks the concept's status as an unexamined "ground norm" of American regulatory scholarship, showing its founding texts rest on a demonstrably wrong short history of American regulation — while explicitly not denying that capture happens. The three converge on one thing that matters for this wiki: **none of them supports capture as an age-driven phenomenon**, and the two critics show that the canonical evidence for the age reading is a single 1892 anecdote laundered through decades of citation.

**Evidence tier note.** Stigler: **(b) formal model with two quantitative tests** — one robust (trucking), one the author himself calls non-robust (licensing), and otherwise illustration. Carpenter & Moss: **(b) literature critique and definitional essay** — no new primary empirical work; the volume's central empirical claim (capture can be mitigated) lives in chapters this wiki has not read. Novak: **(b) historiographical critique** — close reading of canonical texts plus citation of a revisionist historical literature whose primary archival evidence he cites rather than reproduces. No source on this page measures capture in an agency.

## Position A — Stigler: regulation is acquired

**[model]** The unit is two-level and Stigler does not always keep them apart: the *industry or occupation* as a rational collective actor deciding whether to demand regulation, and the *legislative and regulatory process* as the supply side. The **agency itself is a passive instrument** shaped by the resulting equilibrium — no intra-agency dynamics, no headcount, no hierarchy, no organisational life of its own. This is a political-economy model of why regulation takes the shape it does, not an organisational-behaviour model of the regulator.

The mechanism, stated explicitly:

1. Political power is coercive and can redistribute wealth through four instruments: direct subsidy, control of entry, control of substitutes and complements, and price-fixing.
2. Democratic decision-making is costly, infrequent and "global" — everyone votes on everything — so voters rationally stay ignorant of any single issue, and the channels reaching them are "filtered or noisy".
3. Concentrated groups can organise cheaply relative to their stakes and pay for political outcomes in votes and resources (campaign funds, party-worker patronage). Diffuse groups cannot organise in proportion to their aggregate loss.
4. Legislators and parties are modelled as profit-seeking sellers of coercive power — explicitly analogised to Demsetz's natural-monopoly firm — supplying regulation to whichever side can pay.

**[wiki synthesis]** Note what this is and is not. It is a genuine mechanism, not a bare correlation; every step is derived from assumed rational behaviour, and no step is independently verified against data in the paper.

### What Stigler actually tested

**[empirical — the paper's strongest evidence]** **Trucking weight limits.** A regression of 1932–33 state truck weight limits on (a) trucks per 1,000 agricultural labour force, (b) average railroad haul length, and (c) share of state highways with a high-quality surface, across 46–48 states. All three coefficients significant with the correct sign, R² = 0.24–0.50 (his Table 2). This is a real structural test of a specific falsifiable prediction — which states set which limits, given the local balance of pro-truck and pro-rail interests — and it is the best-evidenced claim in the paper.

**[empirical — explicitly weak, by the author's own statement]** **Occupational licensing timing.** A regression of year-of-first-licensure on occupation size and urbanisation across 11 occupations and 48 states (Table 3). Stigler's own words: "The results are not robust... over half the regression coefficients are not significant (and in these cases often of inappropriate sign)." In the pooled check, urbanisation is significant (t = 4.00) and occupation size is **not** (t = 0.59) — one of his two hypothesised drivers fails outright.

**[empirical — descriptive only]** Licensed vs. partially-licensed vs. unlicensed occupations (Table 4): licensed occupations show higher income, more stable membership, less business employment; national-market occupations go unlicensed. No significance test, no controls, no identification. Income differences could precede and cause licensing rather than result from it; the source neither rules this out nor tests direction.

**Everything else is illustration chosen to fit the theory**, not a sample designed to test it: oil import quotas (~$5bn/yr cost), airline mail subsidies ($1.5bn through 1968), merchant marine subsidies (~$3bn since WWII), the CAB admitting zero new trunk lines since 1938, the FDIC's ~60% cut in new-bank entry, savings-and-loan dividend caps, butter versus margarine, building-code opposition to labour-saving materials, prohibition of interest on demand deposits.

**[wiki synthesis]** The gap is the point: the master claim — regulation is acquired by the industry *as a general rule* — is asserted up front, supported by illustration, and tested only partially and unevenly. The one robust result tests a narrow derived prediction about regulatory *design details* inside an already-regulated industry. **No industry-level test of "was this industry captured, yes or no" appears anywhere in the paper.**

### The falsifiability gap, visible in Stigler's own text

**[wiki synthesis, but every element is quoted from the source]** Three moves, in the source itself, prevent the theory from being contradicted:

- The single explicit falsification statement is footnote 8: "A direct and informed vote on oil import quotas would reject the scheme. (If it did not, our theory of rational political processes would be contradicted.)" A direct and informed vote on a complex quota scheme has never occurred and the paper proposes no way to construct one. Not implementable.
- Counter-examples are absorbed rather than counted. "Onerous" regulations that visibly harm the regulated industry — heavy excise taxes on whiskey, on playing cards — are explicitly not disconfirming: "these onerous regulations, however, are exceptional and can be explained by the same theory that explains beneficial ('acquired') regulation."
- The conclusion doubles down: the standard economist's complaint that the ICC is pro-railroad is dismissed as "misdirected", i.e. the finding the theory predicts is treated as confirmation rather than as a case that could have gone either way. Reform is waved off as futile by definition, not by evidence that it was tried.

**No operational ex-ante falsification condition is stated anywhere in the source.** This is exactly the unfalsifiability charge the critics on this page bring, and it does not have to be inferred from silence.

### Stigler's capture is not an age phenomenon

**[wiki synthesis — load-bearing for this batch]** This is a cross-sectional equilibrium model, not a lifecycle model, and the source volunteers as much without being asked.

- No variable for agency age, years since founding, or agency maturity appears in either regression or anywhere in the theoretical argument.
- Where age-adjacent facts appear they read as **founding-conditions** stories: motor-carrier regulation is explained by railroads organising against trucking competition as it emerged in the 1920s — regulation arrives already shaped by industry interest at the moment of creation.
- The CAB's zero-new-entrants record is cited as running continuously "since it was created in 1938" — 33 years at the time of writing — presented as a **constant, not an escalating trend**. No comparison of the CAB's early years against its later years is offered.
- The mechanism itself predicts capture-like outcomes as an equilibrium property of *any* regulation with the relevant industry and political characteristics, operative from inception. Nothing in it requires or predicts a lag, an onset threshold, or intensification with age.

**Any page citing Stigler for an age-dependent capture claim is over-reading the source.** This is one of three founding decay-camp texts in this batch that state their own mechanism's age-independence unprompted — see also [[goal-displacement-and-bureaucratic-ritualism]] and [[cooptation]].

### The dimensions Stigler names

Concentration of the regulated group (geographic, in large cities, lowering organising cost; and industry concentration among few firms, raising resources per firm); group size (more votes but higher solicitation cost, with the "fixed size of the political market" bending the cost curve sub-linearly); group income and wealth; elasticity of demand or supply for the group's product or labour (determining whether cash subsidy or entry control is preferred and whether price-fixing is feasible at all); stability of membership and tenure (longer expected tenure raises the payoff from entry control); presence of a cohesive organised **opposition** (dampens capture); market scope (national-market occupations — engineers, college teachers, accountants — are effectively uncapturable at state level because of resource mobility); one-party vs. competitive party systems on the supply side.

**[empirical — a distributive detail worth keeping]** Political allocation of quotas is **regressive within the industry**: small firms receive proportionally larger quotas than cost-minimising allocation would give them (Table 1, oil import quotas), because political weight is not output share.

## Position B — Carpenter & Moss: capture is a variable, and the literature has been sloppy

**[model]** Their reframing is that capture is a **matter of degree**, not a Stiglerian near-inevitability, and that the degree is a function of institutional checks that vary by agency — courts, consumer advocacy, OIRA review, internal agency procedure. Their definition is deliberately **mechanism-agnostic**, "not model-dependent or mechanism-dependent", designed to subsume every channel the literature has proposed.

### The three-part evidentiary test

**[model]** To diagnose capture, a claim must establish all three of:

1. **A specified public-interest baseline** — a stated standard from which the policy is a departure. Without it, "capture" is unfalsifiable by construction.
2. **Intent** — demonstrated industry intent to influence the policy, not merely industry benefit from it.
3. **Action and effect** — demonstrated action that moved policy toward the industry's preference.

And a fourth requirement that functions as a gate on the other three: **innocent alternatives must be ruled out.** Industry can benefit from a regulation by coincidence, by mistake, as a genuine byproduct of public-serving policy, or through **bureaucratic drift** — all of which are named as explanations that must be excluded before capture is diagnosed.

**[wiki synthesis]** The test is aimed at two failure modes of scholarship rather than of institutions, and Carpenter & Moss name them as such:

- **Tautological inference**, after Noll: "impossible to imagine regulation could be imposed without redistributing income... a look for winners is virtually certain to succeed." Stigler's method of deducing true intent from actual effects is close to unfalsifiable by construction.
- **Functionalist fallacy** — inferring design intent from observed function, "actor-centered functionalism" per Pierson, explicitly called invalid as capture evidence. And motive is not a proxy for capture: "every death was a suspicious death if one looked only at motive."

They apply the test critically to prior econometric capture claims — Bartel & Thomas (1987) on "predation through regulation", Viscusi (1992) on cotton dust and OSHA, Djankov et al. (2002) "Regulation of Entry" — arguing each over-inferred capture from correlational or aggregate data without establishing industry intent.

### The typology

- **Strong capture** — industry influence is severe enough that no regulation at all, or full replacement of the agency, would better serve the public. This is the Stiglerian limit case.
- **Weak capture** — influence reduces but does not eliminate the net positive social benefit of regulation against a no-regulation baseline.
- **Corrosive capture** — their own coinage, and a reversal of the classical framing: capture operating *through deregulation* — weakening enforcement, cutting stringency and compliance cost, absent public support or welfare rationale. They argue this was the dominant real-world pattern in the 2000s (financial deregulation, OTS/OCC preemption of state mortgage law, the MMS as a "rubber stamp" on oil-rig safety). Cross-cuts strong/weak.
- **Cultural capture** — the regulator's cognitive frame captured, possibly non-consciously, through repeated social interaction with industry (Kwak).

Mechanisms catalogued: implicit bribes and rent-seeking (Laffont-Tirole; Grossman-Helpman); **capture by threat** — industry threatens political or legal retaliation rather than paying (Gordon-Hafer; Dal Bo & Di Tella); cultural capture; legislative "hardwiring" of outcomes into statute via lobbying and contributions (McNollgast; Huber & Shipan); and boundary manipulation (preemption, regulatory arbitrage).

### The coordination asymmetry

**[model]** One claim from this source is a genuine, general axis and is registered as one: **entry-barrier capture requires coordinating a subset of firms against others and is therefore hard; deregulatory and cultural capture require only a single shared "less regulation" message and are therefore easy.** This is an explicit argument about why corrosive capture may be more tractable for industry than the classical Stiglerian kind — and it predicts that the *form* capture takes depends on how many actors must agree. See [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D64.

### The Olney letter

**[empirical — the batch's cleanest piece of historiographical work]** Richard Olney's 1892 letter to a railroad president: "the older such a Commission gets to be, the more inclined it will be found to take the business and railroad view of things." This is the canonical textual source for capture-as-ageing. Carpenter & Moss decline to treat it as evidence: "Olney's letter, although certainly powerful, provides no direct evidence that the Commission did in fact 'take the business and railroad view of things.' Rather, it reveals only that one potentially interested observer predicted that the [ICC] would take such a view."

They use it as their lead case of how weak anecdotal evidence becomes canonical through repeated citation — Bernstein, Huntington and Senate floor remarks all cite the same one letter. **No systematic age-to-capture claim is made or endorsed anywhere in this source; the one age-flavoured claim on the record is flagged as unproven prediction.**

## Position C — Novak: capture as an unexamined ground norm

**[model]** Novak's causal claims are about the *literature*, not about institutions. Two of them:

1. Capture theory's temporal narrative — regulation as a post-1887 aberration from a laissez-faire baseline — is factually wrong per a revisionist historiography documenting continuous American economic regulation from 1787, and colonial "commonwealth" governance before that (his own *The Myth of the Weak American State* and *The People's Welfare*; Mashaw; Richard John; Edling; the commonwealth studies). This wrong premise drives the conclusions.
2. Capture is presented as a newly discovered 20th-century social-scientific insight when private or factional corruption of public power is the continuously theorised central concern of the Western political tradition from Plato and Aristotle through Madison to the Progressives. "Capture theory mistook the discovery of a general precondition for an immanent and incisive critique."

**He offers no competing mechanism for why agencies behave as they do.** This is a critique of an explanatory apparatus, not a rival causal theory — and the capture mechanisms inside the literature he surveys (Huntington's marasmus, Bernstein's life-cycle) are reported and contextualised, not endorsed or independently tested by him.

**[wiki synthesis — grading his own evidence]** Strongest: the citation-pattern argument that capture theorists "rarely cite any" of the substantial modern historical literature on the long American regulatory tradition — verifiable but narrow. Weakest: the Plato-to-Madison argument, which establishes that concern about corruption is ancient but does not by itself establish that the modern application is over- or under-diagnosed. He does not fully disentangle "the concept is old" from "the modern application is wrong."

### The Charles Francis Adams finding

**[empirical — the concrete, falsification-relevant datapoint]** The canonical capture case is the ICC and the railroads. Novak reads Charles Francis Adams's own 1871 writings — *before the ICC existed* — and finds the capture question explicitly anticipated at the moment of design: "Who will guard the virtue of the tribunal? Why should the corporations not deal with [the commissions] as [they did] with the legislatures?" The independent commission form was chosen precisely to relocate decision rights away from state legislatures held to be irreparably corruptible — "there is no power which can purify a corrupted legislature", against a backdrop of "the sturdy corporation beggars who infested the lobby".

**[wiki synthesis — and flagged in the ingest as inference, not Novak's words]** If the capture risk was fully anticipated and theorised ex ante, then "marasmus" and ageing-into-capture is not a story of decline from a healthy youth. It is a story of a risk present from inception. Novak never tests whether older agencies show more capture symptoms than younger ones; this is an implication of his broader argument that capture is a constitutive, ever-present condition of self-governing institutions rather than a late-life development.

### What Novak does not claim

He does not deny that capture is real, that drift toward the regulated industry can happen, or that it is worth preventing — his chapter appears in the Carpenter & Moss volume and endorses its aim. His target is the concept's deployment as an unexamined ground norm and the deregulatory prescription that is treated as automatically following from a capture diagnosis. **[wiki synthesis]** His own normative conclusion — reject deregulation as the default remedy, favour continuous Madisonian redesign, "all the checks and balances that human ingenuity can devise" — outruns the strictly historiographical evidence he presents: showing capture theory's *premises* are shaky does not establish that deregulation is the wrong policy in any given case.

## The age question, specifically

**[wiki synthesis]** Capture-as-an-age-phenomenon is the one genuinely **contested** proposition on this page, and the contest is asymmetric in an unusual way: the affirmative case has famous texts and no measurement, and the negative case is made by dedicated critics working on the affirmative case's own sources.

| | The age reading | What this batch shows about it |
|---|---|---|
| Canonical texts | Huntington 1952, "The Marasmus of the ICC" — marasmus is "a biological pathology featuring a gradual and continuous wasting away of the body from a morbid cause", applied to an agency "during its sixty-five years of existence". Bernstein 1955 extends it to an explicit life-cycle: commissions decline "into a ripe 'old age,' where sclerotic relations with specific interest groups yielded agencies protecting the industries they were originally designed to regulate". Olney 1892. | Novak reports the genealogy and does not endorse it. Carpenter & Moss show the Olney letter is an interested party's *prediction*, re-cited as data by Bernstein, Huntington and the Senate floor. Novak shows the risk was anticipated at the ICC's 1871 design stage, which removes the healthy-youth baseline the decline story needs. |
| The rational-choice text | Stigler 1971 | Contains no age variable, treats the CAB's record as a constant rather than a trend, and locates capture in the *creation* of regulation. |
| A measurement of agency age against a capture indicator | — | **Nobody in this batch supplies one.** |

Neither critic tests an age correlation. That is worth stating plainly in both directions: **the age reading is not refuted, it is unevidenced** — and the specific evidence usually offered for it does not survive inspection.

## What all three agree on

**[wiki synthesis]** Strip out the disagreement and a real finding remains, and it is structural rather than temporal: **regulation and procedure create rents, and those rents accrue to organised, concentrated interests over diffuse publics.** Stigler models it and gives one robust regression on regulatory design details. Carpenter & Moss accept the entry-barrier mechanism as one real form of capture while denying its generality. Novak accepts that public/private entanglement in the allocation of economic privilege is real and old — Jacksonian special incorporation distributed "monopoly privileges, land grants, rights-of-way, and other valuable statutory benefits" long before the ICC. [[regulatory-accretion]] supplies the same finding in a modern instance (the JPMorgan "moat"), and [[state-capacity-and-level-of-government]] supplies it in procedural rather than substantive form. **This is a cross-sectional finding about who benefits. It is not an age-decay finding, and this wiki should not let the two blur.**

## Levers

**[wiki synthesis]** Weak on all three sides, and worth recording as a gap rather than a menu:

- **Stigler** proposes exactly one, in a single sentence, as a corollary rather than an argument: "change the political support for the Commission, and reward commissioners on a basis unrelated to their services to the carriers." No mechanism design, no evidence it has been tried.
- **Carpenter & Moss** offer a methodological lever — the three-part test as a gatekeeper against both over- and under-diagnosis, intended to enable graduated targeted reform instead of wholesale deregulation. Their substantive levers (consumer advocacy, judicial review, OIRA, unspecified internal agency mechanisms) are **pointers to chapters this wiki has not read**, not claims this source substantiates.
- **Novak** offers a meta-lever only: reject deregulation as the default prescription. His positive recommendation is explicitly non-programmatic and carries no claim that any specific check reliably prevents capture — markedly weaker than, say, Ostrom's design principles in [[polycentric-governance]], and it should be weighted accordingly.

## Scope and priors

**Stigler** — Chicago, a founding figure of public choice; this paper is a foundational text of the deregulatory intellectual case of the 1970s–80s. Priors visible in the prose, not merely inferable: the "public interest" view of regulation produces "uproarious laughter at the Petroleum Club"; criticising the ICC for being pro-railroad is like criticising "the Great Atlantic and Pacific Tea Company for selling groceries"; the closing line is programmatic advocacy for the rational-choice research agenda. Data span the 1890s–1969, US federal and state only. He never uses the word "capture" — it does not appear in the source.

**Carpenter & Moss** — Harvard; the Tobin Project, oriented toward evidence-based governance after 2008. They position themselves against both Chicago-school fatalism and blanket "capture is everywhere" narratives from left and right, and state their own commitment plainly: "regulation is also a fact of modern life and undoubtedly necessary in some circumstances." Their central optimism — the evidence strongly suggests capture can be prevented — outruns what the introduction itself presents.

**Novak** — legal history; the long-history revisionist school, sympathetic to the regulatory state's legitimacy and skeptical of deregulation narratives. He frames capture theory's rise as entangled with mid-century anti-statist politics and later Chicago public choice. US only, 18th century to early 21st, heaviest on 1871–1913 and the 1950s–70s. No cross-national comparison.

**All three are US-only and public-sector-only.** None claims the capture mechanism generalises to purely private governance; Stigler's mechanism specifically depends on the state's coercive taxing and prohibiting power, which has no direct private analogue — a scope-limiting feature of the theory rather than an oversight. Novak's invocation of the Hale/Veblen/Pound "private coercion" tradition — corporations as bearers of quasi-sovereign power, Pound's "new feudalism" — gestures at generality by citing older political theory, not by demonstrating it. Record as an open question.
