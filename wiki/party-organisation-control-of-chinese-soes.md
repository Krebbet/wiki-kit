# Party-organisation control of Chinese SOEs (party command vs chief responsibility, 1949-2024)

Jie Zeng's institutional history argues that Chinese Communist Party (CCP) branch control of state-owned enterprises (SOEs) has swung between a "party command" regime (party secretary decides, manager executes) and a "chief responsibility" regime (manager decides, party handles ideology and supervision) with the leadership's priority on economic efficiency versus political control, but has never disappeared. When corporatisation cut the direct administrative tie, the Party rebuilt control through four mechanisms: cadre appointment, cross-holding posts, ex-ante review of major issues, and charter amendments. The 2024 Company Law (art. 170) legalises a structure already built through intra-party documents; the formal board loses its position as the real decision forum and minority shareholders gain no recourse.

**Evidence tier note.** Mixed, and the layers differ in strength. (a) The regime history rests on documents (CCP regulations, State Council measures, five Company Law texts, CCP Constitution editions) and gives a dated, checkable sequence: **[empirical]** on texts. (b) The post-2013 "party-centric" practice claim rests on 41 interviews (2020-21, doctoral fieldwork), unreleased and confidential, clustered in Chongqing, Shanghai and Beijing. 14 respondents are SOE in-house lawyers, the rest outside lawyers, non-state in-house lawyers and private entrepreneurs. None are directors or party secretaries, and the same dozen interviewees support many claims. **[empirical]**, weak. (c) The stakeholder-benefit section is an argumentative claim resting on SASAC-published figures and a state-media report (Qiushi), which are self-reported by the party-state: **[model]**. Source type: English-language law-journal scholarship about China, written by a UK-based law scholar in the corporate-governance and law-and-development tradition. It is not primary Chinese-language scholarship, and the party documents it cites are mediated through the author's reading. The interviews describe pre-amendment practice (the 2024 law took effect 1 July 2024).

## The regime swing

**[empirical]** (documentary history) The history alternates between the two poles of one axis of decision-right location. The chief responsibility system replaces party command when leadership prioritises efficiency; the reverse follows when it prioritises political control. Turning points named: the 1961 Seventy Articles after the Great Leap Forward, the Cultural Revolution, 1978, 1982-86, post-1989, 1997, 2010, 2015 onward. Trigger variables cited: Tiananmen, the Asian Financial Crisis, loss of faith in socialism, Eastern European collapses, corruption and inequality, SASAC's supervisory weakness, and mixed-ownership dilution. **[wiki synthesis]** Seventy-five years of oscillation with ever-present party influence reads as persistence, not decay with age: the time series is of a regime, not of firms, and the source makes no per-institution age claim. Cf. [[gradual-institutional-change]]: intra-party practice layered over a corporate-law shell, then converted to statute ("reform first, regulate later").

## Four control-substitution mechanisms

**[empirical]** (documents plus interviews) Corporatisation (legal personality from 1988/1993) removed the administrative tie. The Party rebuilt control via:

1. **"Party Manages Cadres."** The CCP Organisation Department appoints leaders of strategically important central SOEs; SASAC appoints the rest subject to Department approval. Some central-SOE heads outrank the SASAC officials supervising them (rank inversion). See [[chinese-cadre-rotation-and-the-iron-triangle]].
2. **"Two-Way Entry and Cross-Holding Posts."** The same people sit on party committee, board and management. Leutert's count, via the source: joint appointments reached 89% of core central SOEs in Xi's first term (76% under Hu-Wen), with board chair = party secretary in more than 90% of firms. A 1986 rule separating secretary from chief is the counter-lever that was later reversed.
3. **"Three Majors and One Large."** Ex-ante party review of major issues. The list has a low threshold, so most decisions fall inside it.
4. **Charter amendments.** Party authority written into articles of association (2015-18, with a 2020 template). Art. 170 of the 2024 Company Law states that party organisations "shall play a leadership role... deliberate on major business and management matters".

**[empirical]** (interviews, weak) Boards are reported to be nominalised: single merged minutes signed by party committee, board and management, and skipped board meetings with minutes drafted afterwards. Nothing quantifies prevalence. The author concedes merged meetings are "difficult for inspection teams to discover retrospectively", so the practice is unobservable by design.

## Accountability and the minority-shareholder gap

**[empirical]** (statutory text) Removal runs only through intra-party discipline. Party-committee members without corporate roles fall outside director-liability rules. "De facto director" liability in the 2024 law reaches controlling shareholders and actual controllers, which a collective party committee is not. Party organisations answer upward to higher party organs and the Central Committee; accountability to shareholders is unspecified. 380 derivative suits were filed 2006-19 (about 27 per year). The minority-harm argument applies only where minority shareholders exist (listed or mixed-ownership SOEs). A 2024 minority repurchase right was introduced; its effect is unknown.

**[wiki synthesis]** The gap was closed by legalising the real holder of authority, not by restoring the formal one: the board's formal right to decide survives while the ex-ante approval right sits with an organ holding a decision right with no payoff claim and no liability. See [[formal-and-real-authority]] and [[decision-rights-and-authority-theory-of-the-firm]]. This adds a third party (the party organisation) to the shareholder/manager split in [[separation-of-ownership-and-control]]. It is also a control right that cash-flow and voting-rights tracing does not capture ([[corporate-ownership-and-control-around-the-world]]). Supervisory fragmentation (SASAC, Ministry of Finance, NDRC and the Organisation Department each hold a lever) fits [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]]. Formalisation re-couples practice to law, the reverse of the decoupling pattern in [[institutional-myths-and-decoupling]].

## Politicisation and caution

**[model]** (asserted, not measured) Stronger party building produces loyalty-weighted promotion; selection criteria that read "de, neng, qin, ji, lian" on paper are loyalty in practice. The anti-corruption campaign is said to produce leaders "too wary of trouble" who "stopped reforms". The link from party-centric structure to firm performance is explicitly untested. Scale enters as another asserted claim: SOE growth in assets, complexity and overseas operations is given as the reason SASAC could not supervise, which pulled the Party in. For the mechanism, see [[blame-avoidance-and-negativity-bias]] and [[risk-aversion-in-large-institutions]].

## Stakeholder benefit (a separate, weaker claim)

**[model]** The author argues the policy orientation of SOEs may benefit employees, communities and the environment, and may be "difficult to replicate in market economies". Cited support is secondary or state-published: an SOE wage premium of about 10% (1990-2017), employment held up near labour unrest, lower environmental non-compliance, and SASAC poverty-alleviation figures (RMB 100bn+ invested or introduced, RMB 54bn non-repayable aid over eight years, 37,000 dispatched personnel). Offsetting costs are also cited from secondary sources: lower ROA/ROE and an SOE productivity "30-40% of private firms" figure drawn from an IMF volume. Do not cite those figures to this article. Stakeholder gains and shareholder losses are never netted, so net welfare is untested, and corporatisation outcome evidence is mixed (Chen/Firth/Zhang vs Aivazian/Ge/Qiu). Mixed-ownership reform (4,000+ cases, RMB 1.5tn by 2020) made party building a precondition, so market discipline is diluted by design. **[wiki synthesis]** Read as a description of a state-capitalist structure where the party-state captures the resulting power, with policy-instrument roles (Belt and Road examples) taking priority over enterprise profit; compare the performance-conditional discipline of [[developmental-state-and-embedded-autonomy]].

## Scope and open questions

China only, state-controlled firms, 1949-2024, Leninist one-party state; strongest grounding 2013 to present. The source claims China is "the only jurisdiction" where a ruling party holds national-law authority in corporate governance without being a shareholder, and makes no comparison with other SOEs (Nordic, Temasek-type holdings). **[wiki synthesis]** Open: whether the loyalty-and-caution mechanism appears in any large political-appointee-run firm elsewhere. Candidate dimensions of variation for [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]]: direction of accountability (upward vs to owners), role-overlap rate across control organs, intra-party-first-then-statute formalisation lag, and decision-scope threshold of ex-ante political review. Offered levers (disclosure of party-organisation decisions, director liability for non-managerial party members) are description, not tested recommendations.
