# Personnel Economics of the State

Finan, Olken & Pande's Handbook of Field Experiments chapter aggregates roughly **40 field experiments and quasi-experiments** on who works for the state and how they are paid and monitored, plus original 32-country descriptive evidence on the public-sector pay, tenure and benefits premium. **This is the strongest evidence base in the `enabling-institutional-change` batch by a wide margin** — the only source in it with randomised identification and reported effect sizes — and it is also the source most easily over-read: what it identifies are narrow personnel mechanisms inside specific agencies, not the success rate of any reform programme. The chapter keeps two mechanisms analytically separate throughout: **selection** (job attributes change who applies, before anyone is on the job) and **incentives/monitoring** (conditional pay or better information changes the behaviour of people already employed). It also supplies the wiki's first genuinely RCT-evidenced material on personnel selection as a design variable — the gap [[open-questions]] Q4 has flagged since the foundations batch.

**Evidence tier — unusually clean, and the authors grade it themselves.**
- **(a) strongest:** randomised recruitment, pay and monitoring experiments, listed with effect sizes below.
- **(a) weaker:** the 32-country wage-premium regressions on household-survey micro-data — descriptive, explicitly not causal.
- **(b/weakest, and flagged by the authors):** cross-country correlational work on intrinsic motivation and performance (World Values Survey, Petrovsky's meta-analysis, Park & Rainey, Naff & Crum), and one non-randomised management-practices survey (Rasul & Rogger, Nigeria), on which the authors state the endogeneity problem plainly: "one might choose to give performance incentives to those bureaucrats who behave badly and to reward high performers with autonomy."
- The authors repeatedly call their own field "nascent", "scant" or "mixed". If anything the chapter under-claims.

## Why the state is a different employer

**[b — structured argument, five named mechanisms]** Section II.A gives five reasons state personnel economics differs systematically from the private-sector version: the state's near-indefinite horizon makes long-lived promises (pensions) credible in a way a firm's cannot be; its contracting technology is narrower and coarser because the ultimate principal — citizens, acting through elections — has a control mechanism the authors call "coarser and more limited" than shareholders'; state provision of subsidised, low-competition goods removes market discipline and raises the relative value of direct monitoring; the organisational mission differs (public service vs. profit), shaping self-selection; and the state leans on self-regulation where a firm would buy third-party audit.

**[empirical] The descriptive facts underneath.** Across 32 countries spanning $264–$45,710 per-capita GDP, the unadjusted public-sector wage premium exceeds **100%** in the poorest sample countries (Malawi, Niger, Tanzania, Kenya) and runs **4–20%** in the richest (Korea, UK, US); ex-communist countries show the opposite gradient and are run as a separate regression line. Public employees have about **three years longer tenure** than private-sector counterparts after adjustment, with no significant trend by country income. Wage compression is treated as a near-universal, defining feature of civil-service systems, contrasted with private-sector pay dispersion.

**[c — wiki synthesis; a nuance, not a conflict]** This five-mechanism list reads as a claim of *systematic, multi-mechanism divergence* between public and private personnel economics, which sits in tension with Dixit's position that public/private differences are "of degree, not kind" ([[incentives-under-multiple-principals]]). The tension is real but neither source engages the other: Finan, Olken & Pande never make a degree-vs-kind claim, and their own wage data — a premium that shrinks toward small in rich countries — is more consistent with a degree-graded story than their mechanism list is. Recorded as a nuance on that page, not as a conflict file.

## Selection: the job attributes change who applies

**[a — randomised]**

- **Higher recruitment wages improve the applicant pool with no motivation tradeoff.** Dal Bó, Finan & Rossi (2013), Mexico: wage offers randomised across 106 recruitment sites, ~350 hires. Higher offers drew more able and *no less* prosocial applicants, raised the fill rate by **35.2%**, and imply a short-run labour-supply elasticity of **2.15**.
- **Career framing beats mission framing on the margin that matters.** Ashraf, Bandiera & Lee (2015), Zambia: job advertisements randomised across 48 districts, 2,400+ applicants. Career-framed ads drew more qualified applicants **without** loss of prosocial motivation; those hired performed better and had lower 18-month attrition.
- **The one genuine selection tradeoff found.** Deserranno (2015), Uganda/BRAC: advertising a high expected pay level drew **30% more** applicants who were *less* experienced and less prosocial — 24 percentage points less likely to donate in a dictator game — with no quality gain, and agents recruited under low-pay framing performed better in year one. Same lever family, opposite sign, because what was randomised was the advertised *expected earnings*, not the wage itself.
- **Screening on integrity is not currently possible.** Hanna & Wang (2014) find India's existing civil-service exams would not filter out dishonest applicants. The authors call better screening design "an important direction for future research" — i.e. a stated gap, not a lever.

**[c] The de jure/de facto gap on selection.** Even inside a merit civil service, Iyer & Mani (2012) show that caste affinity to the sitting Chief Minister predicts plum postings in the Indian IAS as strongly as measured ability. Rigid civil-service rules exist historically to strip hiring and firing discretion from politicians — the US Pendleton Act is the canonical case — and the measured outcome shows the de jure and de facto accountability structures diverging anyway.

## Incentives on the already-employed

**[a — randomised, and the results are not uniform]**

- **Tax collectors (Khan, Khwaja & Olken 2014, Punjab).** Performance pay of roughly 30% of above-benchmark revenue raised revenue growth by **+9.3 log points (a 46% higher growth rate)**; **revenue-only** schemes outperformed multi-dimensional ones (**+12.8 log points, 62%**). **Bribe rates also rose**, hypothesised as taxpayers compensating collectors for foregone incentive pay. **Both halves of this result travel together or the result is misreported.** It is the batch's cleanest instance of an incentive raising the measured objective and creating a rent-extraction channel at the same time — and it is exactly what the source's own task typology predicts (see below).
- **Teachers (Muralidharan & Sundararaman 2011; Muralidharan 2012, Andhra Pradesh, school-level RCT).** Performance pay worth ~3% of salary produced **+0.27 SD** in maths and **+0.17 SD** in language at two years, rising to **+0.54 SD** and **+0.35 SD** at five years, with **positive** spillovers to non-incentivised subjects.
- **The opposite multitasking result on the same lever.** Glewwe, Illias & Kremer (2010), Kenya: teacher incentives raised scores only on the incentivised exam, not on an independent one. The evidence on multitasking is genuinely mixed, not confirmatory — absent in Andhra Pradesh and in the Punjab revenue-only scheme, present in Kenya and arguably in the China anemia-incentive study. This is the empirical record [[multitask-incentive-theory]] was missing.
- **Facility-level health performance pay (Basinga et al. 2011; Gertler & Vermeersch 2012, Rwanda).** A 38% compensation increase produced **+0.53 SD** infant weight-for-age and **+0.25 SD** height-for-age at ages 2–5.
- **Village block grants tied to indicators (Olken, Onishi & Wong 2014, Indonesia).** Health indicators **+0.04 SD** on average — twice as large where the baseline was low, shrinking over time — and malnutrition down **15% (2.6pp)**. No detectable education effect.

**[b] The task typology that predicts which way it goes.** The chapter splits public employees into **agents of government authority** (tax, police, courts — citizen and state interests *opposed*, so stronger incentives risk more bribery and over-enforcement) and **front-line service providers** (teachers, nurses — citizen and state interests *aligned*). This is a clean, source-native axis the wiki did not have, and the Punjab and Andhra Pradesh results fall on opposite sides of it. Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D71.

## Monitoring, and who watches the watchmen

**[a — randomised]**

- **Government audits work directly.** Olken (2007), Indonesia: raising audit probability from 4% to 100% cut unaccounted expenditure by **8 percentage points**, about **30% of baseline**.
- **Auditor payment structure is the binding variable, not auditor existence.** Duflo, Greenstone, Pande & Ryan (2013), Gujarat: third-party environmental auditors paid by the plants they audit systematically under-reported emissions; **randomising assignment and paying a fixed salary from a pooled fund** sharply reduced falsification and produced real pollution reductions. A tested fix for auditor capture — and a direct empirical analogue of Ostrom's monitor-accountability principle ([[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D4).
- **Community oversight works only with deliberation.** Björkman & Svensson (2010), Uganda: community scorecards **plus facilitated meetings** reduced absenteeism and raised utilisation and health outcomes. Björkman, de Walque & Svensson (2014) ran a stripped version — **information disclosure with no meeting — and found no effect.** The mechanism is the deliberation, not the information.
- **Participation structure, not training or money.** Pradhan et al. (2014), Indonesia, 520 schools: linking school committees to the village council raised test scores **+0.17 SD** at two years, linkage plus elections **+0.23 SD**; **training alone had no effect and grants alone were statistically insignificant.**
- **Payment infrastructure.** Muralidharan, Niehaus & Sukhtankar (2014), Andhra Pradesh: biometric Smartcard welfare payments across 158 subdistricts and 19 million people were faster, more predictable and less corrupt, and cost-effective.
- **Discretion is not uniformly a corruption risk.** Duflo et al. (2015) find inspector discretion has positive allocative value — scarce inspections get targeted at the worst polluters.

## Failure modes with experimental evidence

**[a/b]** Multitasking (mixed, above). Rent-seeking as a *response* to incentives (Punjab bribes). **Gaming of monitoring**: nurses broke biometric time-stamp machines and exploited loophole absence categories, decaying an initially positive attendance effect to zero by the end of the study (Banerjee, Glennerster & Duflo 2008). **Monitoring backfire**: anticipated municipal audits in Brazil reduced procurement activity and worsened health outcomes (Lichand, Lopes & Medeiros 2015) — named by the authors as a caution against attacking corruption when the binding constraint is incompetence. **Politicised personnel actions** undermining any incentive design (caste-based postings, Iyer & Mani; politically motivated police transfers, Banerjee et al. 2014). On **motivation crowding**, the authors engage Bénabou & Tirole directly and conclude the evidence inside government settings specifically is thin: "it remains to be seen whether this is a first-order issue in the context of governments" — a scope-narrowing addition to [[motivation-crowding]].

## What the chapter does not establish

- **It is not a source of programme-level or reform-level success rates.** Every result is a specific instrument in a specific agency in a specific country. Nothing here licenses a statement about how often institutional reform works. See [[reorganisation-base-rate]].
- **Non-financial reward channels** — postings and transfers as an incentive device, exploitable only because pay and firing are rigid — are named as important and have no experimental evidence here.
- **Matching the incentive-vs-monitoring mix to task complexity** is flagged by the authors as untested.
- **Technology as a general substitute for discretion** is supported only by scattered single-domain studies (procurement, subsidy cards, fund flow), not as a design principle.
- **Scope: overwhelmingly developing and middle-income countries, 2007–2015** — Mexico, Zambia, Uganda, Indonesia, India, Pakistan, Brazil, Rwanda, China, Ghana, Niger, Afghanistan, Puerto Rico. Rich-country evidence is rare and mostly historical or correlational.
- **Silent on scale and age.** No headcount, hierarchy-depth or organisational-age variable anywhere; the chapter explicitly brackets management practice as out of scope. The only scale-adjacent moderator is country income.
- **Ideological prior**: the randomista / J-PAL-adjacent tradition, technocratic-reformist and pro-state-capacity — government quality is treated as instrumentally central rather than as something to shrink.
