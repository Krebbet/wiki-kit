# Credible Commitment

Capital markets and long-horizon investment require that whoever holds coercive power be *unable* to expropriate — not merely unwilling. North states the claim flatly: capital markets "will simply not evolve where political rulers can arbitrarily seize assets." His argument is that self-restraint does not work — ruler forbearance "was seldom successful for very long in the face of... fiscal crises" — so the only durable solution is structural: shackling the ruler's power, his canonical case being England's Glorious Revolution of 1688 and the resulting parliamentary supremacy over the crown. The same problem recurs one level down. Williamson's selective-intervention argument shows that a firm's owner cannot credibly promise to use fiat only where it creates value, for the same reason: the party with authority also controls the evidence. And Libecap's fisheries data shows the commitment problem is *priced*: US quota holdings, revocable at government discretion, trade at roughly half the lease-to-sale ratio of New Zealand's perpetual rights, implying markets discount for a substantial probability that the right will be eroded.

**Evidence tier note.** The historical material is (b) model plus narrative history, and in an important respect it is weaker than it reads. North's 1991 JEP essay *asserts* the Glorious Revolution argument and offloads the actual demonstration to North & Weingast (1989); the essay presents no new evidence for it. Libecap's restatement of the same argument is **second-hand** — he is recounting North's case comparisons, not retesting them. The ITQ price evidence is (a) empirical and is Libecap's own primary work. The Williamson material is (b), argued by logical demonstration.

## The problem

**[model]** A ruler who can seize assets faces no constraint on doing so when fiscally pressed. Investors know this, so they do not commit capital at long horizons; the ruler therefore has a smaller tax base than a ruler who could commit, but cannot get there from here, because the promise not to seize is not enforceable against the only party with enforcement power. North's two candidate solutions:

1. **Self-restraint ("forebearance")** — which he dismisses on the historical record: "seldom successful for very long in the face of... fiscal crises."
2. **Structural shackling** — altering the rules so the ruler *cannot* act arbitrarily, rather than choosing not to. The Glorious Revolution (1688) is his exemplar: it produced parliamentary supremacy over the crown, and with it a credible constraint on expropriation (citing North & Weingast 1989).

**[model]** North's related second mechanism is *political fragmentation*: early modern Europe's many competing polities made rulers de facto dependent on merchant and constituent cooperation for revenue even where they were nominally sovereign, which forced bargaining — representation in exchange for revenue. He is careful about this one, calling it "only a partial answer," because it fails to explain divergence *within* Europe: Spain's decline against the Dutch and English rise. That admission is worth preserving; the fragmentation story is often repeated without it.

## The contrast case: Spain and Latin America

**[model — asserted, evidence offloaded]** North's negative case runs: the Castilian Cortes was declining in influence precisely as the conquest of the Indies began, so the crown established centralised bureaucratic control with no comparable constituent bargaining. The pattern reproduced in colonial and post-independence Latin America, where wealth-maximising behaviour meant "getting control of, or influence over, the bureaucratic machinery" rather than productive investment, and where post-independence "personalistic relationships are still the key to much of the political and economic exchange." North notes that Latin American states adopted US-inspired constitutions after independence with "radically different" results.

**[wiki synthesis]** The last point is the important one and cuts against a naive reading of the whole framework: *copying the formal rules did not copy the outcome*. On North's own account this is because informal constraints and enforcement characteristics — the other two thirds of his definition of an institution — did not travel with the text. This is the clearest statement in these sources of why institutional transplant fails, and it is also the reason the JEP essay's comparative claims should not be read as demonstrated: the Latin America trajectory claim rests on citation to North & Thomas (1973), not on evidence in the essay. See [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]].

**[second-hand — Libecap summarising North]** Libecap recounts the same body of comparisons (England and the Netherlands versus France and Spain, 17th–18th centuries; North & Weingast 1989 on the Glorious Revolution) and quotes North (1981, 20): "the existence of a state is essential for economic growth; the state, however, is the source of man-made economic decline." Libecap is extending and defending North's framework, not independently assessing it; treat all of this as a second-hand rendering.

## Commitment, measured

**[empirical — Libecap's own primary work]** The most operationally useful material here comes from fisheries quota markets. Under New Zealand's regime an individual transferable quota is a perpetual property right: transferable and usable as collateral. Under the US Magnuson-Stevens Act, an ITQ is explicitly *not* a property right — it is a use right, revocable at government discretion. The measurable consequence (Grainger & Costello 2014): the US lease-to-sale price ratio is about double New Zealand's. A high lease/sale ratio means the market pays comparatively little for the *permanent* claim relative to one year's use — implying, on Libecap's reading, that participants price in roughly a 50% probability that the right will be eroded in future.

**[wiki synthesis]** This is a template, not a fisheries fact. Wherever an institutional entitlement is both leasable and saleable, the ratio of the two prices is a market-generated estimate of the credibility of the commitment behind it. It is the only operationalisation of credible commitment in these four sources that produces a number, and it is registered as such in [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]].

**[empirical — Libecap]** The complementary finding on the supply side: regulators and politicians are described as *deliberately* preferring revocable regulatory instruments over secure transferable rights, because secure rights would "weaken their regulatory discretion" and their ability to reward constituents. On this account weak commitment is not a failure to solve a hard problem; it is a preferred outcome for the party with the power to solve it.

## The same problem inside a firm

**[model — Williamson]** Two appearances in TCE, and they are the same problem in different clothes:

- **Credible commitments as a lever.** Consciously agreed assurance mechanisms — information disclosure and auditing, specialised private dispute-settling bodies, "hostages" (Williamson 1983) — expand the range of transactions sustainable *without* vertical integration. Illustration: the Nevada Power / Northwest Trading 32-year coal contract. Commitment devices are substitutes for ownership.
- **The failure of self-commitment under hierarchy.** Selective intervention fails because the owner cannot credibly promise to exercise fiat only where it creates net value: the owner controls the accounting system, can manipulate transfer prices, depreciation, and claims about which state of the world obtained, and the firm "is its own court of ultimate appeal" because courts refuse to hear intrafirm disputes. See [[governance-structures]].

**[wiki synthesis — flagged as synthesis, not source claim]** North's ruler and Williamson's firm owner have the same structural position: unreviewable authority plus control over the adjudicative and evidentiary apparatus. In both cases the remedy that works is external and structural (parliament; courts; three-way arbitration) and the remedy that fails is a promise. Neither author draws this parallel; the wiki is drawing it. It is a candidate for a genuinely public/private-invariant mechanism and should be treated as a hypothesis to be tested against sources that examine both sides, not as an established finding. Logged as [[open-questions]] Q22.

## Contested: cases where the unconstrained ruler got the investment anyway

**[empirical — added by the `institutions-and-growth` ingest, 2026-08-20]** The claim at the top of this page — that self-restraint does not work and only structural shackling does — meets a case set it does not address, and four sources with incompatible priors report it independently.

- **Wade**: South Korea, Taiwan and Singapore sustained the fastest non-Western catch-up on record under a military dictatorship and two one-party states, with investment/GDP rising from 10% to 36–39% (Korea) and 14% to 40% (Taiwan, 1954–74). "Taiwan was a repressive one-party state until the late 1980s; Korea was a repressive military dictatorship until the late 1980s." Labour was excluded from the "embedded" relationship and repressed. Wade's own discriminating variable is not constraint on the ruler but **performance-conditioned assistance, withdrawn when firms fail to close price and quality gaps with imports** — a discipline running from the state onto capital, the reverse direction from this page's mechanism. See [[developmental-state-and-embedded-autonomy]].
- **Glaeser, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer**: the standard institutional-quality indices score a ruler who *chooses* not to expropriate identically to one who *cannot* — the USSR and Singapore sit among the ten lowest-expropriation-risk countries in 1984; Pinochet's Chile scores the worst possible Polity constraint score of 1 with relatively secure property. Their reading is that this is a measurement failure with substantive content: self-restraint cases exist, are numerous, and include the highest-growth episodes of the period.
- **Rodrik, Subramanian & Trebbi**, from inside the affirmative camp: Chinese entrepreneurs invested heavily with no formal private property under a socialist legal system; Russian investors stayed wary under a formal Western-style property-rights regime. Their own conclusion is that *credible* commitment matters more than nominal legal form — which concedes that formal constraint is not the operative variable.
- **Besley & Persson** supply a model in which the thing suppressing investment is the power-holder's expectation of **losing power**, not the absence of constraint on them — which predicts, read across, that a stable authoritarian regime builds more capacity than an unstable constrained one. See [[endogenous-state-capacity]].

**[wiki synthesis]** This does not refute North. Two things about the page's existing material are untouched: Libecap's lease-to-sale ratio is a direct market-priced measurement that revocability itself is discounted, on an asset class where the price is observable; and North's mechanism — a promise from the party controlling enforcement is not a commitment — is logically sound. What the East Asian cases establish is that **the mechanism is not the only route to the outcome**, and that the constraint variable this literature measures comes apart from investor behaviour in exactly the highest-growth cases.

Two candidate reconciliations, neither stated by any source, both recorded as hypotheses rather than adopted:

1. **Different objects.** North and Libecap measure whether a *specific entitlement* will be honoured; Wade measures whether a *state can direct and discipline an investment programme*. A firm can face high entitlement risk and invest anyway if the same state is supplying capital, demand and protection.
2. **Selectivity as a commitment technology.** Conditional withdrawal only disciplines if the threat is credible, which requires visible prior withdrawals. That is a reputational commitment built from repeated behaviour with a small set of large counterparties — the "self-restraint" North dismissed, but equipped with an enforcement mechanism a fiscally pressed monarch did not have. If this is right, the dispute is about which commitment technologies are available at what scale, not about whether commitment is needed.

Filed OPEN at [[constraint-vs-capacity-as-the-investment-mechanism]]. It adds a third test to the two this page already names below: **sustained investment under an unconstrained executive**, which the shackling mechanism predicts should not happen and which happened at record scale.

## What would settle the historical claim

**[wiki synthesis]** The 1688 argument is, in these sources, a single-case narrative with the demonstration cited elsewhere. What would move it: (i) reading North & Weingast (1989) directly and assessing whether the interest-rate and debt-capacity evidence supports the mechanism; (ii) cases where ruler-constraint was imposed *without* the accompanying commercial and legal changes, to separate the shackling from the bundle it arrived in; (iii) the reverse direction — cases of constraint being removed, and whether capital markets contracted as the mechanism predicts. Until then the credible-commitment mechanism should be labelled as a well-motivated framework claim with one canonical illustration, not as an established empirical regularity.
