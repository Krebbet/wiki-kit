# Colonial Rule and Trust in Traditional Leaders

Gueyap Kounga (2026) uses the British/French colonial partition line running through Cameroon as a within-country geographic discontinuity and finds that **indirect (British) rule produced a durable 22–26pp trust premium for traditional leaders/chiefs relative to direct (French) rule**, with a matching gap in actual citizen contact with chiefs. The design nests three tiers of evidence, explicitly ranked by the author from weakest to strongest: a 31-country OLS benchmark the author himself calls merely descriptive; a 4-border West African discontinuity sample (16,900 respondents); and the Cameroon within-country border, the paper's strongest design because national institutions, and much of the local ethnic/religious composition, are held constant across a boundary drawn by European negotiators "with little regard to conditions on the ground."

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]** — natural-experiment / geographic regression-discontinuity design (following Lee & Schultz 2012 and Dupraz 2019), with the border-discontinuity results robust to 60–120km bandwidth choices. Single-author working paper, August 2026, not yet peer-reviewed — treat as provisional pending publication status, below the wiki's usual bar for a source this central.

## The mechanism, kept explicitly as a bundle

The paper argues three channels, and is careful to say none is isolated separately from the others — the border design identifies the whole bundle, and the mechanism argument rests on pattern-consistency (the gap survives education/religion controls) rather than a decomposition.

1. **Preserved-function channel.** British-administered chiefs kept real governance functions — land allocation, dispute resolution, representation — so repeated beneficial interaction built trust. French *chefs de canton* were stripped to enforcement-only functions.
2. **Coercion-stigma channel.** French-appointed chiefs were tasked with the colonial regime's most resented functions — taxation, forced labor (*prestations*), the *indigénat* — attaching durable stigma to the office itself, transmitted across generations via family and community socialization. The author draws this explicitly parallel to [[slave-trade-and-mistrust]]'s transmission mechanism.
3. **Legal-tradition channel.** Common law gave British-side customary courts formal, persisting recognition; civil law centralized adjudication in state courts, leaving French-side chiefs with no formal adjudicative role.

## Dimensions of institutional variation named

This is the paper's highest-value contribution to the wiki's framework, and none of it is currently registered in [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] — flagged here as candidate material for the register rather than formally numbered this run.

- **Source of authority / selection mechanism.** Custom-and-succession (British-preserved chiefs) vs. administrative appointment for loyalty and education, with French chiefs "hired and fired at will" (quoting Lee & Schultz).
- **Accountability direction.** Mamdani's **"decentralized despotism"** — indirect-rule chiefs backed by colonial force but freed from customary checks, accountable *upward* to the colonial state rather than *downward* to subjects. This is a named failure mode distinct from a simple absence of accountability: it is accountability present but pointed the wrong way. The paper's own interpretive caveat is sharp and worth preserving: high trust on the British side may be measuring exactly this failure mode, not good governance — "deference to chiefs can reflect entrenched and unaccountable authority rather than earned legitimacy" (citing Mamdani and Acemoglu et al. 2014).
- **Functional-portfolio breadth.** Preserved governance functions vs. stripped-down enforcement-only mandate — a direct instance of mandate breadth affecting legitimacy, not just efficiency.
- **Formalization / legal recognition.** Common-law recognition of customary courts as a parallel formal-adjudication channel vs. civil-law centralization of all adjudication in state courts.
- **Discretion.** British chiefs "retained substantial latitude to interpret and apply policy" vs. French chiefs as "petty bureaucrats" with none.
- **Centralization** — named by the paper as the master variable organizing all of the above: comparatively decentralized British administration vs. comparatively centralized French administration.

## A clean falsification case, inside the paper's own evidence

Indirect rule does not always build trust. The **warrant-chief system in Nigeria** — where the British installed authority where no organic customary claim existed — produced no such legitimacy, and provoked the 1929 Aba Women's War. The mechanism requires a *preserved, functioning* customary authority, not merely the indirect-rule label.

## Persistence is channel-specific, not uniform lock-in

The paper cites Dupraz (2019), on the same Cameroon border, showing the British-side *education* advantage was period-specific — it "emerged under colonial rule and narrowed, and partly reversed, after independence." So the same historical treatment, on the same border, produces a **persistent** trust gap and a **non-persistent** education gap. This is direct evidence that colonial-legacy persistence is mechanism-dependent, not an automatic property of "colonial treatment" as such — a second instance, alongside Rajan & Wulf on [[path-dependence-and-increasing-returns]], that persistence is not automatic even from the same shock.

## Scope and what the paper explicitly disclaims

Sub-Saharan Africa only (Portuguese, Belgian, German administration excluded); settler colonies (Kenya, Southern Rhodesia) saw much more direct British rule than the average this paper reports, and French rule in Senegal via the Muslim marabouts was comparatively indirect — the author repeatedly stresses these are average tendencies, not a hard rule. The Cameroon result is further scoped to the inland Grassfields section of the border, which the author considers more credible than the coastal section (uneven German pre-administration weakens the common-baseline assumption there).

The author pre-empts both a pro- and anti-colonial misreading explicitly, in language worth preserving: "nothing in this paper implies that colonization benefited the colonized," and higher trust in traditional leaders "is not treated here as intrinsically good."

## A named measurement gap

The outcome is a single Afrobarometer survey-trust question. The paper does not address [[measuring-trust-glaeser-et-al]]'s finding that survey trust questions predict trustworthy behavior far better than they predict trusting behavior in incentivized games — an unflagged weakness in an otherwise methodologically disciplined paper.
