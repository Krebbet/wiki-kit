# Capital Requirements and Entrepreneurial Entry

A minimum-equity requirement for incorporating a company can restrict entry substantially without functioning as the quality filter its designers assumed — and the reason is not the mechanism textbooks usually name. Norway's 2012 statutory cut to its minimum incorporation-equity requirement (NOK 100,000 → 30,000, roughly $17,000 → $5,000, a 70% reduction) nearly doubled new incorporations with no detectable decline in survival, profitability, productivity, leverage, or founder quality — evidence against the standard "quantity-quality trade-off" rationale for capital requirements, and against liquidity constraints as the binding channel.

## The regulatory mechanism

**[wiki synthesis — framing]** A minimum-capital rule is a distinct entry-regulation instrument from licensing, registration fees, or wait times: it locks a minimum amount of paid-in shareholder equity into a firm's account at the moment of incorporation, as a stated creditor/stakeholder-protection buffer against undercapitalized failure, then imposes no ongoing compliance burden. Norway's requirement dates to its first 1910 limited-liability statute; the 2012 cut was proposed by the Ministry of Justice in September 2011 "without referencing empirical evidence" and legislated by Parliament effective January 1, 2012 — a stated secondary motive was regulatory competition, explicitly against the UK, which has no capital requirement and had already drawn Norwegian-comparable firms via EU freedom-of-establishment rules.

## Identification strategy and evidence

**[empirical]** A regression-discontinuity design around the sharp, dated statutory cutoff, using linked Norwegian administrative registers (business register, tax returns, shareholder register, executive/role register, employment register, national education database), 2004–2018, N = 132,052 incorporation events (47,566 restricted to genuinely new entrepreneurs). The reform is unusually clean for this literature: no other major deregulation coincided with it, and the authors run extensive robustness checks against confounds — a concurrent 2011 audit-requirement reform, tax-motivated relabeling of existing activity, co-founding-dilution artifacts, and industry-composition shifts.

**[empirical]** Entry nearly doubled overall (+75% among individuals not already entrepreneurs). The paper then discriminates between two candidate mechanisms, extending the Evans & Jovanovic (1989) occupational-choice model to add heterogeneous returns to scale alongside the model's existing heterogeneity in ability and outside options:

- **Liquidity constraint** (can't afford the requirement): would predict lower ex-ante liquidity/wealth and younger entrants (less time to self-finance) among post-reform incorporators. **Neither is found** — no shift in ex-ante liquidity, wealth, age, or any ability/outside-option proxy (past income, education, cognitive and non-cognitive high-school measures) between pre- and post-reform entrants.
- **Size constraint** (wanted to start a firm smaller than the mandated minimum scale, independent of liquidity or ability): confirmed via production-function estimation (Ackerberg-Caves-Frazer method) showing lower capital sensitivity and returns-to-scale parameters among post-reform entrants.

The mechanism is therefore genuinely discriminated, not merely asserted: **[model]** the extended occupational-choice model generates the two rival predictions, and **[empirical]** the data reject the liquidity channel and support the size channel.

## What the requirement actually screens

**[empirical]** No detectable effect on survival, creditor debt-forgiveness incidence, profitability, or productivity (value-added margin, revenue-based TFP residual) — the requirement was not functioning as a working quality filter. It does compress entrant scale (post-reform entrants smaller by roughly $100,000–$200,000 in assets, ~$170,000 less revenue at a fixed later date) and concentrates new entry in less capital-intensive sectors. Net aggregate effect is positive: total revenue from post-reform cohorts runs about 40% above pre-reform cohorts, because the +75% entry-quantity effect dominates the per-firm size compression.

**[wiki synthesis]** The requirement is neither pure [[red-tape]] (Bozeman's burden-without-achieving-its-purpose category) nor a working screen — it burdens entry without screening quality, a combination Bozeman's typology does not itself carve out as a distinct case.

## Scope and generalization

**[a — weak link]** Norway, 2004–2018, standard limited-liability incorporations only (not sole proprietorships or partnerships — the paper finds no substitution into those forms). The authors argue the finding should generalize to "developed countries" on the basis of Norway's modest, business-friendly regime and a loose, non-causal cross-check against Sweden; a genuinely different regime — China's 2013 capital-requirement cut, at 200% of GDP per capita versus Norway's 16% — shows a smaller, genuinely liquidity-constrained response (Cui & Wei 2022; Cheng, Ding & Liu 2024), which the authors treat as complementary rather than contradictory. **Bindingness relative to income, not the nominal requirement, is the variable that determines which mechanism binds.**

## Bearing on institutions and reform levers

**[wiki synthesis]** This is non-Anglosphere comparative regulatory-design evidence — a standing coverage gap for this wiki (see [[reference-sources]]) — and one of this wiki's cleanest tier-(i) natural-experiment results for an entry-deregulation lever: a large, precisely measured effect (near-doubling of entry) with no detected downside on any measured outcome. It also supplies a case of a near-century-old rule (1910–2011, unchanged in real terms from 1997) being revisited and cut by ministerial proposal and legislative amendment — a different institutional channel than the US administrative-rulemaking gatekeeping [[regulatory-accretion]] documents, and a rare counter-instance to that page's "creation is gatekept, survival is not" pattern.
