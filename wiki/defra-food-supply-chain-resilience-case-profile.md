# Defra and UK Food-Supply-Chain Resilience (NAO Audit Case Profile)

A case-study page scoring the UK Department for Environment, Food & Rural Affairs (Defra) on food-supply-chain resilience against a National Audit Office value-for-money audit (HC 587, Session 2026-27) — a primary institutional document, not secondary literature. This extends the wiki's case-profile genre (alongside [[fda-case-profile]] and [[meta-case-profile]]) in a new direction: a *policy-domain* audit of a government department's crisis-preparedness capability, rather than a single-agency structural profile built from org charts and financial reports. The unit of analysis is Defra itself, assessed both internally (capability, governance, staffing) and as one node in a wider institutional field it depends on for levers it does not itself hold (Cabinet Office, DESNZ, DfT, DBIST, MHCLG, the Food Standards Agency, 38 local resilience forums, and the private food industry).

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]** throughout for NAO's own audit findings — document review, 56 interviews (Oct 2025–Apr 2026), one site visit, and NAO's own quantified time series. One section below is marked **[wiki synthesis]** where this wiki draws an inference the source itself does not state.

## Formal versus real authority over crisis response

**[empirical]** Defra is the nominal lead UK government department for food-supply-chain security, but the emergency legal powers relevant to an actual food-supply crisis — for example, relaxing competition law to let retailers coordinate supply, as happened during COVID-19 — sit with other departments (held then by BEIS, now by DBIST), not with Defra itself (§22, §3.14). This is a clean formal/real-authority-gap instance: the department publicly accountable for an outcome does not control the statutory tools needed to produce it. NAO frames this as requiring "a comprehensive review of legal powers, with a timetable for legislative change if gaps are found" (rec. f) — the fix is not better coordination within Defra's existing toolkit but an acknowledgment that the toolkit is scattered by design.

## Undefined risk appetite and absent outcome metrics

**[empirical]** Defra's 2024 conclusion that a full emergency food plan was "disproportionate to the level of risk" was explicitly "not informed by an explicit assessment of risk appetite" (§19, §3.5) — the department made a resource-allocation call without a formalised risk-tolerance framework to test it against. NAO's own diagnosis is structural, not personal: absent a stated risk appetite, discretionary decisions of this kind cannot be shown to be rational after the fact, only asserted.

**[empirical]** The department's 2025 strategy, "Towards a Good Food Cycle," names resilience as a priority outcome but "has not yet set out how it will achieve this or measure improvements" (§12) — a strategy with a named goal and no KPI system behind it. NAO's recommendation (rec. b) is to build performance metrics tied to the strategy's stated outcomes. This is the kind of gap [[measurement-validity-framework]] treats as a live register candidate rather than a hypothetical one: an institution can hold a stated priority for over a year with no instrument to tell whether it is being achieved.

## Multi-principal coordination and self-rated confidence

**[empirical]** Delivering food-supply resilience requires levers Defra does not itself hold: Cabinet Office (cross-government CNI coordination), DESNZ and DfT (energy and transport dependencies), DBIST (the competition-law powers noted above), and MHCLG (local government, which runs the local resilience forums). Defra self-rated its confidence in the resulting cross-government coordination as commanding "very little confidence" (§15) — not an external auditor's characterization but the department's own self-assessment survey response. The 2023 "Mighty Oak" exercise independently found siloed departmental responses, and NAO notes Defra "has not meaningfully tested changes it made to address limitations identified by that exercise" (§21) — an assurance gap layered on top of the coordination gap. This is a real-world instance of the collapse mode [[incentives-under-multiple-principals]] treats formally: an agent (Defra) answerable in practice to several principals with divergent levers, none of which it can compel to act, and the agent's own confidence in the arrangement is low by its own admission.

## Public/private resilience split

**[empirical]** Commercial incentive, not government direction, has handled every moderate disruption in the audit period: private firms facing profit pressure switched suppliers and sourced alternates fast enough to maintain food availability through COVID-19, the Ukraine war, and 2025 retailer cyber-attacks, "largely without government intervention" (§8, §1.7). NAO's central critique is not that this failed but that it does not generalize to catastrophic-tail risk, where the same efficiency-optimised, low-stock "just-in-time" model that keeps costs down also "relies on continuous restocking, making it more vulnerable to disruptions" (§1.11). The audit's power asymmetry is sharp: government holds nominal responsibility for food-supply resilience, industry holds the practical capability that has actually delivered resilience so far, and industry carries no formal accountability to government for resilience outcomes. Local resilience forums sit awkwardly inside this split — they execute local response but have no legal power to direct local businesses such as supermarkets, and report that Defra "has not been clear on what it expects of them" (§23), a state-capacity gap that bites hardest at the sub-national tier, paralleling [[state-capacity-and-level-of-government]]'s finding that fixed procedural and expectation costs land disproportionately on lower tiers of government.

## Regulatory intensity as a comparator axis

**[empirical]** Food is "largely unregulated" outside food-safety rules, so Defra has "few levers... to incentivise or compel supply chain businesses to improve resilience" (§16, §2.17) — unlike energy and water, both of which carry statutory duties: mandated multi-year investment plans (water resource management plans) and Ofgem-approved network investment (£70bn, funded through consumer bills, no food-sector analogue). This is a concrete comparator pair for a regulation-intensity/monopoly-vs-open-market axis, with a funding-mechanism corollary: a resilience commitment backed by a ringfenced, mandated funding stream is durable in a way a discretionary departmental budget line is not. Finland's government grain reserve (roughly nine months' consumption, contracts reset every four years, funded by a ringfenced share of an energy-use tax) is the report's other named instance of funding source functioning as a resilience lever, though NAO explicitly flags this and other international comparisons (Figure 9) as unaudited for effectiveness — descriptive, not evaluative.

## Staffing build-out as a capacity data point

**[empirical]** Defra's Food Supply Chain Resilience team headcount rose from 6.5 to 16.5 FTE by May 2026, for a 12-month funded period — a concrete, dated staffing-capacity figure, though only a before/after headcount snapshot with no causal design and no longitudinal data yet on how the added capacity performs. It sits alongside other markers that the department's resilience apparatus is young and still scaling: the Resilience Board was established in 2023, the Group Resilience Strategy dates to 2024, and the Policy Response and Resilience Unit is recent. Cabinet Office's own capability rating for Defra moved from "amber" in 2023 to "Moderate Improvement Needed" in 2026 — improvement, but starting from a low base and still short of adequate by the rater's own scale.

## Cross-country coercive-capacity contrast

**[empirical]** Norway's "Total Defence" model can require private-business participation in an emergency, with government-funded mandated actions, in contrast to the UK, where Defra cannot direct businesses to act during a crisis (§3.16). This is a structurally different concept from [[credible-commitment]]'s usual direction — that source's canonical case is the *state* binding itself so it cannot expropriate investors; here the state's capacity is to *compel* private actors, the reverse polarity of restraint. It is a genuine cross-country variation in the state's coercive reach over private firms, not a contradiction of the credible-commitment mechanism, and worth keeping conceptually distinct rather than merged into it.

## What could not be scored, and why

**[wiki synthesis]** Several things this audit gestures at cannot be scored cleanly from this one document. NAO's own strongest evidence is its quantified time series (food-price inflation vs. CPIH, the self-sufficiency ratio 1956–2025, the FTE figures above, Cabinet Office capability ratings); its weakest evidence is the bulk of the diagnostic claims about engagement quality, playbook adequacy, and departmental understanding, which rest on stakeholder interview impressions ("some stakeholders said," "several stakeholders told us") without independent verification — real signal, but not independently corroborated the way the quantitative figures are. The report also does not test whether large private firms exhibit the same named failure modes it finds in Defra (undefined risk appetite, decaying consultation forums, unassured contingency plans): it audits only the public side, so whether these are general bureaucratic-coordination pathologies or specific to public-sector accountability structures is left genuinely open by the source, not resolved by it. Finally, the report is scoped to England only — food policy is devolved, and the audit explicitly excludes the devolved governments, the Cabinet Office's own cross-government coordination role, and domestic food-production resilience/sustainability, flagging all three for future NAO work rather than covering them here.
