# Regulatory Accretion

Rules accumulate because **creation is gatekept and survival is not**. Miller & Dudley's account of the US federal regulatory corpus identifies an asymmetry in the review architecture: OMB/OIRA requires an agency to justify a rule before it issues, but no body reviews whether an existing rule still earns its place, and no consequence attaches to leaving one unexamined. Three further mechanisms compound it — cognitive biases in regulators that produce crisis-driven rulemaking (imported from Bull), vested-interest lock-in in which compliant incumbents lobby against repeal of rules that have become their competitive moat (imported from Stigler), and rules written without the metrics needed to evaluate them, which are sticky by default regardless of anyone's intent. The claim is stated at the **level of the regulatory regime** — the whole federal corpus since the ICC in 1887, 175,000+ CFR pages — and the source neither makes nor tests any claim about an individual agency's own age. It is a Response article, not a study: it asserts a 130-year time series and does not reproduce one, and its single dataset is a cross-section of 22 rules from a single year.

**Evidence tier note.** (b) **Argumentative law-review Response**, written as a reply to Reeve Bull, built almost entirely on citation to others' work.

- **Strongest content:** Miller's own 2015 study of 22 high-priority rules proposed in 2014 — a real, if small, content analysis of rule text scoring whether rules specify the problem they target and quantifiable directional metrics for their effect. It is **cross-sectional within one year**, comparing independent against executive-branch agencies. It is not a time series and it is not keyed to agency age.
- **Weakest content, and it is the headline:** *that accretion is occurring at all is asserted, not demonstrated here.* The support is citation to external Federal Register page counts (1936–2014) and CFR page breakdowns (1975–2014) whose underlying trend data the article does **not reproduce** — only a single summary figure. They claim a time series and do not produce one. Anyone citing this page for the *fact* of accretion is citing a citation.
- **Absent throughout:** no formal model, no regression, no natural experiment, no original panel on rule counts over time.
- The JPMorgan "moat" material is a single quoted data point from a business journalist, used illustratively.

## Four compounding mechanisms

**[model — Bull's, partially endorsed] 1. Cognitive biases in regulators.** Availability heuristic, loss aversion and endowment effect produce a **"crisis and response"** pattern: agencies regulate after a visible calamity rather than by prospective risk-weighting, and the resulting rules are anchored to the salient event. Miller & Dudley accept this as real and treat it as insufficient — it explains why particular rules arrive, not why none leave.

**[model — the paper's own core addition] 2. Asymmetric ex ante / ex post review.** This is the most original claim in the source and the one the page is organised around. New rules face a gatekeeper: agencies must justify them to OIRA before issuance. Existing rules face nothing. In the source's words, *"the consequence of not conducting ex post analysis is less problematic from the agency's perspective... the regulation will remain on the books."* The asymmetry is **structural and procedural, not psychological** — it operates on an agency with no biases at all. A rule is created against a check and survives without one, so the stock ratchets even where every individual decision is rational.

**[model, imported from Stigler; the underlying rent finding is (a)] 3. Vested-interest lock-in of the rule stock.** Once a rule exists it confers competitive advantage on the firms that have already complied. The cited case: Dimon describes Dodd-Frank, the Volcker rule and higher capital requirements as raising costs industry-wide while widening a *"bigger moat"* against smaller entrants — JPMorgan can absorb the compliance cost and a new bank cannot. Firms with sunk compliance costs gain nothing from repeal and may face fresh competition if it happens, so they lobby to *prevent* repeal harder than anyone lobbies to achieve it; the interest in repeal is diffuse and loses the asymmetric contest (Stigler's collective-action logic, cited explicitly). The source generalises this into a design principle: *"regulations can confer competitive advantages on private parties, benefiting certain technologies or practices or imposing complex requirements that are harder for some firms to manage than others."*

**[wiki synthesis]** This is one of the batch's genuinely well-evidenced structural findings, and it needs stating carefully because it is easy to over-read. What is established is **who benefits**: regulation and procedure create rents that favour organised, concentrated incumbents over diffuse publics, and compliance-cost asymmetry is a rent-creation mechanism distinct from red tape as pure waste ([[red-tape]]). What is *not* established by it is anything about age. The finding is cross-sectional — large incumbent versus small entrant, at one moment — and holds identically for a rule issued last year and one issued in 1935.

**[model] 4. Non-measurable-by-design rules.** Compounding, not independent. Rules are not written with the metrics or the data-collection machinery needed to evaluate them later, so even a motivated retrospective reviewer often cannot establish whether a rule works. Stickiness follows from the drafting, not from anyone's preference for the status quo. This is what Miller's 22-rule study measures.

## Level of analysis — why this page matters to the batch

**[wiki synthesis]** The source mixes levels and does not signpost the mixing, so the levels have to be separated here.

- **The headline accretion claim is system/regime-level.** The unit is the entire US federal regulatory corpus, dated to the creation of the first regulatory agency (ICC, 1887) — roughly 130 years of cumulative rulemaking, with a current stock above 175,000 CFR pages. That is a claim about the age of *the regulatory regime as a whole*.
- **No institution-level ageing claim is made or tested.** The source never asks whether older agencies write more rules, worse rules or stickier rules than younger ones. It does not correlate agency founding year, agency age or years-in-existence against rule count, rule quality or review behaviour. Nothing in it is age-indexed at all.
- **The one dataset is a single-year cross-section**, and the axis it varies is independent-versus-executive status, not age.
- **The agency examples are current-state illustrations, not data points.** EPA's Clean Air Act §812 reviews and CPSC rulemaking appear as instances of the present condition, not as observations in an age-ordered comparison.
- **The available inference, and the discipline required about it.** The mechanism — asymmetric gatekeeping plus vested-interest lock-in — is generic to any rule-issuing body over any span it operates, which *would* predict that a single agency's rule stock accretes with its own operating tenure. The source stops short of stating this and never tests it. **The wiki must not attribute that inference to Miller & Dudley as a finding.**
- **This is rule-stock accumulation, not organisational growth.** No headcount, no budget, no hierarchy layers appear anywhere. Keep it analytically separate from the Parkinson/Niskanen/Garicano growth literature in [[bureaucratic-growth-and-parkinsons-law]]: same shape of curve over calendar time, different object, different mechanism.

## Dimensions named

**[model]** Axes the source names, each usable on a real institution:

- **Independent vs. executive-branch agency status.** Independent regulatory agencies (FCC, SEC, CPSC and the like) are exempt from the executive-order retrospective-review regime binding executive agencies — and they score categorically worse on the one thing the source actually measures.
- **Ex ante vs. ex post gatekeeping symmetry.** Whether an institutional action is checked before it happens versus after it exists. The paper's central structural lever.
- **Designed-in measurability of an output.** Whether a rule specifies (a) the problem it targets and (b) quantifiable, directional metrics for its effect. Treated as a designable *input* property of the rule, not an emergent property of the agency.
- **Concentrated vs. diffuse interest organisation.** Which side of a retrospective-review fight can organise.
- **Strength of the review mandate by instrument.** Statutory (Regulatory Flexibility Act, with its 10-year review requirement for rules with small-entity impact) versus presidential (Executive Orders 12866, 13563, 13579, 13610 — which do not bind independent agencies) versus proposed-but-not-shown-enacted (Smarter Regs Act of 2015, Heitkamp/Lankford, S. 1817).

These are filed on [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]].

## Levers — all asserted, none evaluated

**[model]** State this plainly: **not one of the following is backed by before/after or comparative evidence in the source.** They are recommendations that follow from the diagnosed mechanism.

1. **Design rules for ex post measurability at the time of drafting** — "prospective retrospective review": a stated problem, quantifiable directional metrics, and a data-collection plan built into the rule itself. Supported by citing the proposed Smarter Regs Act and a CPSC commissioner's statement; no evaluation of whether it would raise repeal or revision rates.
2. **Reallocate agency and OMB resources from ex ante to ex post analysis.** Asserted.
3. **Quasi-experimental and pilot rule designs** — impose a rule on some regulated entities and not others to permit causal comparison (citing McGinnis 2013 and Dominici et al. 2014 on air-quality methods). Presented as methodologically sound, with no discussion of whether agencies facing the same asymmetric incentives would adopt it.
4. **Institutionalise retrospective review with real consequences.** The diagnosis is sharp — every prior directive from Carter (1978) through Obama's three executive orders failed specifically because they *"did not change underlying incentives"* — but the fix goes no further than "institutionalise". No enforcement mechanism is proposed.

**Their critique of Bull's remedy is worth keeping** because it generalises. Bull proposes private rulemaking petitions to trigger review. Miller & Dudley object on power grounds: petitioners will use the process to serve private rather than public interest, so shifting review initiation to private parties **relocates the accountability asymmetry rather than fixing it** — the bootleggers-and-Baptists dynamic, citing Smith & Yandle.

## Ideological priors

**[wiki synthesis]** The authors are affiliated with the George Washington University Regulatory Studies Center; Dudley previously headed OIRA under George W. Bush. Three things follow that a reader should hold in view:

- **Accretion is treated as presumptively a problem**, not as a possibly-functional response to genuinely new risks. The possibility that some accumulation reflects real new problems needing new rules is conceded only in passing, via a brief note that regulators "learn" from feedback.
- **Stigler's capture theory is adopted as the settled account** of why early-20th-century price and entry regulation existed — "theory and evidence revealed..." — rather than as one side of a live debate. This wiki carries that debate on [[regulatory-capture]], where Stigler's own evidence base and the Carpenter & Moss and Novak critiques are set against each other. The load-bearing use here is legitimate (the vested-interest mechanism is one of the parts of Stigler that has real support); the framing of it as settled is not.
- **The conclusions are consistent with, not forced by, the evidence marshalled.** Read as a well-cited policy argument rather than as a study.

**Scope.** US federal executive-branch and independent regulatory agencies; principally Carter (1978) through Obama's retrospective-review executive orders (2011–2013), with the regime's origin dated to 1887. The OIRA gatekeeping mechanism at the centre of the argument is a feature of the US executive-order review process and does not transfer to parliamentary systems or other administrative-law traditions without translation. Nothing in the source addresses whether the same create-gatekept/survive-ungated asymmetry operates on the internal rulebooks of private organisations — that remains open ([[open-questions]]).
