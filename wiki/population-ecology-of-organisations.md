# Population Ecology of Organisations

Hannan & Freeman (1977) is the founding statement of organisational ecology: it proposes that organisational structural diversity should be explained substantially by **environmental selection among populations of organisations**, rather than primarily by individual managers adaptively reconfiguring their own organisation. The paper's lasting contribution to this wiki is less any single result than the vocabulary it puts into circulation — **structural inertia**, **organisational form**, **liability of newness** (carrying Stinchcombe's 1965 term into wide use), **niche**, **specialism vs. generalism**, **fine-grained vs. coarse-grained environmental variation** — and a formal apparatus (logistic growth, Lotka-Volterra competition, Levins's fitness-set theory) borrowed wholesale from population biology.

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** — a programmatic, largely mathematical piece. The paper is explicit that systematic empirical testing was left for future work: "we are frustrated at least as much by the lack of empirical information on rates of selection in populations of organizations." It reports two small, directly authored descriptive data points (below) but no formal statistical test.

## Inertia and the case for selection over adaptation

**[model]** The paper enumerates internal sources of inertia (sunk costs in plant, equipment and personnel; limited information flow to decision-makers; internal political constraints, since reorganisation threatens resource redistribution; normative/historical constraints from institutionalised procedure) and external sources (legal/fiscal entry and exit barriers; costly external information acquisition; legitimacy penalties for deviating from expected form; the "collective rationality problem," where a strategy rational for one organisation is not rational if adopted by many). The stronger these pressures, the more appropriate a selection-based account becomes relative to an adaptation-based one — a claim [[structural-inertia-and-age-dependent-mortality]] (the companion 1984 paper) later formalises and re-derives from a different starting point.

## Niche theory: when specialism beats generalism

**[model]** Applying Levins's (1962, 1968) fitness-set theory: under environmental certainty, specialism always dominates. Under uncertainty, the optimal strategy depends on whether environmental states are "close" (convex fitness set) or "far apart" (concave fitness set), and whether variation is **fine-grained** (short relative to organisational lifetime) or **coarse-grained** (long). The counter-intuitive result: with a concave fitness set and fine-grained variation, **specialism** — not generalism — is favoured, because an organisation that keeps readapting to each shift spends its resources on adjustment rather than on acting. Generalism (or, under high uncertainty, polymorphism / federated holding-company structures) is favoured specifically under coarse-grained, concave-fitness-set conditions. Illustrated with Stinchcombe's (1959) craft- vs. bureaucratic-administration contrast in construction, and university "holding company" cross-subsidy structures as a hedge against coarse-grained resource cycles.

## Competition and diversity

**[model]** Extending the two-population Lotka-Volterra model to *M* competitors (via Levin 1970; MacArthur & Levins 1964): the number of distinct resources/constraints in an environment bounds the number of organisational forms that can coexist — the **principle of competitive exclusion** (Gause 1934) applied to organisations. Implication: centralising control (replacing many local constraints with one uniform national one) should reduce organisational diversity; layering new constraints atop existing ones should increase it. Along a size gradient, organisations compete most with similarly-sized others, so entry of large organisations should squeeze *medium*-sized incumbents while potentially improving small organisations' relative position — refining Simon & Bonini's (1958) classical lognormal firm-size distribution.

## The two empirical anchors

**[empirical]**, both directly authored, both simple descriptive counts rather than modelled analyses:

- **Fortune 500 turnover, 1955→1975**: of the 1955 list, only 268 (53.6%) remained by 1975; 122 disappeared via merger, 109 via relative decline off the list, and just 1 (a Cuban-sugar specialist) was outright liquidated. **[wiki synthesis]** This is an early data point for the distinction the wiki should carry into any mortality claim: outright dissolution is rare even among large firms; loss of relative standing and absorption are the dominant modes.
- **US firms since the Revolution**: citing *Nation's Business* (1976), "of the thousands of firms in business in the United States during the Revolution, only 13 survive as autonomous firms and seven as recognizable divisions of firms."

## Liability of newness, in this paper's own words

**[model]** "In part this high failure rate reflects what Stinchcombe (1965) called the liability of newness. Many new organizations attempt to enter niches that have already been filled by organizations that have amassed social, economic, and political resources that make them difficult to dislodge." — a resource/legitimacy-competition account of why new organisations fail, distinct from (and compatible with) the reproducibility/inertia account [[structural-inertia-and-age-dependent-mortality]] derives seven years later, and from the routine/boundary-building account [[liability-of-newness-empirical-hazard-evidence]] measures directly forty years later.

The paper flags, as an explicit gap it does not fill, the need for longitudinal organisational life-cycle data — "we see much value in studies of the organizational life cycle that would inform us as to which aspects of structure get locked in during which phases of the cycle" — naming the transition beyond single-owner/manager control as a conjectured critical period, untested here.
