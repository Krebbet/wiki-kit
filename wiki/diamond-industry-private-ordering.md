# Diamond Industry Private Ordering

Bernstein's account of the New York diamond industry is a sharper test than [[maghribi-traders-reputation-mechanism]] of "private order beats public order," precisely because formal law is not absent here — courts are accessible, contract law is established, litigation is not prohibitively costly — and dealers still overwhelmingly choose private arbitration and reputational enforcement over the courts available to them. The Diamond Dealers Club's private system: a handshake plus the words "*mazel u'broche*" creates a binding contract; every member signs a binding agreement to arbitrate all industry disputes with other members; unpaid arbitration judgments are posted, with photograph, in the clubroom of every one of the 20 member bourses of the World Federation of Diamond Bourses worldwide.

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]**, from DDC bylaws, WFDB rules, dealer interviews, and case counts — not a lab or field experiment, but a documented institutional case with real usage statistics (~150 disputes/year, ~85% settled in mandatory pre-arbitration conciliation, only 30–40/year reaching judgment).

## Why courts are available and still not chosen

**[model]**, Bernstein's own economic argument: expectation damages systematically undercompensate in this market (idiosyncratic lost profit is hard to calculate, courts are reluctant to award it, judgments can take up to three years); and — the argument's sharper half — **litigation itself destroys the industry's secrecy norm**, so even a dealer who wins a meritless suit "will suffer a loss in the value of his reputation" simply for having litigated. Bernstein explicitly rules out the standard transaction-cost explanation (that private contracting is just cheaper to draft): existing customary documentation (weight slips, bills of sale) would make legally-enforceable standard contracts cheap to write, so cost is not the operative variable.

## Secrecy as the enforcement lever, not just a norm

**[empirical]** Arbitration outcomes are kept secret **only as long as judgments are paid promptly** — the threat of public disclosure (posted with photograph, circulated to all 20 WFDB bourses) is the sanction, not the arbitration itself. Expulsion is used sparingly relative to suspension because of an **"end-game problem"**: an expelled member has nothing left to lose and may retaliate through litigation (the Martin Rapaport case — expelled in the early 1980s, triggered an antitrust suit and an FTC investigation, later settled and readmitted). Bernstein's own read of that episode: reputational sanctions bind "only when they capture information the market values" — Rapaport hadn't breached a contract, so the club's ostracism tools didn't fit his case regardless of how disliked his public statements were.

## A case that stresses Helmke & Levitsky's typology

**[wiki synthesis]** This does not fit cleanly into [[informal-institutions-typology]]'s four cells. Formal law here is both *effective* (it functions as designed) and *compatible* (dealers do use it for third-party-affecting transactions — bank loans, consignments) — yet still displaced for the bulk of intra-industry dealing. This is **substitution by preference and efficiency, not by necessity or goal-incompatibility**, which the typology's effective/ineffective × compatible/conflicting cross does not have a clean cell for. Worth citing as a documented limit of the typology rather than forcing the case into "substitutive."

## Enforcement layering, concretely

**[empirical]** Four distinct, stacked enforcement layers, useful as a worked instance of [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D3 (enforcement party): (i) diffuse market reputation, no formal body; (ii) bourse-internal quasi-judicial bodies (Floor Committee: up to 20-day exclusion and $1,000 fines; Board of Arbitrators: binding judgments); (iii) the WFDB's cross-bourse reciprocal enforcement network, extending sanctions internationally; (iv) referral to Jewish rabbinical courts for Orthodox members, "a powerful threat" independent of the trade bodies.
