# Trends in the industry

- **Surveillance pricing bans have achieved legislative escape velocity.** Three states enacted laws in 60 days (Maryland signed April 28, Connecticut signed May 27, NY legislature passed June 4 — Hochul pending). 24 states total are advancing legislation, the California bill is the broadest, and the framing has shifted from "privacy violation" to "algorithmic price discrimination" — a consumer-harm theory that maps directly onto auditable technical claims. The compliance gap that 24 concurrent state-level regimes creates is the substrate for a price-transparency tooling wedge: distinct compliance surfaces, uneven enforcement, and no single federal preemption.

- **Chrome's MV3 endgame is complete and the browser privacy ecosystem just bifurcated.** Chrome 150/151 (June–July 2026) removed the last command-line flags keeping uBlock Origin MV2 alive on Chromium; dynamic filtering is fully dead on that engine. The consequence is structural: 29M+ Chrome users must migrate to Firefox + uBO, accept the weaker uBO Lite on Chromium, or use Brave/Edge. Counter-algorithmic browser defenses are now concentrated on one engine. This is an acquisition window for any counter-power product targeting privacy-motivated users — the migration pressure is real and there is no adequate replacement on Chrome.

- **The open Android sideloading escape hatch is closing simultaneously.** Google's Developer Verification mandate (September 2026) requires all Android app developers — including those distributing entirely outside Play Store — to register with Google, pay a fee, surrender government-issued ID, and disclose signing keys. The 71-org Keep Android Open coalition is the only organized resistance. For cooperative and FOSS app distribution, this is the mobile-OS analogue of MV3: the distribution layer is being brought under Google's gatekeeping. The wiki's bootstrap-strategy assessment ("browser extensions dead post-MV3") now extends to Android sideloading.

- **EU automated consent architecture took a step backward via industry lobbying.** The EU Commission's Digital Omnibus proposal to replace cookie banners with automated browser signals was dropped from the Council position paper on June 18 — Germany and France sided with Google lobbying. noyb's ADPC browser signal remains the main live alternative; GPC remains the US path. The gap between "user should be able to signal consent once, automatically" and "user must interact with dark-pattern banners 500 times a day" is being actively maintained by lobbying pressure. Any consent-automation tool now has to operate in an adversarial regulatory environment, not a permissive one.

- **The DMA is creating mandated data infrastructure at field-resolution.** The Google Search FRAND data-sharing decision (due July 27) specifies at field level — anonymized ranking signals, query text, click/view events — what data must flow to authorized third parties on FRAND terms for 5 years. Combined with the UK CMA's Google search portability API (September 2026), the EU Android interoperability ruling, and the UK DUAA DSAR complaint mechanism, a consistent pattern is emerging: competition regulators are mandating API surfaces that cooperative intermediaries and consumer-side tools can use. These are not privacy-law hooks (GDPR basis) but competition-law hooks — different standing, broader data scope, and structural durability.

- **Cooperative platform launches are maturing structurally.** Subvert.fm (music, 0% fee, 22K members, launched July 2026) demonstrates the dual-entity LCA + PBC structure launching at meaningful scale with major-label buy-in. This is the most structurally sound cooperative platform template seen in the wiki: member vote on fees, optional fan contributions as the funding model, AI music ban as a governance position. The "Mondragon of Music" framing is operationally grounded, not aspirational — it describes a governance architecture already in place at launch.
