# Reform Levers

Every lever surfaced by the `enabling-institutional-change` batch, sorted into three tiers by **what kind of evidence stands behind it**. This is the triage the manifesto (project outcome 3) has to start from, and the sort order is the page's entire point: the batch's evidential spread is unusually wide, and a manifesto that lists these levers in one undifferentiated column would be worse than no manifesto. **The tiers are not degrees of confidence in the same kind of claim. They are different kinds of claim.** A tier-(i) lever has been randomised and measured. A tier-(ii) lever has a mechanism and some cases where it was present when things went well. A tier-(iii) lever is somebody's proposal, in several instances explicitly labelled untested by the person proposing it, and in one instance contradicted by its own author's cases. **Nothing in tier (iii) is advice this wiki is giving.**

**The base rate, first, because it constrains everything below.** Only two of the batch's eight sources supply anything resembling a reform base rate — [[reorganisation-base-rate]] (NAO) and [[political-economy-of-public-sector-reform]] (Bunse & Fritz) — and both point the same way: **measured success substantially overstates genuine change.** The wiki has no basis to generalise a number beyond those two populations. And one specific misuse is worth naming here because it is the tempting one: **treating Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás's design-principles review as reform evidence is a category error.** It measures whether the presence of a principle correlates with an existing commons institution *surviving and managing its resource well*; no case in it installs a principle where one was missing. "Institutions with feature X survive" is not "adding feature X works." See [[polycentric-governance]].

## Tier (i) — Real evidence: randomised or quasi-experimental, with measured effect sizes

**[a — empirical]** Every lever in this tier comes from one source, [[personnel-economics-of-the-state]], because it is the only source in the batch with randomised identification. Every one is a **personnel or monitoring mechanism inside a specific agency**, mostly in developing and middle-income countries, 2007–2015. **They are not reform programmes and none of them has been shown to transform an institution.** They are the levers for which somebody measured what happens when you pull them.

| Lever | Measured effect | Design |
|---|---|---|
| **Raise the recruitment wage** | Better and no less prosocial applicant pool; fill rate **+35.2%**; short-run labour-supply elasticity **2.15** | Wage offers randomised across 106 sites, ~350 hires (Dal Bó, Finan & Rossi 2013, Mexico) |
| **Career-framed rather than mission-framed job ads** | More qualified applicants with no loss of prosocial motivation; better on-the-job performance; lower 18-month attrition | Ad framing randomised across 48 districts, 2,400+ applicants (Ashraf, Bandiera & Lee 2015, Zambia) |
| **Tax-collector performance pay** | Revenue growth **+9.3 log points (46% higher growth rate)**; revenue-only schemes **+12.8 log points (62%)** — **and bribe rates rose**, hypothesised as taxpayers compensating collectors for foregone incentive pay | RCT, ~30% of above-benchmark revenue (Khan, Khwaja & Olken 2014, Punjab) |
| **Teacher performance pay** | **+0.27 SD** maths / **+0.17 SD** language at 2 years, rising to **+0.54 / +0.35 SD** at 5 years, with positive spillovers to non-incentivised subjects | School-level RCT, bonus ~3% of salary (Muralidharan & Sundararaman 2011, Andhra Pradesh) |
| **Randomised, fixed-salary third-party auditors** (instead of auditors paid by the audited party) | Sharply less falsified compliance reporting and real pollution reductions | RCT (Duflo, Greenstone, Pande & Ryan 2013, Gujarat) |
| **Government audits** | Unaccounted expenditure **−8pp, about 30% of baseline**, at 100% vs. 4% audit probability | RCT (Olken 2007, Indonesia) |
| **Community scorecards *with facilitated deliberation*** | Reduced absenteeism, higher utilisation and better health | RCT (Björkman & Svensson 2010, Uganda) — **and the stripped version with information disclosure and no meeting found no effect** (Björkman, de Walque & Svensson 2014). The lever is the deliberation, not the information |
| **Biometric payment infrastructure** | Faster, more predictable, less corrupt welfare payments across 158 subdistricts and 19 million people; cost-effective | RCT (Muralidharan, Niehaus & Sukhtankar 2014, Andhra Pradesh) |

**Three qualifications that must travel with this tier.**

1. **The tax-collector result is not a clean win and must never be quoted as one.** It raised revenue *and* bribery. Reporting only the revenue half misrepresents the source. It is also exactly what the source's own task typology predicts: where citizen and state interests are **opposed** (tax, police, courts), stronger incentives risk more extraction; where they are **aligned** (teachers, nurses), they do not. That axis is registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D71 and is the best available guide to which incentive levers are safe to pull where.
2. **The same lever gives opposite multitasking results in different settings.** Teacher performance pay produced positive spillovers to non-incentivised subjects in Andhra Pradesh and gains *only* on the incentivised exam in Kenya (Glewwe, Illias & Kremer 2010). Whether multitasking bites is an empirical question per setting, not a general law.
3. **Same tier, same source, not in the brief's headline list:** facility-level health performance pay (**+0.53 SD** infant weight-for-age, Rwanda), village block grants tied to indicators (health **+0.04 SD**, malnutrition **−15%**, no education effect, Indonesia), and school-committee elections plus village-council linkage (**+0.17 to +0.23 SD** test scores across 520 schools — while **training alone had no effect and grants alone were insignificant**).

## Tier (ii) — Plausible, case-illustrated only

**[b — mechanism plus illustrative cases; no base rate, no counterfactual, no measured effect]** Each of these has a stated mechanism and a set of cases where it was present alongside a good outcome. None has been compared against a control, and for several the case set contains no failures at all — which means the cases cannot distinguish "the lever worked" from "successes get described in these terms afterwards".

- **Windows of opportunity and policy entrepreneurship** — concentrate effort on implementation during the moments when crisis, growth or an external anchor shifts the political calculus, rather than on lengthy up-front design. Backing: Kingdon's framework plus Brazil, Ghana and Tanzania narratives ([[political-economy-of-public-sector-reform]]), with independent distributional support for the *shape* of the claim from [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-policy-change]] — which also supplies the discipline: agenda access is a **precondition, not a guarantee**, subsystems can and do resist expansion, and venue-shopping sometimes backfires on the shopper.
- **Ex-ante political-economy analysis at project design** — analyse who loses rents before designing the reform. Backing: **the weakest quantitative base in the batch** — two operations with such analysis both currently rated satisfactory (n = 2), plus a 17-operation QAG sample in which responsive operations do better. The authors call it "not hard evidence" themselves, and they are World Bank staff assessing whether the Bank's own practice pays off.
- **Structural ambidexterity** — separately aligned exploit and explore subunits, held together by shared vision, common-fate senior incentives and targeted integration. Backing: roughly five recurring success-firm narratives plus larger-N studies supporting *fragments* (vision, top-team integration), and one N=22/13 comparison of organisational designs. **Private-sector only, success-selected, no base rate for attempts.** See [[ambidexterity-as-a-dynamic-capability]].
- **The experimentalist four-step cycle** — provisional framework goals, delegated local discretion, peer review against units doing it differently, periodic revision. Backing: case description from the paradigm's originators, **with zero failed cases examined** and no effect sizes reproduced for its two strongest empirical claims. The associated **destabilization regime / penalty default** — make the status quo unworkable rather than seizing control — is the most interesting lever in this tier and the authors concede its payoff is uncalculable even in their own telling. See [[experimentalist-governance]].
- **Incremental adjustment as a substitute for disruptive reorganisation** — "incremental adjustments in state administrative reorganizations can act to obviate the need for sweeping reorganizations in the future" (Berkman & Reenock 2004, cited in [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-policy-change]]). **One study, cited not replicated.** It is nonetheless the only lever in the batch that speaks directly to [[reorganisation-base-rate]]'s subject matter, which is why it is worth flagging rather than burying.
- **Reform accountability checklists** — require, before a reorganisation is approved: a business case saying why *this* option, an ex-ante cost and benefit projection with a budget set in advance, and post-hoc tracking against it. Backing: NAO's finding that the *absence* of all three is what makes value for money undemonstrable, plus **one** positive case — the Department of Health's arm's-length-body rationalisation, which had a quantified 1:4 cost-benefit target (£61m spent, tracking toward £255m) and was on course to meet it. Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D72. This is the strongest item in tier (ii) *as a diagnostic*, and it is in tier (ii) rather than (i) because no one has run the counterfactual: the report shows the missing machinery correlating with unaccountability, not that installing it improves outcomes.

## Tier (iii) — Pure prescription

**[b/c — proposals with no supporting outcome evidence in their own source, and in two cases evidence pointing the other way]** These are recorded because they are the levers this literature actually offers, and because a manifesto that quietly promoted them into tier (ii) would be laundering. **This wiki is not recommending them.**

- **PDIA's four principles** — problem-driven rather than solution-selling; an authorising environment for experimentation and positive deviance; tight iterative feedback loops; broad engagement across agents. The authors' own status statement is the reason this sits here: PDIA is "tentative", forward-looking, and had **not been tested at time of writing** — "Our aim beyond this article is to use PDIA methods in particular interventions, and to gather accounts of where they may already have been introduced." Its supporting concepts are imported by resemblance from learning-organisation theory, nutrition research on positive deviance, Lindblom's incrementalism and commons governance. See [[isomorphic-mimicry-and-capability-traps]] — and note that the *diagnosis* on that page is a different matter from this prescription.
- **"Quick wins" building credibility toward broader reform** — **the source's own cases show they do not cascade.** Bunse & Fritz report Ghana and Tanzania as instances where quick wins did not lead on to broader reform, and say so plainly. A lever whose proposing source reports its failure mode should not be presented as advice, and it directly contradicts PDIA's second principle, which recommends small quick steps for the same purpose.
- **Better integrity screening in hiring** — selecting for honesty and motivation at the recruitment stage. The one existing test (Hanna & Wang 2014) finds India's current civil-service exams would **not** filter out dishonest applicants, and Finan, Olken & Pande classify improved screening design as "an important direction for future research", i.e. a research gap, not an available instrument. It is worth noting that this is a tier-(iii) item sitting inside the batch's strongest source, which is exactly why the tiering is done per lever and not per source.
- **Five of NAO's six recommendations** — a single central oversight team with advance warning and power to impose conditions including a mandatory cool-off period and two-year review; a mandatory parliamentary statement quantifying costs and benefits before announcement; separate tracking of planned and actual costs in accounting systems; mandatory reporting to Parliament in the annual report; mandatory lessons-learned sharing within two years. **No natural experiment or comparison group is presented for any of them.** The sixth — state benefits in specific measurable terms — has one illustrative case and is placed in tier (ii) above. Note also the standing discount: NAO is an audit body whose recommendation is more auditable process and a new central team with blocking authority.

## How to use this page

**[c — wiki synthesis]**

- **Do not promote across tiers on the strength of plausibility.** Two levers in tier (ii) — the experimentalist cycle and structural ambidexterity — are supported by case sets that contain **no failures at all**, which is the specific evidential defect that makes a lever look better than it is. Three of the batch's eight sources have confirmed selection on the dependent variable; only Finan, Olken & Pande's RCTs and NAO's near-census audit largely escape it. See [[open-questions]] Q72.
- **Watch the scope conditions, which differ by tier.** Tier (i) is agency-level personnel mechanisms, mostly in low- and middle-income countries. Tier (ii) mixes World Bank lending, EU/US regulation, US federal budgeting and large US firms. Tier (iii) is developing-country state capability and UK central government. **No lever anywhere in this batch has been tested in both a public and a private setting**, and only one source in the batch is private-sector at all.
- **The batch supplies almost nothing on scale and nothing on age.** Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock assert that mimicry effects amplify with size and informality; True, Jones & Baumgartner show punctuation magnitude varying with level of aggregation. Neither is a lever, and no source ties any lever's effectiveness to institutional size or years since founding.
- **The honest summary line for the manifesto**: the levers with real evidence are narrow personnel and monitoring instruments; the levers that address institutional *transformation* are the ones with no evidence; and the two sources that measured transformation both found that the measured success rate overstates it.
