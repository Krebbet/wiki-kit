# Democracy, Epistocracy and Information Specificity

Chirat & Hédoin (Journal of Institutional Economics 22, 2026, e17; peer-reviewed, open access) build a formal baseline model that allocates political issues one by one among three idealised decision procedures: democracy, epistocracy (electorate restricted on competence) and hybrids. The allocation criterion is the minimum of a social-costs function. Its driver is an issue's "information specificity", a function of uncertainty, quantity and technicality of the relevant information. Higher specificity favours epistocracy and lower favours democracy, because disenfranchisement is costly and the cost grows with moral uncertainty. The model is borrowed from Williamson's discriminating alignment.

**Evidence tier note.** (b) throughout: a stylised formal model plus three illustrative applications (nuclear energy, the death penalty, monetary policy), with no data collected or analysed. The authors call the main result "formally trivial", because it follows from assumed cost shapes (convex costs, ordered marginal costs). They also say the model has no empirical operationalisation, treats information as exogenous, and compares idealised regimes only. The authors work in the institutional-economics / polycentricity tradition (Ostrom, Aligica-Tarko, Hédoin) and engage the public-choice-adjacent epistocracy literature (Brennan, Caplan, Somin). They are not themselves epistocrats.

## Findings

- **[model]** Information specificity is the product of three independent, continuous dimensions: uncertainty, quantity of information, technicality. Four costs depend on it: disenfranchisement, bad outcome, information search, deliberation. They are summed into a social-costs function per procedure.
- **[model]** Proposition 1: there are pivotal specificity values, one between democracy and hybrid and one between hybrid and epistocracy. Below the first, democracy is cheapest; above the second, epistocracy is. When specificity sits at a pivot, decision frequency breaks the tie, as transaction frequency does in Williamson. Hybrids win for frequent decisions through lower deliberation costs.
- **[model]** The steelman of epistocracy is stated by the paper itself: set disenfranchisement cost to zero and epistocracy dominates for any nonzero specificity. So the democracy-defending result rests on one assumption, that citizens' perceived illegitimacy is a real cost that rises with moral uncertainty. The paper argues this is a cost in citizens' own eyes, not a claimed natural right to a vote. Epistemic defences of democracy must instead show some other cost rises more slowly under democracy.
- **[model]** Applications are the authors' own scoring, not measurements. Nuclear mix: hybrid. Death penalty: democracy. Monetary policy: epistocracy for implementation given agreed objectives, but choosing objectives has higher moral uncertainty and pulls back toward democracy.
- **[model]** Extensions, argued but not formalised: political culture shifts the disenfranchisement and deliberation costs, so the optimum differs across countries at equal specificity. Epistemic institutions (schools, science, media) shift the bad-outcome cost. A polycentric system could act as a cost-revealing mechanism and make information on procedures a public good, converging on the cost-minimising allocation only under a stable environment and a complete criteria list.
- **[model]** Self-flagged gap: who scores information specificity is left open to avoid infinite regress. They suggest polls, Nominal Group or Delphi methods as ways to test whether the scoring method itself skews the answer toward one regime.

## Reading for the wiki

**[wiki synthesis]** This is the wiki's first page that applies the Williamson transaction-attribute logic to the choice of a political procedure rather than a firm boundary. It follows [[governance-structures]] closely, with specificity in place of asset specificity and decision frequency kept. The shared weakness is also Williamson's: the discriminating variable is hard to measure independently of the outcome, and the paper concedes it. The disenfranchisement cost is a legitimacy term that [[transaction-costs]] lacks, close to Libecap's claim there that the binding costs are political. The polycentric extension is a conjecture that [[polycentric-governance]] could test; the paper offers no case. Candidate dimension for [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]]: who holds decision rights as a function of the knowledge the issue requires, a political counterpart of the knowledge-allocation logic in [[knowledge-hierarchies-and-the-cost-of-scale]]. [[misperceptions-and-support-for-democracy]] is the one empirical source on the citizen-knowledge side: correcting misperceptions moved support only partly, which bears on how large the bad-outcome cost of democracy is. No existing page is contradicted. Note the model deliberately takes a Bergson-Samuelson welfare function as given, so it sets aside the aggregation and capture problems of [[selectorate-theory-and-the-winning-coalition]] and [[iron-law-of-oligarchy]], where the experts' own incentives are a cost the paper does not price in.
