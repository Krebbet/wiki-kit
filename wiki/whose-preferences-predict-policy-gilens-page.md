# Whose Preferences Predict Policy: Gilens & Page

Gilens & Page (2014) is the wiki's central empirical instrument for the "who is this institution actually accountable to" question, applied to the US federal government. Using 1,779 US federal policy proposals (1981–2002) coded for the preferences of average citizens (50th income percentile), economic elites (90th income percentile), and interest groups (a net alignment index), the paper regresses policy adoption on all three simultaneously. **Bivariately, all three predict adoption; in the multivariate model, average citizens' effect collapses to near zero (0.03, n.s.) while economic elites (0.76, p<.001) and interest groups (0.56, p<.001) remain large and significant.**

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]**, and the authors' own framing is narrower than its popular reception. See [[oligarchy-result-methodological-critique]] for a direct, statistically-grounded rebuttal of the mechanism behind the headline coefficient — captured for the ideological/methodological balance this contested question requires. ## What the regression actually tests

**[empirical]** Four rival theories of American politics are given precise, falsifiable predictions (the paper's own Table 1): Majoritarian Electoral Democracy (only citizens matter), Economic-Elite Domination (elites matter, others secondary), Majoritarian Pluralism (interest groups in aggregate represent citizens), Biased Pluralism (business-oriented groups dominate). The design isolates *independent* influence: citizens and elites are correlated at r = .78, so a bivariate "public opinion tracks policy" finding is compatible with citizens winning purely because their preferences happen to align with elites', not because citizen preferences cause the outcome. The multivariate result is read as evidence for Economic-Elite Domination combined with Biased Pluralism (business-group coefficients roughly double mass-group coefficients, .43 vs. .24 in the split model, Table 4; business-group alignment is *negatively* correlated with citizen preferences at −.10).

## The caveats the authors state themselves

**[empirical]** Three qualifications frequently dropped in secondary discourse, present in the paper's own text:

1. **"Our evidence does not indicate that in U.S. policy making the average citizen always loses out... ordinary citizens often win the policies they want, even if they are more or less coincidental beneficiaries rather than causes of the victory."** — the correlation with elite preferences means citizens frequently get what they want without their preferences doing independent causal work.
2. **The word "oligarchy" appears nowhere as the authors' own conclusion** — it originated in press coverage and commentary. The authors' own claim is that "the majority does not rule — at least not in the causal sense of actually determining policy outcomes," a narrower claim than a regime-type label.
3. **R² = .074** for the full model — the authors flag this themselves as possibly reflecting weak proxies, or the existence of explanatory factors outside all four theories: **"With our present data we cannot tell."** The elite proxy (90th percentile) is explicitly named as imperfect — "neither very rich nor very elite" — likely *understating* true elite influence rather than overstating it.

## Status-quo bias, independent of whose preferences count

**[empirical]** Even strong majorities rarely get their way: proposals with narrow pro-change majorities are adopted ~30% of the time; even at 80%-support majorities, adoption reaches only ~43%. Policies with strong support from *both* elites and interest groups are adopted only ~56% of the time. **[wiki synthesis]** This is a separate, structural dampener on accountability — attributable to federalism, separation of powers, bicameralism — that operates on *any* actor's preferences, not only citizens'.

## A transferable accountability-auditing method

**[wiki synthesis]** The design generalises beyond national policy: given (a) a measurable outcome an institution controls, (b) preference or position measures for distinct stakeholder groups, and (c) enough cases to run multivariate regression, the same "whose preferences survive controlling for the others" test could in principle be run on a corporate board, a central bank, or an international body. No source in this wiki has done so. Registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D130** — a method turned into an instrument, in the same spirit as D120/D121, untested outside this one national-government case.
