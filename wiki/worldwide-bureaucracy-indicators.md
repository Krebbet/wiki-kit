# Worldwide Bureaucracy Indicators

The WWBI is the World Bank Bureaucracy Lab's cross-country dataset on public-sector employment, wages and wage bill — 192 indicators over 202 economies in the 2021 methodology report, 302 variables over 144 countries and 1,050+ surveys in the v3.0 codebook — built mostly from harmonised household and labour-force survey microdata plus a small slice of administrative fiscal data. **It is the closest any source in this batch comes to measuring an institution, and it is worth being exact about where it stops.** The panel unit is country-year, cross-tabulated by sector (public/private), industry (healthcare, education, public administration, *core* public administration, public safety, social services) and occupation (five ISCO major groups, plus nine named occupations for the wage-compression variables: senior officials, judges, government economists, hospital doctors, hospital nurses, university teachers, secondary teachers, primary teachers, police officers). **None of these is an identifiable organisation, and there is no agency or ministry identifier variable anywhere in the schema.** An analyst cannot score "the Ministry of Health of Country X"; the finest achievable claim is "public-sector health-industry employees in Country X, [year]" — an aggregate spanning every hospital, clinic and health ministry in the country. Two further facts carry weight here. WWBI positions itself explicitly *against* perception indices — "complementing existing, expert perception-based approaches", naming WGI Government Effectiveness and QoG — yet **most of its own indicator space is household self-report**, not administrative record. And its wage-premium measures show **"no clear relationship" with WGI Government Effectiveness**: two instruments purporting to bear on state capacity that do not agree, reported as an open finding with no explanation offered.

**Evidence tier note.** This page merges two documentation sources on one instrument — the v3.0 codebook (indicator definitions, construction, limitations) and the 2021 methodology report (construction procedure, limitations, worked applications) — because both summaries independently proposed the merge and they describe the same object from complementary angles. Neither is a study. **(a) empirical** for the dataset itself and for the applications chapter's descriptive statistics and Mincerian OLS. **Strongest components**: the wage-bill and ICP compression variables, drawn from government fiscal accounts rather than self-report. **Weakest**: self-reported survey wages, especially for the self-employed, and the EEA industry proxy (below). Every "finding" in the applications chapter is a bivariate or lightly-controlled correlation with **no causal identification strategy**, and the wage-premium regression has **no correction for selection into the public sector** — stated by the report as a deliberate trade-off ("there is no variable in the raw data that may reasonably allow for a more precise instrument… while controlling for selection or endogeneity"), not as an oversight. **(c) wiki synthesis** is marked inline.

## Where exactly it stops

**[empirical]** The industry breakdown runs through ISIC divisions 75/80/85 (rev. 3.1) or 84/85/86–88 (rev. 4.0). The codebook's own note makes the boundary explicit: division 75/84 covers "administration of the school system (regulations, checks, curricula)… but teaching itself does not" — i.e. **the classification tracks what kind of work a person does, not which named institution employs them**.

"Public administration" — the segment closest to core-state bureaucracy and the single largest component of the public workforce at **35% globally** — is treated as one undifferentiated block, and the report states it cannot even be compared to the private sector "since the industry does not exist in the private sector". No decomposition into ministries, agencies or departments is attempted or claimed possible.

**The agency-level facts that appear in the prose are not WWBI outputs.** Cameroon's per-diems rivalling base civil-service wages; Serbia's functional review finding a third of executive-branch positions were internal administrative support; Romania's ageing civil service; DRC retirees kept on payroll — all four are **citations to separate World Bank country diagnostic reports**, used to illustrate why the aggregate numbers matter. They are exactly the kind of finding this wiki wants and none of them comes from this instrument.

**[wiki synthesis]** WWBI is a step down from "the state" as a unit; it is not a step down to "the FDA". Registered `rejected` at [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] **D123**, and the row records that the failure is structural rather than a coverage gap: the schema has no field in which an agency identifier could be recorded.

## Construction: survey self-report against administrative record

**[empirical]** Two provenances, disclosed indicator by indicator, and the split matters more than the totals.

**Household and labour-force survey microdata** supply the great majority — 909 surveys across 135 countries in the 2021 report (550 I2D2, 343 EU-SILC, 12 LAC Equity Lab, 4 LIS); 1,050 surveys across 144 countries in v3.0. In the report's own count, **107 demographic + 39 wage-premium + 9 gender-pay-gap = 155 of 192 indicators** derive from these. Sector of employment is identified by a **direct self-report question** ("what sector do you work in") for every source except EU-SILC.

**Administrative and fiscal-accounting data** supply the rest: the **general government wage bill** (2 indicators) from the IMF Government Compensation and Employment dataset, and **pay-compression and cross-country occupational wage ratios** from the International Comparison Program's 2017 cycle (part of the 35 relative-wage indicators).

**The trade-off, stated by the source.** Administrative data is "potentially a more accurate and detailed measure of employment and wages in the public sector" but does not permit public/private comparison, and "many countries lack administrative and information technology systems to be able to regularly and effectively produce accurate data." Survey data buys public/private comparability at the cost of self-report error.

**[wiki synthesis] The second-order consequence the report names but does not correct for: the countries where administrative record quality is weakest are exactly the countries where WWBI must lean hardest on self-report.** The measurement mode is therefore correlated with the thing being measured. This is Munck & Verkuilen's failure mode 6 — unrecognised measurement error whose driver is a third variable, here state statistical capacity — appearing in a dataset built partly to escape the perception problem.

**Definitional harmonisation.** WWBI adopts the IMF Government Finance Statistics Manual's broad "public sector" (general government plus market-active public corporations), matched to the ILO employment definition. **One named exception: EEA countries 2004–2018** fall back to the narrower "general government", constructed not from a survey question but from a NACE Rev. 2 industry proxy — codes O (public administration/defence/social security) + P (health) + Q (education). The source states the proxy's bias outright: it "may overestimate the size of the public sector" since not everyone in health and education is a government employee. This is a genuine comparability break baked into the dataset, not a footnote caveat.

## Every stated limitation

**[empirical]** Reproduced because a documentation source's value is its limitations list:

- **Self-report vulnerability, and the error is not random.** "Surveys are based on self-reported quantities and thus are vulnerable to systematic errors that may be related to the level and characteristics of employment and income" — the error may correlate with exactly the variables being measured.
- **Missing-data thresholds are conventions.** Surveys with >40% missing employment-status data are dropped entirely (666 surveys excluded in building v2.0); surveys with >40% missing wage, demographic or education data are kept with the affected indicator groups suppressed (218 surveys). The threshold was **raised from 30% to 40%** between v1.0 and v1.1 on the strength of two cited methodology papers — a chosen convention, not a constant.
- **Outlier removal is judged against different reference groups depending on data density.** Countries with ≥4 surveys: within-country >3 SD trimming (833 observations, 0.73% of 114,099). Countries with ≤3 surveys: >20%-of-variance structural-break trimming (204). The sparsest: deviation from their **region's or income group's** mean rather than their own trend (42 + 52). **[wiki synthesis]** A data-poor country's outlier status can therefore be adjudicated against an unrelated regional average.
- **No guaranteed panel continuity.** "It is possible that for a particular country data may come from different surveys over different years" — so year-over-year movement can reflect a survey-source switch.
- **Multi-taxonomy reconciliation by researcher judgment.** Industry and occupation coding is manually reconciled across ISIC 2.0/3.1/4.0, NACE Rev. 2, ISCO-88/08 and an ad hoc NAICS mapping for Mexico, re-coded to "best match" — a human harmonisation layer not separately auditable from the documentation.
- **Questionnaire heterogeneity forces lowest-common-denominator coding.** The team restricts itself to "a core set of variables that are common to most if not all surveys" — a stated ceiling on granularity imposed by cross-country harmonisation.
- **Contract and benefit definitions are explicitly not comparable**: "there is no indication that these terms are based on internationally accepted concepts."
- **Wage-bill comparability.** Cash against accrual accounting differs by income group; benefit and bonus recording, headcount against FTE, permanent against temporary definitions all vary. The sharpest example, and it is a warning about institutional form masquerading as a data problem: **"in France, most healthcare professionals are government employees… in the Netherlands, they are contractors whose compensation is classified under goods and services expenditure instead of compensation of employee."** The same government function scores differently depending on the employment model.
- **Wages exclude bonuses, allowances and in-kind payments**, which the report states are "significant in the public sector" — so wage-premium estimates are a lower bound on the true compensation difference. Not net of tax, because tax data is "almost complete[ly] lack[ing]". Winsorised at the 99.9th percentile.
- **Coverage tracks state capacity.** Europe and Latin America: 44% of countries with post-2015 data, 7.5 years of coverage on average. Middle East and East Asia: **5%** of countries with post-2015 data, 1.5 years. Framed by the report as a gap to close in future iterations; functionally it is a validity threat to cross-region comparison, since the least-covered regions are plausibly those where institutional quality is most in question.

## What it finds, at the granularity it has

**[empirical]** The applications, listed to show what the data can and cannot slice:

| Application | Result |
|---|---|
| Public-private wage premium | **19% globally** (111 countries, 80 positive), range −33% to +100%; falls to **7.3%** against *formal* private-sector workers only (62 of 84 significant) |
| By education | Concave — secondary-educated get the largest premium, tertiary get a **penalty in 38 countries** |
| By occupation | Elementary +9%, clerical +6%; technicians, professionals and managers penalised |
| Gender pay gap | Public sector at 87% of male wage against private sector's 74% — yet penalties persist inside female-dominated public industries: education −14%, healthcare −20%, public administration −21% |
| Wage compression | Public 90th/10th ratio averages **4.9** against private **6.3**; lower dispersion in 70 of 99 countries |
| Within-public relative wages | Senior officials and judges ≈5× clerical; university teachers ≈2× school teachers; doctors ≈2× nurses; police only marginally above clerical |
| Wage bill | **~30% of government expenditure globally**, up to ~50% in low-income countries; fiscally sticky (expands countercyclically, does not contract in growth periods); **uncorrelated with fiscal balance cross-nationally** (Denmark: 17%-of-GDP wage bill, budget surpluses) |
| Public sector size | 16% of total employment, >30% of paid employment, 37% of formal employment; formal share **converged** across regions 2000–2018 (Sub-Saharan Africa 54% → 22%, Latin America 31% → 23%) |

Every one is a national average for a worker type. **[wiki synthesis]** The wage-bill-uncorrelated-with-fiscal-balance result is a genuine negative finding worth keeping — it cuts against a naive "big wage bill means fiscal risk" framing that would otherwise be assumed.

## The disagreement with WGI

**[empirical]** WWBI's wage-premium indicators show **"no clear relationship"** with WGI Government Effectiveness scores. The report states this as an open finding and offers no resolution.

**[wiki synthesis]** Two instruments both presented as bearing on state capacity, both national, and they do not agree. Three readings are available and the source picks none: they measure different constructs (a labour-market price against a perceived-quality score); one or both is invalid; or the relationship is real and non-monotonic. What can be said without choosing is that **"state capacity" is not one measured thing** — the field's two most-used families of public-sector data give uncorrelated answers about the same countries. That is a fact about the register, not about countries, and it sits alongside the [[world-management-survey]] versus [[organizational-economics-of-the-state]] tension already recorded at [[open-questions]] Q90: how much of institutional quality is national, and whether two instruments agree at all, both depend on what is being scored. **Not filed as a conflict**, because neither source makes a precise claim the other contradicts; recorded as a disagreement between instruments.

## Perception, behaviour, and what WWBI's self-positioning is worth

**[empirical]** WWBI's own framing: "The dataset is derived from nationally representative household surveys augmented with administrative data, thereby complementing existing, expert perception-based approaches", and, more directly, "The WWBI complements existing, perception-based measures of public sector institutional quality", naming WGI's Government Effectiveness Index and QoG as "expert-perception measures… ordinally rank[ing] countries". WWBI describes itself as "objective, micro-level data".

**[wiki synthesis] The self-positioning is half-earned, and the distinction matters for D90.** Every core survey-derived variable is a **behavioural self-report of fact** — are you employed, in what sector, what occupation, what wage — not an evaluative judgment about institutional quality, which is a real difference from expert perception. But 155 of 192 indicators are still *report*, by employees and households rather than by outside experts, with the source's own warning that the error is systematic and correlated with income and employment characteristics. Only the wage bill and part of the compression family reach genuine administrative record. **So WWBI clears the "not expert opinion" bar and does not clear a "directly observed institutional record" bar for the bulk of its content.** The residual risk class differs from an expert index's — self-report, recall and misclassification error on real facts, rather than evaluative-scale compression — but "not perception-based" and "documentary" are not the same thing, and D90 should carry that distinction rather than a binary.

## What transfers

**[wiki synthesis]** Nothing at the level of data. Two things at the level of practice, both modest.

- **Disclose provenance per indicator, not per dataset.** WWBI's best feature is that the survey/administrative split is stated indicator by indicator, so a user can tell which of the 192 numbers is a payroll record and which is a household recollection. **QoG does the opposite** and the contrast is instructive: it merges perception-based composites (WGI, V-Dem, CPI, SGI, ICRG) alongside administrative data (WDI, WWBI, IMF GFS) into one file with a uniform documentation format, so a researcher pulling `wbgi_gee` and a WWBI headcount variable into the same regression gets **no signal from the codebook's formatting** that one is expert perception and the other is administrative. (QoG was sampled at roughly **3–5%** of its ~2,000 variable entries; this observation comes from the ~10 source families read in detail.)
- **State the granularity ceiling as a property of the schema.** WWBI's documentation is explicit that there is no field below sector and occupation, which is why the negative finding here is clean rather than a matter of interpretation. A register row that cannot be scored should say so at the level of the missing field, not the missing study.
