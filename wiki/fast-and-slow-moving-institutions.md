# Fast-Moving and Slow-Moving Institutions

Roland independently arrives at essentially North's formal/informal change-speed distinction — already registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D2 — and generalises it into three tiers (political institutions: fast, can change "nearly overnight"; legal systems: intermediate; social norms/culture: slow, incremental, continuous) with a sharper operational criterion than North's one-line assertion: **whether an institution can change by authoritative decision.** "The effectiveness of the legal system and the enforcement of laws depend on their acceptance and legitimacy in society... the system of rewards and punishments is legally codified and can be changed more rapidly than social norms, which can never change by fiat."

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** — a theoretical/framework paper built on historical illustration (English vs. French monarchy, Ottoman vs. feudal France, China vs. USSR transition, settler colonies vs. India/Africa), not data-driven empirical work. No tables, regressions, or datasets.

## What Roland adds to D2 — and what he explicitly does not

**[model]** Two genuine additions: (i) the **fiat-changeability criterion** itself — a sharper, more general operationalisation than North's intuitive assertion, and one that extends naturally to a three-tier rather than binary scheme; (ii) an **interaction mechanism** for what the differential *produces*, via an explicit earthquake analogy: "pressures along fault lines build up continuously but slowly, then suddenly provoke an earthquake that abruptly changes the topography... Slow-moving institutions are the equivalent of these tectonic pressures; fast-moving institutions are the equivalent of the topography." Continuous slow-moving pressure builds inconsistencies with fast-moving institutions until the latter snap to a new configuration — a genuine account of institutional *punctuation*, complementary to but distinct from [[punctuated-equilibrium-and-the-state]]'s Krasner-derived version.

**What Roland does not supply, stated as plainly as he states it himself**: a mechanism for *why* norms/culture change slowly. His own words: **"It is not my purpose here to analyze why social norms or values change slowly, but simply to state the fact."** The one candidate mechanism he gestures at — an analogy to knowledge accumulation, "education... the acquisition of both technology and culture" — is offered as hedged speculation, not derived or tested. **[wiki synthesis] The mechanism gap North's one-line assertion left is still open after Roland — a whole paper on this exact distinction did not close it.** Worth stating explicitly rather than letting a second naming source be mistaken for a resolved question.

## The transplantation hypothesis

**[model]** Imposing fast-moving (formal, political) institutions into a mismatched slow-moving (cultural) context tends to fail — settler colonies succeeded because colonists brought matching culture with them; post-colonial India (caste-system mismatch) and much of Africa (Western-institution transplants, "institutional monocropping," credited to Peter Evans) did not. The China/USSR contrast is used differently than the framing might suggest: Roland attributes the gradual/continuous (China) versus abrupt/discontinuous (USSR) character of transition to **concentration of political power and elite-commitment strategy** (Deng's gradualism vs. Gorbachev's inability to commit to the old guard), not directly to a formal/informal lag story.
