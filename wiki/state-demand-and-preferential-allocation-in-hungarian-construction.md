# State Demand and Preferential Allocation in Hungarian Construction

Bartha (arXiv 2610.03791; Acta Oeconomica 76(3), 350-375, 2026) argues that post-2010 Hungarian construction grew by intervention: expansionary state demand (housing subsidies, a VAT cut, public investment) stacked on preferential allocation to politically connected firms, under inelastic supply, gave extensive growth. Prices rose faster than volume, profits concentrated in connected firms, and productivity stayed flat. This is a **Central/Eastern European, non-Anglosphere** case: one sector, one country (2010-2024), one governing party. **Treat as a descriptive pattern with strong price and volume data and an inferred, unmeasured rent mechanism.** Nothing here is causally identified.

**Evidence tier note.** Tier (a), descriptive: a single-sector case study using official price and output series (KSH, Eurostat), a top-1,000-firm panel (CrefoPort) and a hand-coded list of 29 "politically connected" firms. The author states that the paper does not estimate causal effects. The mechanism is tier (b): a two-channel conceptual scheme with four propositions, where "supported" means consistent with prediction. The capitalisation magnitudes (about two-thirds, about 60%) are **imported from other studies**, not re-estimated here. Rent inference cannot be separated from return: the data do not measure rent, and high ROE is not rent. Status: peer-reviewed (Acta Oeconomica); the arXiv posting is the capture.

## Mechanism

**[model]** Two interventions reinforce each other. A is expansionary demand; B is preferential allocation to national champions (Voszka's "political ownership"). Favouritism raises the political return on further spending, and spending raises the value of being favoured. Two channels follow:
1. **Market-level subsidy capitalisation.** Under inelastic supply, subsidies are absorbed into prices for all buyers and producers, so the intended beneficiaries capture little.
2. **Firm-level rent extraction.** Contracts go to connected firms at inflated prices, and rents weaken the pressure to raise efficiency.

Propositions: P1 prices outpace volume; P2 profitability decouples from competitiveness; P3 productivity stagnates; P4 gains are distributed unevenly. Supply inelasticity is treated as partly endogenous: temporary, politically uncertain stimulus deters capacity investment by unconnected firms, while connected firms with secured state demand rationally expand.

## Evidence

**[empirical, descriptive; price and volume data]**
- Real construction output is up about 62% over 2010-2024 (Romania about +67%, Slovakia about -40%).
- Construction producer prices exceed the GDP deflator from 2017; in 2022 the gap is 10.5pp (24.5% vs 14.0%).
- Productivity per hour in 2024 is back near its 2000 level, against Romania's roughly +50%. The two sharpest post-2010 productivity drops coincide with the two intense stimulus periods (2015-16, 2020-22). The Romania contrast matters: it shows the boom alone is not anomalous.
- Scale: sector gross value added share 3.9% (2012) to 6.4% (2022); employment 247k to 391k; construction output 10.1% of GDP in 2022. Flows: at least HUF 4,500bn in household housing subsidies and loans since 2015; public investment over HUF 30,000bn, 2010-2025 (a government statement).

**[empirical, descriptive; ownership and profit, on a lower-bound coding]**
- Connected firms in the top 10 by turnover: 0 in 2008, 6 in 2024 (three tied to one owner circle). About two-thirds of the 29 coded firms trace to four individuals.
- Connected firms' share of sector profits: 15.9% (2008) to 56.1% (2024). ROE in 2024: 41.8% vs 17.3% for others. Export intensity is at or below about 1%. The 56.1% figure rests partly on a self-citation (Bartha 2026).
- Tangible share of connected firms' fixed assets fell from 55% to 26% while their fixed assets rose more than 20x: expansion by acquisition and financial holdings, not new capacity.

**[empirical, imported identified results from other authors]**
- About two-thirds of the CSOK grant absorbed into Budapest prices (Banai et al. 2019); about 60% of the 2016 VAT cut capitalised into net prices, 40% passed to buyers (Fellner et al. 2019).
- Governing-circle-linked firms' share of procurement value about 1% rising to about 18% (Toth & Hajdu 2022).
- Czech bunching/RDD evidence: tighter procurement rules lower prices except for connected firms (Celis Galvez et al. 2025). This supports the claim that favouritism neutralises rules.

## Caveats

1. **Rent is inferred, not measured.** The mechanism is "inferred from the conjunction" of patterns. A 56% profit share and 41.8% ROE are equally compatible with rent and with superior performance; export intensity near zero is weak evidence because it is low for everyone (never above 6%).
2. **Lower-bound coding, retroactive status.** Connection is coded from two published classifications plus investigative reporting, and post-2010 status is applied to all years, which biases the 2008 comparison.
3. **Sample composition.** Top-1,000 by revenue with changing membership; the unconnected fixed-asset series is erratic (index 84 in 2024 vs 335 in 2020). These firms are about 4% of sector employment but 12-35% of gross value added.
4. **Competing explanations dismissed by argument, not test.** Post-crisis catch-up, global cost shocks, labour shortage and the EU-funds cycle are not ruled out empirically.
5. **Imported estimates do not transfer automatically.** The capitalisation shares come from specific episodes (Budapest housing, one VAT change).
6. **Measurement masking.** Firms operate under many legal identities, so entity-level concentration measures understate ownership concentration. Compare [[measurement-validity-framework]].
7. **Not tested on regime change.** The author's forward test, that a change of government should register in prices, ownership and procurement, is stated but not run.
8. **One-sided sourcing.** The connection classifications (Toth & Hajdu, Laki) and the reporting come from critics of the regime; no source defending a national-champion rationale is engaged.
9. **Scope.** Single country and sector; the claim of generality (inelastic supply plus discretionary allocation) is argued, not tested.

## Tension with the developmental-state cases

State-chosen national champions that receive directed capital are the structure that [[developmental-state-and-embedded-autonomy]] and [[koreas-economic-planning-board]] credit with fast catch-up. Here the same structure yields extensive growth and flat productivity. This is a tension, not a refutation: Bartha does not engage Wade, and the Hungarian sector is construction, a largely non-tradable activity, not a manufacturing export sector. **[wiki synthesis]** The most plausible reconciliation is the missing performance conditionality: Korean support was tied to export discipline and withdrawn on failure, while Hungarian connected firms have export intensity near 1% and no evident exit penalty. If so, preferential allocation is not the discriminating variable; discipline on the favoured is. This is untested here, and it also leaves open that the Hungarian outcome reflects a sector-specific feature (inelastic, domestic, procurement-dependent demand) and not the allocation regime. The wiki has no source defending the developmental rationale for domestic-ownership preference in this setting.

## Levers proposed, and their evidence

The paper prescribes, without testing: (1) condition demand-side programmes on the target sector's supply capacity; (2) supply-side complements (workforce development, long-term programming); (3) credible multi-year commitments in place of renewable short-term schemes; (4) competitive-tendering safeguards and transparency; (5) EU conditionality on competitive allocation rather than funding volume. **[wiki synthesis]** The evidence base is indirect: the imported capitalisation studies bear on (1), and the Czech RDD bears on (4); (2), (3) and (5) rest on the mechanism alone. In the [[reform-levers]] scheme these are weak-tier claims, not results of this paper. Lever (3) is a [[credible-commitment]] argument about policy design.

## Public/private and power

**[wiki synthesis]** The case is not a public/private test. "Political ownership" sits at the state/private boundary, which blurs the dichotomy, as in the Chinese SOE case ([[party-organisation-control-of-chinese-soes]]). Power is central: decision rights over contracts, subsidy design and tax rates sit with the ruling party and build a dependent business class; formal procurement rules persist while allocation follows connection ([[de-jure-vs-de-facto-power-and-captured-democracy]]). The direction is state-to-firm patronage, not firm-captures-regulator as in [[regulatory-capture]]. Age enters only as regime duration (15 years) and the entrenching effect of rent-financed acquisition. Candidate axes for [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]]: procurement competition and discretion; temporal design of intervention (temporary vs credible multi-year); connected-owner share of sector profits.
