# Polycentric Governance

Ostrom's case is that neither "the market" nor "the state" is the general-purpose institution for governing common-pool resources and public goods, and that the dichotomy itself is a classification error. She replaces the two-goods / two-organisational-forms model with a four-way goods typology (subtractability of use × difficulty of exclusion, both treated as continuous) crossed with a range of ownership forms — government, private, communal, or unowned — that is **orthogonal** to goods type: "A common-pool resource can be owned and managed as government property, private property, community property, or owned by no one." What predicts performance is not the ownership label but the rules-in-use, and above all whether monitoring is done by people accountable to the users. Her eight design principles are the enumerable structural features that distinguish long-surviving institutions from failed ones; the primary test of them — Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás's 2010 review of **91 studies and 77 coded cases** — finds every principle positively correlated with successful long-term management, at a mean study-level support of **3.73 on a 1–5 scale**, with nesting the weakest. **The "100+ studies / two-thirds confirm" figure this page previously carried came secondhand from Ostrom's Nobel lecture and appears nowhere in the primary source; it is superseded — see "Empirical testing of the design principles" below.** The scope condition matters and Ostrom states it: this is a body of evidence about local governance of *small-to-medium-scale* common-pool resources.

**Evidence tier note.** This is the only source in the foundations set with a substantial empirical layer, and its internal hierarchy is worth keeping. **Strongest (a, causal):** controlled lab experiments varying one factor at a time against a Nash-equilibrium benchmark, replicated by field experiments with real resource users in Colombia (Cardenas 2000; Cardenas, Stranlund & Willis 2000) and Brazil (Fehr & Leibbrandt 2008). **Strong but correlational (a):** meta-analyses of commonly coded case studies — 47 (later 229) Nepal irrigation systems, 44 fishery subgroups, 152–178 forests — screened from much larger source pools, with Ostrom herself flagging "minimal overlap of variables identified by case study authors" as a data-quality problem. **Weakest (b, closer to framework):** the design principles themselves, derived inductively from her own reading of surviving versus failed institutions and only later validated externally. **Qualitative case work (b/a):** the California water industry and US metropolitan police studies, foundational to the concept but not statistically tested.

## What replaces the dichotomy

**[model]** Three components, and they are separable:

1. **A four-way goods typology.** Samuelson's two-way split (private / public) is expanded by crossing *subtractability of use* (rivalry) with *difficulty of exclusion*, both continuous rather than binary, giving private, public, toll (club), and common-pool goods. The point of the reclassification: CPRs share subtractability with private goods and non-excludability with public goods, so the market/state dichotomy necessarily misclassifies them and prescribes the wrong instrument.
2. **Polycentricity.** Many formally independent decision centres, which may take each other into account, contract with each other, or resolve conflict through central mechanisms — as against a single hierarchical authority.
3. **A common structural language (IAD).** Rules-in-use and action situations, applied identically to markets, firms, families, community organisations, legislatures and agencies. See [[what-is-an-institution]].

## The evidence against ownership form

**[empirical]** Three findings, all pointing the same way:

- **Protected forests.** Officially protected government forests showed **no statistically significant difference** in forest density from non-protected forests (Hayes 2006; 76 legally protected vs. 87 other forests). Legal designation alone does not predict outcome.
- **Nepal irrigation.** Only 40% of 12 government-managed systems achieved high performance, against 72% of 25 farmer-managed systems. Farmer-managed systems also outperformed on three measured production metrics: physical system condition, water availability at the tail end, and rice yield (Lam 1998; later confirmed on a 229-system database).
- **Metropolitan policing.** The most efficient producers supplied more output per input in metropolitan areas with *many* producers than the efficient producers in areas with fewer — the opposite of what consolidation-based reform theory predicted.

Ostrom's own summary of what does the work: "it is not the general type of forest governance that is crucial in explaining forest conditions; rather, it is how a particular governance arrangement fits the local ecology, how specific rules are developed and adapted over time, and whether users consider the system to be legitimate and equitable."

**[wiki synthesis]** This is the strongest direct evidence in the foundations set bearing on [[open-questions]] Q3 (public/private invariance). It is negative evidence — ownership category does not predict outcome — rather than positive identification of what does, and it is drawn from natural-resource commons, not from agencies or firms. It does not license the general claim that sector is irrelevant everywhere.

## Mechanisms

**[empirical — lab and field]**

- **Face-to-face communication ("cheap talk").** Lets users build trust and detect defection through aggregate feedback, sustaining cooperation well above the Nash equilibrium *despite* having no enforcement power: "no external 'third party' ensured that the promise was fulfilled."
- **Self-designed sanctioning.** Users who design their own sanctioning system achieve about **90% of optimal** returns — the highest of any tested CPR lab condition. Contrast: subjects *given* a sanctioning option they did not choose "substantially reduced net returns due to the overuse of costly sanctions," some used as revenge rather than enforcement.
- **Local monitoring.** Repeatedly the single strongest correlate of good resource condition (forest density, irrigation performance). The mechanism is that monitoring makes rule-breaking observable, which is a precondition for any sanction or reputational effect to operate at all.
- **Congruence / fit.** Rules matched to local ecological and social conditions are causally necessary; one-size-fits-all prescriptions (blanket ITQs, blanket protected-area designation, blanket privatisation or nationalisation) fail because they ignore the fit requirement.
- **Trust as the mediating variable.** Ostrom's stated model: situational structure (communication, reputation, marginal per capita return, exit, time horizon, sanctioning) → trust that others will reciprocate → cooperation → outcomes, with feedback. She is explicit that a general theory of the individual is not yet established: it is "not yet possible to point to a single theory of human behavior that has been... tested."

**[empirical] Crowding out.** Externally imposed rules can reduce voluntary cooperation. In the Cardenas, Stranlund & Willis (2000) field experiment an imposed rule *increased* withdrawal relative to the communication-only condition (see also Frey & Oberholzer-Gee 1997; Reeson & Tisdell 2008). This is a direct hazard for top-down institutional design: the intervention can destroy the informal enforcement it was meant to replace.

## The eight design principles

**[framework, inductively derived; empirically tested by Cox et al. 2010 — the numbers are in the next section]** Recorded in the updated Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás formulation as quoted in the source:

- **1A. User Boundaries** — clear, locally understood boundaries between legitimate users and nonusers.
- **1B. Resource Boundaries** — clear boundaries separating the CPR from the larger social-ecological system.
- **2A. Congruence with Local Conditions** — appropriation and provision rules congruent with local social and environmental conditions.
- **2B. Appropriation and Provision** — appropriation rules congruent with provision rules; cost distribution proportional to benefit distribution.
- **3. Collective-Choice Arrangements** — most individuals affected by a resource regime are authorised to participate in making and modifying its rules.
- **4A. Monitoring Users** — monitors who are accountable to, or are, the users, monitoring appropriation and provision levels.
- **4B. Monitoring the Resource** — monitors who are accountable to, or are, the users, monitoring the condition of the resource.
- **5. Graduated Sanctions** — sanctions start low and escalate with repeated violation.
- **6. Conflict-Resolution Mechanisms** — rapid, low-cost, local arenas for resolving conflict.
- **7. Minimal Recognition of Rights** — the government recognises the users' right to make their own rules.
- **8. Nested Enterprises** — governance activities organised in multiple nested layers when the CPR is connected to a larger system.

**Evidence base:** the review Ostrom cites for these principles is now captured as a primary source and is reported in full in the next section. Ostrom's own gloss on the non-confirming cases is worth keeping: some of the non-confirmation is attributed to analysts applying the principles too rigidly, which is itself an admission that the principles are not crisply operationalised. Cox et al. make the same point in their own words — the principles warrant "a probabilistic, rather than deterministic, interpretation."

**[wiki synthesis]** Principles 3, 4A/4B and 7 are the ones that generalise beyond resource commons, because they are statements about decision rights, the direction of monitor accountability, and the surrounding state's recognition of subsidiary authority — none of which is specific to a fishery. Principles 1A/1B and 2A/2B are more tightly bound to resources with a physical boundary and a measurable yield. That split is a hypothesis about transferability, logged as [[open-questions]] Q19.

## Empirical testing of the design principles: Cox et al. (2010)

**Correction, 2026-08-19 — the earlier secondhand figure on this page is superseded.** This page previously stated, on the authority of Ostrom's Nobel lecture, that "Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás (2009) reviewed 100+ studies" and that "two-thirds... confirm." The published review is now captured directly and reports neither number. It coded **91 studies and 77 cases**; its headline statistic is a **mean study-level evaluation of 3.73 on a 1–5 scale** ("slightly below moderately supportive"); its per-principle results are reported as **supportive-to-unsupportive case ratios with Fisher's exact tests**, not as a fraction of studies confirming. No "two-thirds" or "100+" figure appears anywhere in it. The likely explanation is stated in the paper's own acknowledgments: the 2010 article revises an earlier report titled *Design Principles Are not Blue Prints, but Are They Robust?: a Meta-analysis of 112 Studies* — a 2009 working draft covering **112** studies, narrowed to 91 by publication. That accounts for "100+" and for the "2009" date; "two-thirds" corresponds to no reported statistic in either version and should be treated as Ostrom's own paraphrase. **Prefer the primary numbers below. The secondhand figure is left visible here rather than deleted, because the failure mode — a Nobel lecture's paraphrase of a co-located colleague's working draft hardening into a wiki fact — is the kind of thing this wiki should be able to catch twice.**

**Evidence tier: (a) empirical, but a correlational synthesis of secondary literature, not primary data.** Two nested units, both at the class-of-institutions level: 91 **studies** (journal articles, books, dissertations, all post-1990) coded 1–5 for overall support, and 77 **cases** within them coded per principle for presence of satisfying conditions ("evidence") and for successful long-term management ("success"), both binary. Association tested by Fisher's exact test on the 2×2 evidence-by-success table for each principle separately, with phi as effect size. The authors state plainly that mechanism is out of scope: "a full account of the theoretical mechanisms... is beyond the scope of our analysis." So this is a correlational meta-finding wrapped around an inherited, untested mechanism (uncertainty reduction → trust and reciprocity → sustained collective action → resource condition).

**Principle-by-principle result.** Every principle had at least twice as many supportive as unsupportive cases; every association was positive; Fisher's exact was significant at the 5% level for principles 1–7 and only at the **10%** level for principle 8. The authors' own bands are ratio > 10 "very strongly supported", 5–10 "strongly", 2 to < 5 "moderately well".

| Principle | Support level (Cox et al.'s own band) |
|---|---|
| 1A User boundaries | strong |
| 1B Resource boundaries | moderate |
| 2A Congruence with local conditions | **very strong** |
| 2B Appropriation/provision proportionality | **very strong** |
| 3 Collective-choice arrangements | moderate |
| 4A Monitors present | moderate |
| 4B **Monitors accountable to the users** | **very strong** |
| 5 Graduated sanctions | moderate |
| 6 Conflict-resolution mechanisms | moderate |
| 7 Minimal recognition of rights | moderate |
| 8 Nested enterprises | moderate; **the only principle not significant at 5%** |

The three very-strongly-supported components are congruence with local conditions, proportionality between what a user contributes and what they take, and **the direction of monitor accountability** — not the mere existence of monitoring, which is only moderate. That distinction is exactly the one already registered as [[dimensions-of-institutional-variation]] D4, and this is its strongest empirical backing in the wiki.

**Other results from the same analysis.**

- **The critiques are theoretical, not empirical.** Abstract/theoretical studies had a median evaluation of 2.0 ("moderately unsupportive") and differ significantly from the three empirical study types taken as one group (Mann-Whitney, P = 0.002); once abstract studies are excluded, detailed case, statistical and synthesis studies do not differ from each other (Kruskal-Wallis, P = 0.122). The authors' headline — "the most trenchant critiques were abstract, rather than empirical" — rests entirely on this split.
- **No sector effect** across forest, fishery, irrigation, pasture and mixed systems (P = 0.747), and **no drift by publication year**. Overt evaluations of the principles were not significantly less supportive than implicit ones (3.60 vs. 3.97, P = 0.343).
- **Coding safeguards, stated by the authors.** The impressionistic evaluation variable was double-coded with disagreements resolved to the *lower* (less supportive) value, and where the coders' reading conflicted with the study author's own stated conclusion they deferred to the author. Both cut against false confirmation.
- **The reformulation.** Their own actionable output: split principles 1 and 2 into their two components each, add congruence between user and resource boundaries, and split principle 4 into *social* monitoring (users monitor each other) and *environmental* monitoring (users monitor the resource) — a different cut from the presence/accountability split used in the analysis.

**Caveats the authors state themselves, and this page keeps.**

- **Incompleteness is the strongest surviving empirical critique**, in their own judgement. User-group size, heterogeneity within and between user groups, government regime type and market integration all matter in the reviewed cases and are not part of the principles.
- **Market integration erodes self-governance.** Bardhan (2000, 48 south Indian irrigation communities): cooperative behaviour "significantly related negatively to... urban or market connections"; Tucker et al. and Cinner & McClanahan report rising inequality, declining forest condition and overfishing of high-value species near markets. Mechanism offered: integration reduces local dependence on the resource, weakening the incentive to cooperate. This is real evidence for the economic-production-versus-institutional-robustness question and is not the review's own primary result — it surfaces from the discussion of critiques.
- **Scale generalisability is unresolved and they refuse to resolve it.** "We remain uncertain as to whether the principles may apply to systems at a variety of scales." They did not formally analyse large, non-community systems, and offer only that it is "plausible" that proportionality, conflict resolution, nesting and participatory collective choice transfer upward — explicitly *not* a claim that communities can resolve large-scale problems alone.
- **Reverse causality is live.** Agrawal & Chhatre (2006, 95 Indian forest systems) found guards *negatively* correlated with forest condition, and read it as villages hiring guards *because* forests are degrading. A cross-sectional presence-of-mechanism correlation does not establish that installing the mechanism helps.
- **Ideological prior.** The authors sit at Ostrom's own institutional home (the Indiana Workshop) and thank Ostrom for feedback on the manuscript. Strong prior toward vindicating the inherited framework; the coding safeguards above are the stated mitigants and do not fully dispose of it.

**[wiki synthesis] What this review is not.** It measures whether the presence of a design principle correlates with an existing commons institution having **survived and managed its resource well**. It is not a study of reform: no case is an attempt to *install* a principle in an institution that lacked it, and no outcome is measured before and after such an attempt. **Reading Cox as evidence about reform success rates is a category error** — the population is surviving self-governance regimes, not reform attempts. See [[reorganisation-base-rate]], where the wiki's actual reform base-rate position is recorded, and [[reform-levers]], where the levers are triaged by evidence type.

## Nesting, scale and power

**[empirical]** Scale effects in this source are **non-monotonic and interact with governance**, which is the useful finding:

- Technical efficiency for *direct* metropolitan services rises with the number of autonomous producers; for *indirect / support* services (radio communications, crime labs) it is enhanced by a *small* number of producers. Scale effects invert direction depending on what is produced.
- Small-to-medium cities are named as more effective than large cities at monitoring citizen performance and costs.
- Regenerating forests tend to be small-to-medium with low subsistence dependence, low commercial value, high local enforcement and strong collective action (Chhatre & Agrawal 2008) — but *larger* forests are more effective on joint carbon-plus-livelihood outcomes when paired with high local rule-making autonomy (Chhatre & Agrawal 2009).
- User-group size is one of roughly ten social-ecological-system variables affecting the likelihood of self-organisation, alongside leadership, users' knowledge of the system, and users' dependence on it.

**[empirical / framework] Power.** Two claims to keep:

1. **Nominal decentralisation is not decentralisation.** Top-down decentralisation that leaves local officials and users in the dark caused deforestation of previously stable forests (Banana et al. 2007). Formal transfer of authority without real transfer of decision rights and accountability fails — a direct nominal-versus-actual accountability finding.
2. **Local authority is fragile without state recognition.** Design principle 7 is explicitly about the relationship between a self-governing body and the power structure above it. This is the clearest statement in these sources of how a surrounding power structure sets the incentives of a subsidiary institution, and it is a commitment problem — see [[credible-commitment]].

Ostrom's underlying normative claim is a redistribution-of-decision-rights claim: authority to make rules should sit with those affected, layered rather than centralised. She attacks the mainstream framing — that an external, benevolent, single sovereign restructures society — as denying ordinary people decision rights over their own collective problems.

## Scope conditions and the boundary with Libecap

**[wiki synthesis]** Ostrom's evidence base is explicitly *local governance of small-to-medium-scale common-pool resources*: groundwater basins, inshore fisheries, irrigation systems, forests, police services, across the US, Nepal, and the IFRI multi-country forest network, with lab groups of eight. She is careful not to oversell — "many groups have struggled and failed," and no general theory of the individual is available. Cox et al.'s review adds the same caution in its own terms: a probabilistic rather than deterministic reading of the principles, and explicit uncertainty about whether they apply at other scales.

Libecap's cases are drawn from the *opposite* end of the same axis: large, valuable, heterogeneous, poorly-boundable resources (oil reservoirs, large fisheries, basin-scale groundwater) with many heterogeneous claimants, where he documents self-organisation failing through holdout and heterogeneity, and the political fallback failing through capture and discretion-preservation. The two bodies of work reach opposite conclusions about whether user self-organisation solves commons problems. Whether that is a contradiction or a scope condition is an open question, filed at [[self-organisation-vs-property-rights-in-commons]].
