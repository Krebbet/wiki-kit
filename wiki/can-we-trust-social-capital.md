# Can We Trust Social Capital?

Sobel's review essay operates at three separable levels the wiki should keep distinct rather than citing as one undifferentiated "social capital is contested" caveat: whether "social capital" coheres as a form of *capital*; whether Putnam's specific causal claims in *Bowling Alone* survive scrutiny; and whether cross-national/cross-cultural trust comparisons are identified at all. He is explicit that he is not a wholesale rejectionist — he declines to follow Manski's recommendation to abandon the term, because "the topics under the social capital umbrella are worthy of study."

**Evidence tier note.** **[model]** — an argumentative review essay (*Journal of Economic Literature* 2002, covering Putnam's *Bowling Alone* and the Dasgupta & Serageldin World Bank volume), presenting no original data of Sobel's own.

## Is social capital actually capital?

**[model]** Channelling Arrow and Solow's own contributions to the World Bank volume under review: Solow calls social capital "an attempt to gain conviction from a bad analogy." Arrow notes physical capital requires extension in time, deliberate sacrifice, and alienability — social capital shares only the first; much of it (childhood friendships, native language, birth into an ethnicity) is "acquired without calculation or sacrifice." Solow adds that physical capital has a computable depreciation path; social capital's dynamics don't fit — Sobel, citing Ostrom, notes it can *appreciate* with use, the opposite direction from depreciation. **[wiki synthesis]** This is a critique of the *metaphor*, not of the underlying phenomena — worth flagging wherever this wiki uses a stock/investment framing for informal-institution accumulation.

## The circularity problem, stated precisely

**[model]** "A successful group succeeded because it has social capital, but the evidence that the group has social capital is its success" (paraphrasing Portes, endorsed by Sobel) — a definitional flaw in how Coleman and Putnam's usage often runs, independent of any specific empirical claim.

## What Sobel specifically disputes in Putnam

**[model]** Not one blanket objection but several: treating survey-measured trust *decline* as direct evidence of social-capital decline "comes close to equating social capital with good outcomes"; no independent test of alternative explanations for declining civic participation (e.g., marginal-tax-rate changes affecting charitable-giving trends); Putnam's own admission, quoted directly, that "the direction of causality has not been established"; and an internal inconsistency — Putnam alternates between civic patterns as deeply rooted/stable and as having shifted sharply in recent decades. Sobel offers an economist's alternative reading Putnam does not consider: declining club participation may be **rational substitution** toward other means (job boards, want ads) rather than a social pathology, or a **coordination failure** (no clubs form because there are none to join) — two explanations with very different policy implications that Putnam never disentangles.

## The measurement-comparability problem, via a live case

**[model]** Sobel's Japan case study is the sharpest illustration for this wiki's purposes: Yamagishi's survey-and-experimental work argues Japan is *low*-trust absent institutional monitoring; Fukuyama argues Japan is *intrinsically high*-trust. Sobel's own conclusion: "both high- and low-trust hypotheses could be consistent with observed behavior... these observations demonstrate how difficult it is to draw conclusions from cross-cultural comparisons of trust." **[wiki synthesis]** This is a direct caution against reading [[determinants-of-trust]]'s cross-community comparisons, or any future cross-national trust comparison this wiki captures, as identified without the same scrutiny.
