# FTC v. Amazon: Secret Ad-Auction Price-Rigging Complaint (August 2026)

On August 31/September 1, 2026, the FTC and 22 state Attorneys General sued Amazon.com, Inc. in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Washington, alleging that Amazon covertly overrode its own advertised "second price" (generalized second-price / Vickrey-style) Sponsored Products ad auction for more than seven years, extracting over $20 billion from roughly 1.2 million advertisers (over 500,000 of them small/medium businesses) while continuing to represent to customers that prices were competitively auction-determined. Unlike the wiki's other captured extraction mechanisms (dynamic consumer pricing, tenant-screening pricing algorithms), this is a **seller/advertiser-side** extraction mechanism — Amazon as auctioneer secretly setting the price it charges its own advertising customers — but it is structurally the same pattern: a nominally neutral algorithmic mechanism, engineered to extract surplus, deliberately obfuscated from the party being charged.

## The Alleged Mechanism

- **Baseline (2012–2018):** Amazon adopted a generalized second-price (GSP) sealed-bid auction for Sponsored Products around 2012, representing to advertisers — via UI copy, help documentation, "Ads Academy" training, and account-rep scripts — that the winning bidder pays only the minimum needed to beat the runner-up bid.
- **Covert override begins (late 2018):** Amazon began secretly replacing the GSP-determined price with a higher "soft reserve" price *after* the auction had already run and winners were determined. An Amazon Ads SVP is quoted internally: the price charged is "not set by an actual bidder, but rather by" Amazon, via a "proxy 2nd price that we calculate."
- **Designed for non-detection:** internal documents describe simulating a "'natural' second price bid," and Amazon's Sponsored Products team discussed internally "how much we've moved away from an auction mechanism" — while deliberately capping soft-reserve prices at the advertiser's own winning bid "to reduce the risk that advertisers detect and react."
- **eOPS Based Pricing (2021 tested, 2022 launched):** the primary soft-reserve-setting method became "eOPS Based Pricing" — using Amazon's estimate of Ordered Product Sales a given click would generate, calibrated against Return on Advertising Spend (RoAS), to set surcharges for almost all Sponsored Products ads.
- **Effect, measured via "first-price rate"** (the share of clicks charged exactly the advertiser's own maximum bid — i.e., the auction behaving as first-price rather than the advertised second-price): rose from 30–40% in 2021 to roughly 70% in 2022; an internal 2024 operating plan put it at 79.1%, noting Amazon was "close to charging the maximum amount the winning ads are currently bidding"; a 2024 experiment in Spain raised the first-price rate for some advertisers from 48% to 80% specifically to test advertiser reaction.
- **Peak-event calibration and complaint suppression:** Amazon allegedly calibrated surcharges especially around high-volume events (Prime Day, holidays) to hold advertiser RoAS flat versus normal days, on the theory that the general spike in shopping activity would "obfuscate" the price increase. Advertiser complaints in 2021 and 2023 about CPCs more than doubling on Prime Day were met with false explanations blaming "higher shopper activity"; Amazon built internal "surcharge alarms" and phased in increases specifically to avoid detection and escalation.

**Numeric caveat:** several precise percentages (exact surcharge rates, exact per-category overcharge figures) live in exhibit charts in the complaint PDF that did not extract as text or image in this capture. The figures above are the ones directly confirmed in the complaint's running text; treat any more granular percentage as unconfirmed until the exhibits are separately retrieved.

## Legal Posture

Five federal counts under FTC Act Section 5(a) (unfair-or-deceptive-practices authority) and Section 13(b) (permanent injunction / monetary relief), plus parallel counts under the 22 participating states' own UDAP (unfair-and-deceptive-acts-and-practices) statutes. This is a complaint — allegations, not adjudicated findings. Filed in Amazon's home district (W.D. Wash.).

## The Doctorow / Pluralistic Framing

Cory Doctorow's Pluralistic newsletter ("Amazon achieves enshittification inception," Sept 4, 2026) situates the case within his broader "enshittification" thesis and adds framing beyond the complaint itself:

- **Search-ranking payola.** Doctorow frames Amazon's Sponsored Products auction as search-ranking "payola" — sold to the highest bidder rather than allocated by relevance — drawing an explicit parallel to 1950s radio payola (concentrated gains, diffuse losses, opaque allocation ripe for corruption). He cites this line of business as Amazon's single most profitable, exceeding both AWS and direct retail, and (per Tim Wu) exceeding all the world's newspaper ad revenue combined by roughly 300% — a knock-on harm to publisher ad revenue broadly.
- **"Cheating at its own bribery scheme."** His core framing: Amazon didn't just run a payola auction, it then secretly cheated the payola auction too — charging winners their own full bid (not the advertised second price) in an alleged ~80% of auctions, using an internal carve-out letting Amazon unilaterally override the auction result whenever it judged the clearing price "below true market value." Doctorow calls this incoherent on its face, since the auction result *is*, by definition, the market value.
- **Why multi-state matters.** Doctorow is explicitly skeptical the FTC alone (under the current administration) would sustain the case, citing the precedent of a similar Ticketmaster action getting dropped under political pressure; the 22-state co-filing is framed as the structural safeguard against that outcome.
- **Detection asymmetry.** He flags that Amazon's search results carry no per-unit price comparison tooling — a feature present at Whole Foods, which Amazon owns — as a deliberate design choice shielding payola-driven price premiums (his cited estimate: the top search result runs ~29% pricier than the best-match item) from easy consumer-side detection.
- **Transmission to consumer prices.** Ties the ad-auction surcharge into his separate reporting on AI-enforced "Most Favored Nation" pricing clauses as the mechanism by which Amazon's advertiser-side extraction gets passed through into retail prices economy-wide — connecting this seller-side mechanism back to the wiki's consumer-facing pricing coverage (see [[industries/consumer-facing-dynamic-pricing]]).

## Tooling Hook

The complaint's own internal metric — "first-price rate" (share of clicks an advertiser is charged their exact max bid) — is precisely the independent-detection signal an outside auditor would need to reconstruct. A design sketch, directly implied by the complaint's own internal analytics:

1. **Advertiser-side bid-vs-charge logging.** Per-auction logging of submitted max bid against actual CPC charged, aggregated per advertiser account, to compute an independent first-price rate. This is exactly the metric Amazon tracked internally while working to keep advertisers from noticing the pattern.
2. **Peak-event time-series monitoring.** CPC/RoAS tracking concentrated around high-volume events (Prime Day, holidays) specifically, since the complaint alleges manipulation was deliberately concentrated and obfuscated there.
3. **Cross-advertiser aggregation.** No single advertiser can see the population-level pattern Amazon's internal analytics used (surcharge rate by product category, market-wide RoAS suppression). An independent collective/co-op data-pooling tool — structurally the advertiser-side analogue of the DSAR-coordination and price-transparency levers already tracked for consumer-facing pricing (see [[strategies/data-disruption-strategy-map]]) — could reconstruct that population-level view from the demand side, before or independent of litigation discovery.

This is a genuinely new class of tooling target for the wiki: existing counter-power tracking has focused on consumer-facing price discrimination; this complaint documents the same extraction logic applied one layer upstream, against advertisers/sellers rather than end consumers, with a comparable audit-by-aggregation fix.
