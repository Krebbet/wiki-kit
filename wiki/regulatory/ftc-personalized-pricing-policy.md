# FTC Proposed Enforcement Policy Statement on Personalized Pricing (August 2026)

On August 19, 2026, the FTC voted 2-0 to seek public comment on a proposed enforcement policy statement regarding personalized pricing, opening docket **FTC-2026-1057** (comment period closes **September 18, 2026**). The statement would have the Commission enforce Section 5 of the FTC Act — unfair-or-deceptive-practices authority — against personalized pricing that is not "clearly and conspicuously" disclosed, including disclosure of the types of data used to set the price. The statement explicitly declines to take a position on whether personalized pricing is itself unfair when fully disclosed; it is a disclosure regime, not a prohibition. The American Prospect (David Dayen, Aug 21 2026) published a critique arguing the statement is materially weaker than public and bipartisan legislative pressure demands.

## What the Policy Statement Proposes

- **Legal theory:** absent a congressional ban, the FTC's only lever is Section 5 unfair-or-deceptive enforcement. The statement interprets non-disclosure of personalized pricing as the deceptive act — a company implicitly represents a price is static/uniform when it is not, unless it discloses otherwise.
- **Disclosure trigger:** enforcement attaches when a business (a) fails to disclose that personal data informs the price at all, or (b) fails to state what categories of data are used (e.g., disposable income, browsing history, geolocation, purchase history).
- **No categorical position on fairness:** the statement affirms consumers' reasonable expectation of paying the same price as other shoppers, and cites illustrative examples the FTC itself calls troubling — raising food-delivery prices on people it infers can't leave home, raising milk prices for larger families, surge pricing to funeral or medical destinations, higher online prices for shoppers known to have visited a brick-and-mortar location. But in a footnote the FTC "declines at this time to take any position on whether some personalized pricing practices are unfair even when fully disclosed to consumers."
- **Evidentiary gap acknowledged:** the statement itself states "the extent to which businesses currently use personalized pricing is not well understood" — a direct consequence of the FTC halting the Khan-era 6(b) surveillance-pricing study (see [[surveillance-pricing-retail]]) after only releasing January 2025 interim research summaries.
- **Procedural status:** proposed, not final. Public comment docket FTC-2026-1057 open through September 18, 2026; the Commission has not committed to a timeline for issuing a final statement.

## Context and the American Prospect Critique

Dayen situates the announcement as a reactive move under sustained bipartisan pressure rather than a resumption of substantive investigation. Key points from the critique, presented alongside the countervailing framing for balance:

- **Timing/pressure context.** The statement follows a Senate Judiciary subcommittee hearing (chaired by Sen. Josh Hawley, R-MO) two weeks prior at which four of five witnesses were surveillance-pricing critics (Groundwork Collaborative's Lindsay Owens, UFCW's Hillary Caron, AELP's Lee Hepner), and follows continued reporting on individual company practices (Wired's report that McDonald's app holds up to 515-page customer dossiers; a Northeastern datadepot report on Home Depot's data practices). Groundwork/Data for Progress polling cited in the piece found over 75% of Americans consider surveillance pricing unfair and support a ban outright, with the issue also polling as a top concern for swing voters.
- **"Disclosure, not ban" critique.** Dayen's central argument: the policy statement stops well short of what the cited public and legislative pressure demands. It targets non-disclosure as the violation, not personalized pricing itself — meaning a company that discloses its practice, however buried the disclosure, would not be in violation regardless of how exploitative the underlying pricing is.
- **Named critics quoted in the source.** Groundwork's Lindsay Owens: "Chairman Ferguson makes it clear that he'll let companies play semantics as long as they disclose what they're doing. It shouldn't be on consumers to navigate the fine print and sniff out if they're being played. A disclosed price can still be an unfair one." Consumer Reports' Grace Gedye is cited making a similar point about shifting the compliance burden onto consumers.
- **Contrasted with legislative activity.** Dayen notes a House bill (Rep. Greg Casar, D-TX, introduced 2025) and Sen. Hawley's stated intent to introduce Senate legislation banning surveillance pricing outright — implying the FTC's disclosure-only approach is already behind the position Congress (across party lines) appears to be converging on. See [[counter-power/regulatory-responses]] § 2–3 for the state-legislative disclosure-vs-prohibition split (NY APDA / MD HB0895 / CT PA 26-64 / CO HB26-1210) that this federal move now sits alongside.
- **FTC's own framing (for balance).** The policy statement text itself, as summarized above, affirms consumer harm examples and a "reasonable expectation of uniform pricing" baseline; the Commission characterizes the move as responding to "growing public concern." The dispute is over sufficiency and enforcement theory, not over whether personalized pricing raises legitimate concern — both sources agree on that point.

## Tooling Hook

Two concrete, near-term build targets:

1. **The open comment docket itself (through Sept 18, 2026) is a live, scrapeable public record.** FTC-2026-1057 will accumulate public comments — a defined, time-boxed, structurally similar record to the kind of civic-engagement corpus already flagged as a design target on [[surveillance-pricing-retail]] ("FTC-study commenting aggregator"). A tool that (a) tracks the docket for new filings, (b) extracts named companies/practices cited in comments, and (c) helps consumers submit structured testimony before the deadline would both amplify the record and generate a machine-readable dataset of company-level surveillance-pricing allegations independent of the FTC's own (halted) 6(b) study.
2. **A future disclosure-format mandate, if finalized, defines a scrapeable price-transparency surface.** If the FTC ultimately issues a final statement requiring "clear and conspicuous" disclosure of personalized pricing plus the data categories used, that creates a DOM-testable compliance target structurally identical to the one the NY Algorithmic Pricing Disclosure Act already created (see [[counter-power/regulatory-responses]] § "NY Algorithmic Pricing Disclosure Act (A3008)" — the mandated-phrase / near-price-placement standard that enabled a browser-side audit and the NY AG's Instacart action). A federal version of that standard — even disclosure-only — would give a consumer-facing price auditor (cf. [[surveillance-pricing-retail]]'s "Shopper-side price auditor" design input) a nationwide, not state-by-state, compliance surface to check against, and a federal enforcement backstop (FTC Section 5) to cite in a complaint if disclosure is absent or non-conspicuous. This is worth pre-building the detection logic for now, ahead of any final rule, given the NY precedent already validates the pattern.

Both targets are time-sensitive: the comment docket closes Sept 18, 2026, and whatever influence a structured-comment tool could exert is only available before that date.
