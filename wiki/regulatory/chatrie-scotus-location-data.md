# Chatrie v. United States — SCOTUS Location Data Ruling (2026)

In June 2026 the Supreme Court ruled in *Chatrie v. United States* that the Fourth Amendment protects location data even from short-term surveillance, and that app-generated records shared with third-party tech companies remain the user's "own" data — not surrendered under the third-party doctrine. The case arose from a 2019 geofence warrant compelling Google to expose all device holders within a Virginia crime-scene radius. The ruling is the first digital-surveillance decision since *Carpenter v. United States* (2018) and narrows the third-party doctrine significantly, though it leaves commercial surveillance by data brokers entirely outside constitutional reach.

## The Ruling

The Court held that even brief location tracking can reveal "private matters" — "a wealth of detail about a person's familial, political, professional, religious, and sexual associations" — and therefore constitutes a search under the Fourth Amendment (EFF Deeplinks, 2026-06-29). Crucially, the majority affirmed that app data users "reasonably view" as their own, and "reasonably expect to be shielded from the inquisitive eyes of the government," retains Fourth Amendment protection regardless of transmission to a third party. Justice Gorsuch's concurrence went further, characterizing location data as personal property — an "effect" protected by the Amendment's text.

The immediate case is not yet fully resolved: the Court declined to rule on whether the specific warrant was "reasonable" or whether good-faith doctrine saved it, remanding to the Fourth Circuit on those questions. The constitutional principle, however, is established.

*Chatrie* extends *Carpenter* by confirming the protection is not limited to prolonged tracking; short-duration surveillance suffices if the data is sufficiently revealing.

## Counter-Power Implications

The ruling directly constrains the [[counter-power/regulatory-responses|legal counter-power]] posture against government dragnet access to user data:

- **Geofence warrants presumptively unconstitutional.** Courts must now assess probable cause as to *every individual* swept into the fence — a standard dragnet warrants structurally cannot meet. The Fifth Circuit had already reached this conclusion categorically in 2024; *Chatrie* generalizes the principle.
- **App data broadly protected.** The "own data" framing extends beyond location to emails, documents, photographs, calendars, and any other records generated by phone apps — potentially broad cover for [[mechanisms/dsar-and-data-deletion|data subject access]] claims and litigation posture.
- **Third-party doctrine narrowed.** Clicking "agree" to share data with a tech platform no longer automatically strips constitutional protection from government access. This is a structural shift in the government-surveillance threat model.

EFF filed amicus in this case alongside the ACLU and Georgetown's Center on Privacy & Technology (EFF Deeplinks, 2026-06-29).

## The Open Gap: Commercial Data Brokers

*Chatrie* constrains *government* access. It does not touch the extraction layer directly relevant to this wiki's scope: data brokers collecting and aggregating location signals from phone apps and selling that data commercially — including to law enforcement without a warrant.

As EFF notes, police already route around device-level warrants via data broker purchases: "data brokers collect and aggregate location data from many different apps on our phones and provide that data to police" with a click of a mouse (EFF Deeplinks, 2026-06-29). Cell tower dump warrants are a parallel vector. Neither is addressed by *Chatrie*.

The ruling therefore defines a ceiling on *government* access while leaving the commercial extraction floor unaddressed. For [[strategies/data-disruption-strategy-map|counter-power strategy]], this means judicial wins at the *Chatrie* level do not substitute for structural supply-side intervention against the broker ecosystem.

## "Privacy by Architecture": The Google Precedent

In 2023, Google changed its location-data architecture to store data locally on devices rather than in the cloud. The practical effect: since July 2025, mass geofence searches of Google users' location data have been structurally impossible — Google cannot comply because it does not hold the data (EFF Deeplinks, 2026-06-29).

This is the canonical instance of [[mechanisms/obfuscation|privacy by architecture]]: removing data from a position where it can be compelled, rather than relying on legal protections to block access after collection. The pattern predates *Chatrie* and renders the ruling moot for Google's user base on geofence warrants specifically.

The implication for [[mechanisms/browser-fingerprinting|tracking-technology]] design more broadly: architectural changes that eliminate data custody at the platform level provide stronger protection than legal frameworks that restrict access to data the platform still holds. *Chatrie* reinforces the value of the Google approach as a model — courts now protect data users "own," but platforms that never hold the data provide protection that requires no litigation at all.
