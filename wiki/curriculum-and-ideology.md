# Curriculum and Ideology

Cantoni, Chen, Yang, Yuchtman & Zhang supply this wiki's cleanest causal (not correlational) evidence that school **content**, not schooling per se, shapes political norms — and that the direction of the shift is whatever the content-setter wants. China's staggered 2001–2010 "8th Curriculum Reform," rolled out to entering cohorts at different times across 29 provinces, let the authors identify its effect with a difference-in-differences design (province × cohort fixed effects) on a purpose-built 2013 survey of ~2,000 Peking University undergraduates. Students exposed to the new curriculum were significantly more likely to view China's system as democratic, trust government institutions more, favour extending political influence beyond Party members, and be more sceptical of free markets — with an estimated persuasion rate around 20%, larger than comparable estimates for Fox News (~3–8%) or wartime radio propaganda (~4–5%).

**Evidence tier note.** **[empirical]** — a genuine natural experiment (sharp, staggered rollout with no partial treatment), not correlational survey work. Robust to province/two-way clustering, individual and province×cohort controls, province-specific trends, placebo cohorts, and 10,000 randomised placebo-assignment simulations.

## The state's own stated intent, on the record

**[empirical]** The reform's ideological purpose is documented in the government's own memos, translated and reproduced in the paper's appendix. The textbook-writing committee's chair, quoted directly: **"The Politics textbook is the spiritual material that the country provides for the students. Writing the Politics textbook is an act at the state level, rather than an academic activity of the individual author... With a large readership, it will influence an entire generation of young people."**

## Specificity, not generality — the direct tension with the socialisation mechanism

**[empirical]** Effects were targeted, not diffuse: trust in *government* institutions rose (all six measured institutions individually more trusted) while trust in *non-governmental* entities (NGOs, banks, foreign investors, people in general) was unaffected — a specificity check the authors themselves run to distinguish content-driven belief change from a general rise in sociability. **[wiki synthesis]** This is a direct empirical complication of [[why-democracy-needs-education]]'s own reasoning: Glaeser, Ponzetto & Shleifer reject the political-indoctrination hypothesis partly because they find education raises participation broadly, not selectively — Cantoni shows a real, causal, content-specific shift is achievable and was in fact achieved, in a direction (more trust in an authoritarian one-party state, more market scepticism) opposite to what a pro-democratic-schooling thesis would predict.

## The authors' own both-readings conclusion

**[model]** The paper states plainly it supports two opposed theoretical traditions at once: the **"dark" reading** — education as a tool of elite/state control, able to indoctrinate students into whatever beliefs serve the ruling power (Freire, Bowles & Gintis) — and the **"bright" reading** — that education genuinely can shape civic belief in ways consonant with democratic-education theory (Dewey, Lipset, and explicitly citing Glaeser et al. 2007, i.e. [[why-democracy-needs-education]]). **"[T]he social welfare consequences of these effects depend on the political economy of curriculum choice: to the extent that educational content is selected to shape ideology, rather than to produce human capital, there is obviously a cost of using the education system to indoctrinate... the norms instilled in school may be disproportionately beneficial to the seated elite, who have the ability to shape what is taught."**
